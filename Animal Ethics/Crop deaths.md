> [!summary] Bottom line
> There's no evidence cropland kills more animals than wildland. The studies claiming it ignore the land used by grass-fed beef, misread their sources and overstate mouse plagues by a factor of about 43; even their 7.3 billion figure is below the number of animals killed in US animal agriculture, and mouse poisoning protects pastures too. Using the crop-death estimates the pasture argument relies on, a million calories of beef costs about 29 animal lives and of chicken about 251, against under 3 for vegetables, fruits or grains.

## No evidence cropland kills more

We cannot claim to know that cropland kills more animals than wildland, because if we did know this, there would be evidence for it, but such evidence doesn't exist. So, it cannot be claimed that crop farming leads to more animal deaths.

## Problems with crop-death studies

Studies concluding a vegan diet will result in more deaths generally contain the following problems: They don't account for total land use of grass fed beef and they select areas that are outliers for crop deaths, such as regions of mouse plagues.
[AnimalVisuals: US slaughter rates](https://animalvisuals.org/p/slaughter)

This study is often cited as evidence that 7.3 billion animals are killed every year from plant agriculture. The estimates come from the work of Archer and Davis. Davis misread the sugarcane study he cites, as he ignored the longer growing time, so the annual mortality rate is only 38.5%, not 77% while Archer's analysis offers a similar example. It exaggerates the scope of mouse plagues.

## Animals killed per million calories

The idea that pasture-raised beef kills fewer animals than a vegan diet comes from Steven Davis (2003). He estimated that harvesting annual crops kills about 15 wild animals per hectare a year, and guessed that grazed pasture kills about half that, 7.5. But his comparison assumed a hectare of pasture produces as much food as a hectare of crops. Land suitable for crops produces far more food when it grows crops for people than when it raises cattle, and Gaverick Matheny showed that once this is corrected, Davis's own numbers favor a vegan diet.

Using Davis's per-hectare estimates and the land needed per calorie of each food (from a 2006 study of New York State diets), the estimated animals killed per million calories, about a year of food at 2,740 calories a day, are:

| Food | Slaughtered | Killed in harvest | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chicken** | 237.6 | 13.5 | 251.1 |
| **Eggs** | 83.3 | 9.0 | 92.3 |
| **Beef** | 1.7 | 27.4 | 29.0 |
| **Pork** | 7.1 | 11.0 | 18.1 |
| **Milk** | 0.04 | 4.74 | 4.78 |
| **Vegetables** | 0 | 2.55 | 2.55 |
| **Fruits** | 0 | 1.73 | 1.73 |
| **Grains** | 0 | 1.65 | 1.65 |

Beef kills more animals in harvest than in slaughter, because cattle need far more land per calorie, whether as pasture or as feed crops. Eggs include the male chicks killed at hatcheries, which doubles the count.

### Deaths left out

Only animals slaughtered and animals killed in harvest were counted. Other deaths linked to food were left out:
- predators killed to protect livestock
- wild animals that never exist because their habitat was destroyed
- human deaths from food poisoning, disease and accidents on farms and in slaughterhouses
- male dairy calves raised for veal, about 1.5 per dairy cow

Most of these come from animal farming, so counting them would raise the numbers for animal foods more than for plant foods.

## Mouse plagues are overstated

Archer writes that "each area of grain production in eastern Australia is subject on average to a mouse plague every 4 years". The sources Archer cites support this statement insofar as "each area" is understood to refer in large geographic regions such as states or parts of states every hectare of grain cropland in Australia. The difference here is between a quadrennial mouse plague happening somewhere in all of Victoria and such a plague happening on every last Victorian farm.

A more accurate picture is suggested by the Cooperative Research Centre for Pest Animal Control, which notes that each year between 100,000 and 500,000 ha of grain crops in Australia are subject to mouse plagues. Australia plants approximately 22 million hectares of grain crops annually (Australian Export Grains Innovation Centre 2016). These figures suggest that in an average year 2.3% of Australian grain cropland is hit by plague. When Archer's figure of 55 deaths per hectare of grain is recalculated to only apply to 2.3% of cropland the mortality rate for grain becomes 1.27 animal per hectare (error by a factor of 43).
[Fischer 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10806-018-9733-8)

## Compared with animal agriculture

These numbers should also be compared to the animals killed on farms in the US. 
Cattle: 40 million per year
Pigs: 120 million per year
Broiler Chickens 9 billion per year

This alone puts animal agriculture above plant agriculture in the death count even if the 7.3 billion figure was correct.

## Mouse poisoning protects pastures too

The previous paper also notes that mouse plagues also damage grazing land. A primary reason farmers resort to poison is to avoid the extensive economic issues mouse plagues cause. Insofar as mouse plagues damage farms indiscriminately, it's wrong to argue that farmers poison mice to grow grain. The poisoning that occurs in Australia is done to protect all aspects of affected farming operations, including pastures. Archer's analysis is thus misleading in failing to associate mouse poisoning with animal agriculture, and this undermines his attempt to use mouse poison deaths as grounds to prefer pasture raised meat over plant foods.

## Mammals are denser on farmland

Farmland isn't empty of wild mammals. In a global analysis of 6,729 population density estimates for 468 mammal species, densities were higher where the human footprint was larger, such as agricultural and suburban areas (very built-up urban areas had no populations in the data). For a 15 kg herbivore, average density rose from about 1 per km² with no human footprint to 4.5 at a moderate footprint and 26 at the highest, up to 25 times higher. Each component of the human footprint, including cropland and pasture, was linked to higher densities, and the pattern held within single species too, though it varied a lot between species.

The likely reasons are extra food from crops and other human sources, fewer predators and competitors, and shelter from predators near people. But the gain comes from a few species that thrive in human-modified landscapes while others decline or disappear, and croplands were linked to less taxonomically diverse mammal communities.
[Tucker 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.05126)
