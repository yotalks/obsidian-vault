> [!summary] Bottom line
> Not buying animal products doesn't just lead the government to buy them instead. US livestock subsidies are mostly disaster and emergency payments, like insurance against droughts and catastrophes. Dairy is the main exception, but its price-support payments only covered part of a price drop, on a capped amount of milk, so falling demand still cuts farmers' revenue. The current dairy program works more like insurance that farmers pay into.

## Livestock subsidies

People often assume that when someone stops buying animal products, the government steps in and buys them at taxpayers' expense. But almost all US livestock payments are emergency or disaster payments, acting as insurance against droughts, food shortages and other catastrophes. Of about $12.5 billion in livestock subsidies from 1995 to mid-2020, $7.7 billion came from the Livestock Forage Disaster Program alone, with most of the rest from emergency feed, indemnity and compensation programs.
[EWG: Livestock subsidies](https://farm.ewg.org/progdetail.php?fips=00000&progcode=livestock)

## Dairy price support

The exception is milk. Under the Milk Income Loss Contract (MILC), dairy operations were paid whenever the Boston Class I milk price fell below $16.94 per hundredweight (cwt). The payment was a percentage of the gap between the market price and $16.94: 45% from October 2008 to August 2012, and 34% before and after. Even if the price fell to $0, the most the government would pay is $7.62 per cwt (45% of $16.94).

Payments were also capped at about 3 million pounds of milk per operation per year (2.4 million in 2008). So MILC covered only part of a price drop, on a limited amount of milk, and the government wasn't buying the milk itself. If demand fell, farmers would still lose most of the lost revenue.
[7 CFR 1430.207–208 (2012)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2012-title7-vol10/xml/CFR-2012-title7-vol10-part1430.xml)
[EWG: Dairy subsidies](https://farm.ewg.org/progdetail.php?fips=00000&progcode=dairy)

MILC was replaced by the Margin Protection Program for Dairy (MPP-Dairy) in 2014, and by Dairy Margin Coverage (DMC) in 2018.
[USDA FSA: Dairy programs](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/programs-and-services/farm-bill/farm-safety-net/dairy-programs/index)

DMC isn't a pure subsidy, since farmers have to buy in by paying premiums. It's more like insurance. Through mid-2020 it had paid farmers $368 million (and MPP-Dairy $276 million), compared with $3.5 billion under MILC.
