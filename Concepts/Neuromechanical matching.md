> [!summary] Bottom line
> Neural drive favors the motor units with the best leverage for the intended movement, so the joint angles where a muscle region's internal moment arm is longest point to its best exercise. Leverage turns muscle force into joint torque without costing energy, which is the argument for leverage mattering more than the plateau of the length-tension relationship. Training at long or short muscle lengths adds or removes sarcomeres, which moves the plateau toward the joint angles where a muscle has its best leverage. The elbow flexors fit this pattern. Muscle regions can have antagonistic, different or overlapping functions, and the more opposed they are, the more pronounced regional growth can be. Lagging regions come from this and from two-joint muscles shutting off in multi-joint exercises, so they're fixed with biomechanically different exercises.

Neural drive goes to the motor units with the best mechanical advantage for the intended movement, a principle called neuromechanical matching. Mechanical advantage depends on the internal moment arm of each motor unit's muscle fibers. So the joint and body positions that give a muscle region its longest internal moment arm point to the best exercise for that region.
[Hudson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985474/)
→ [[Internal moment arm]]

This is why large, fan-shaped muscles like the lats and pecs aren't activated as one block: the nervous system activates their regions with some independence, favoring whichever region has the best line of pull for the task. In EMG recordings of 19 segments across the pecs, delts and lats, each segment's activation depended on its moment arm relative to the intended movement.
[Brown 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16458022/)

## Leverage vs the length-tension plateau

It's sometimes claimed that the [[Glossary#CNS|CNS]] takes the location of the plateau of the active length-tension relationship into account when it allocates [[Glossary#CMC|CMC]] to muscles during a movement.

### Energy cost

Joint torques are what produce movements. Joint torques are a product of [[Glossary#IMAL|IMAL]] and the muscle force. Muscle force requires energy while IMAL does not and [[Glossary#NMM|NMM]] goal is to maximize efficiency. Sending more CMC to a muscle with good leverage that works away from the active [[Glossary#LTR|LTR]] plateau (and produces small force) is more efficient than sending CMC to a muscle with poor leverage that works on the plateau of the active LTR (and produces large force). It's been well-documented that working on the active LTR plateau uses more energy in the form of ATP.
[Hilber 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11251057/)
![[Pasted image 20261026150511.png]]

### The plateau follows training

The active LTR plateau will also just end up where a muscle has best leverage anyway. When muscle fibers are repeatedly activated at a joint angle corresponding to a long muscle length, they display sarcomerogenesis. Since sarcomere number determines the location of the plateau of the active LTR within the joint angle [[Glossary#ROM|ROM]], this causes the plateau of the active LTR to move to that joint angle.

And when muscle fibers are repeatedly activated at a joint angle corresponding to a short muscle length, they lose sarcomeres. Since sarcomere number determines the location of the plateau of the active LTR within the joint angle ROM, this causes the plateau of the active LTR to move to that joint angle.
→ [[Sarcomerogenesis]]

This means that if we start with the CNS sending CMC to the muscle or muscle regions with best leverage, the location of the active LTR moves to correspond to that muscle length and we end up with the best of both worlds, max leverage and plateau of active LTR at the same joint angle. If the CNS sent neural drive to the joint angle corresponding to the plateau of the active LTR, it would produce inefficient joint torques because it would have poor leverage, and sarcomere addition and loss would not be possible except with static stretching or limb immobilization.
→ [[Detraining sarcomerogenesis]]

### Muscles where leverage and plateau line up

We also have data that shows many muscles display their active LTR plateaus where they have their best relative leverages. Like with the elbow flexors, the biceps have best relative leverage at long muscle lengths, while the brachioradialis has best leverage at short muscle lengths.
[Murray 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)
![[Pasted image 20261026151129.png]]

Consistent with this, the biceps have their plateau on the active LTR at long muscle lengths, while the brachioradialis has their plateau at short muscle lengths. The same thing can be seen with the gluteus maximus which has best leverage at short lengths along with plateau of active LTR and gastrocnemius which has best relative leverage and active LTR plateau at long lengths.
[Hale 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21145061/)

## Classifying muscle regions

Because motor units are recruited by the leverage of their fibers for the movement, a muscle with regions of different leverage uses some regions more in some movements and others in others. The differences between regions fall into three categories.

### 1. Antagonistic functions

Some muscles have regions with opposite functions that can't work together to produce a movement:
- The gluteus medius' anterior region has leverage for hip internal rotation and its posterior region for external rotation.
- The clavicular pectoralis major has leverage for shoulder flexion and the costal pec for shoulder extension.
- The anterior deltoid has leverage for horizontal flexion and the posterior deltoid for horizontal extension.

So very pronounced regional hypertrophy is possible if we only do exercises that train one region.

In cadaver hips, the rotation moment arms of most hip muscle compartments shifted toward internal rotation as the hip flexed, and some switched from external to internal rotation.
[Delp 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10327003/)

In cadaver shoulders, the clavicular pec was among the most effective shoulder flexors, while the middle and lower pec acted more as a stabilizer and extensor.
[Ackland 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)

### 2. Different but not antagonistic functions

In other muscles, one region has good leverage for a joint action and another has poor leverage, but wouldn't oppose it if activated:
- The upper gluteus maximus has good leverage for hip abduction, the lower doesn't → [[Gluteus maximus training]].
- The anterior deltoid has good leverage for shoulder flexion, the middle deltoid doesn't.
- The clavicular pec has good leverage for shoulder flexion, the sternal pec doesn't.
- The upper (thoracic) latissimus dorsi has good leverage for shoulder extension, while the middle (lumbar) and lower (pelvic) regions have surprisingly poor leverage → [[Latissimus dorsi training]].

One region is activated for the action while the other is barely activated. Pronounced regional hypertrophy is still possible by training only one region, but the poorly leveraged region can be stimulated, unlike in the antagonistic category.

With the arm raised 90°, the pectoralis major had the largest moment arm for horizontal flexion, and the anterior deltoid a larger moment arm for elevation in the forward planes.
[Kuechle 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9356931/)

In healthy hips modeled during walking, the anterior gluteus medius had an abduction moment arm of about 42–53 mm.
[Song 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7737424/)

### 3. Overlapping functions

In some muscles, all regions contribute to a joint action but dominate in different parts of the range of motion:
- The anterior and middle deltoids both abduct the shoulder, with the middle deltoid having better leverage from the arm at the side up to parallel with the floor, and the anterior deltoid from there upward → [[Deltoid training]].
- The triceps long head has better leverage for elbow extension with the elbow flexed, and the medial and lateral heads with the elbow extended → [[Triceps training]].

Opposite resistance profiles on the same exercise could produce some regional differences, but regional growth is unlikely to be as pronounced as in the other two categories.

Moment arms of 23 upper limb muscle segments varied with elbow and forearm position.
[Ettema 1998](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167945797000304)

So the potential for pronounced regional growth is greatest when regions have antagonistic functions, then different but not antagonistic functions, then overlapping functions → [[Fiber-specific muscle growth]].
[Hudson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985474/)

## Lagging muscle regions

Fibers grow only when they're activated and experience [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]], but not every fiber in a muscle is activated in an exercise, and which ones are differs between exercises. So always doing a single exercise for a muscle can leave it looking uneven, with growth mostly in one region → [[Fiber-specific muscle growth]]. There are two reasons.

### The two-joint muscle problem

Two-joint muscles won't activate if doing so would also produce an opposing force at their other joint. The rectus femoris won't work as a knee extensor in squats or leg presses because it's also a hip flexor, and hip flexion force would fight the hip extension the lift needs. So two-joint muscles that are part of bigger muscle groups, like the rectus femoris, triceps long head and gastrocnemius, won't grow if a program only has multi-joint exercises → [[Quadriceps training]].

In 8 men doing isometric hip and knee extension together at 20% of maximum, rectus femoris activity fell to 67% of its activity in knee extension alone, gluteus maximus to 51% and semimembranosus to 42% of their activity in hip extension alone, while the vastus medialis rose to 153%.
[Yamashita 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3220066/)

### Neuromechanical matching

Fibers are activated to the extent that their region of the muscle has leverage to turn the joint in the right direction at the right joint angle. So different parts of a muscle or muscle group grow when peak efforts happen:
1. **In different planes of motion:** moving the arm forward in the transverse plane works mainly the anterior deltoid, while moving it down in the sagittal plane works mainly the posterior deltoid.
2. **At different joint angles:** cable lateral raises from the side up to horizontal are done entirely by the middle deltoid, but raising the arm from horizontal to overhead involves much more anterior deltoid → [[Deltoid training]].
3. **With different positions of neighboring joints:** in curls, a supinated grip works more biceps and a pronated grip more brachioradialis → [[Elbow flexor training]].
4. **At different joints:** hip extension exercises like 45° back extensions work the upper part of each hamstring more, and knee flexion exercises like seated leg curls the lower part. This is likely common to most two-joint muscles, since the rectus femoris does the same → [[Hamstring training]].

In cadaver shoulders, shoulder muscle sub-regions had significantly different moment arms, so different regions suit different movements.
[Ackland 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18691376/)

In 10 trained men, lying leg curls activated the lower lateral and lower medial hamstrings more than stiff-legged deadlifts.
[Schoenfeld 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24978835/)

### Fixing lagging regions

Fix an uneven muscle with a variety of biomechanically different exercises. Target two-joint muscles with a suitable single-joint exercise, or one at each joint. For lagging regions of single-joint muscles, change the plane of motion (if the joint moves in several planes), change the resistance profile so peak force comes at a different muscle length, or change the position of neighboring joints where possible.

## Do two exercises recruit the same motor units?

Two exercises recruit the same motor units if they give the same internal leverages for the movement. Three factors decide which motor units have the best leverage in any muscle, and two more apply only to two-joint muscles:
- **Resistance profile:** in hip extension, the gluteus maximus has its best leverage with the hip extended (hip extensors shortened) and the adductor magnus with the hip flexed (hip extensors lengthened). [Németh 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3988782/)
- **Plane of motion** (multi-planar joints only): in shoulder elevation, the middle deltoid works more in the frontal plane and the anterior deltoid and pecs more in the sagittal plane. [Ackland 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18691376/)
- **Positions of neighboring joints:** in elbow flexion, a supinated wrist lets the biceps contribute more and a pronated wrist the brachioradialis. [Murray 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)
- **Which joint** (two-joint muscles): training the rectus femoris with hip flexion activates its proximal region more, and with knee extension its distal region. In multi-channel EMG of the rectus femoris, activity during hip flexion centered more proximally than during knee extension. [Watanabe 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22153052/)
- **Muscle length** (two-joint muscles): training the hamstrings at long lengths with seated leg curls preferentially grows the biceps femoris long head and semimembranosus, while lying leg curls at short lengths favor the biceps femoris short head → [[Hamstring training]]. In 42 young men, eccentric knee flexion training with the hip flexed grew the hamstrings 18% and biceps femoris long head 19%, versus 11% and 5% with Nordic hamstring curls, which grew the knee flexors that don't extend the hip more. [Maeo 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11419281/)

So exercises have essentially the same effect if these factors are the same. Curls with the same resistance profile and the same shoulder and wrist positions, like standing dumbbell and standing barbell curls, target the same parts of the elbow flexors. Usually that only happens with the same basic exercise using different implements, like barbells and dumbbells.
[Hudson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985474/)
