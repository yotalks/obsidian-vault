> [!summary] Bottom line
> LDL causes atherosclerotic heart disease. Risk rises steadily with cholesterol from young adulthood with no safe threshold, people born with lower LDL have far less heart disease, and every way of lowering LDL in trials cuts major vascular events by about a fifth to a quarter per mmol/L, even from very low starting levels. ApoB and non-HDL cholesterol predict risk a little better than LDL-C, and nonfasting tests work as well as fasting ones. High CRP, a marker of inflammation, adds risk on top of LDL, but lowering LDL is what reliably cuts events.

LDL particles — more precisely, all ApoB-carrying particles — cause atherosclerotic heart disease. Genetic studies, prospective cohorts and randomized trials agree: the more LDL you're exposed to, and the longer, the higher the risk, and lowering LDL with different drugs reduces risk roughly in proportion to how much it falls. So anything that durably raises LDL or ApoB raises heart disease risk, which makes LDL the marker to judge foods and diets by.
[Ference 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28444290/)

## Genetic evidence

Combining Mendelian randomization studies of 9 variants in 6 genes (312,321 people), each 1 mmol/L (39 mg/dL) of lifelong lower LDL-C meant 54.5% less coronary heart disease. Every variant gave the same benefit per unit of LDL, and the benefit was about 3 times what statins give per mmol/L when started later in life.
[Ference 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23083789/)

A review of these studies concludes that LDL's effect is causal and cumulative, and that the benefit of lower LDL depends on how much lower and for how long, not on how it's lowered.
[Ference 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26780009/)

A common variant of PCSK9, a gene that controls how many LDL receptors the liver keeps, raised LDL-C in a dose-dependent way and was linked to more severe coronary atherosclerosis in about 700 people.
[Chen 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15893176/)

In older people, observational studies show no link, or even an inverse one, between cholesterol and mortality. But using a genetic score of 51 LDL-raising variants in three Dutch cohorts, LDL-raising variants became rarer with age, families with exceptional longevity carried fewer of them, and among people over 90, those with the highest scores had 13% higher mortality. So LDL still raises risk in old age, and the flat observational link likely reflects illness lowering cholesterol.
[Postmus 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25855712/)

## Cohorts: risk rises without a threshold

Among 356,222 men aged 35–57 screened for the MRFIT trial, coronary death rose steadily across cholesterol quintiles: compared with the lowest (under 182 mg/dL), risk was 1.29, 1.73, 2.21 and 3.42 times higher. An estimated 46% of coronary deaths were attributable to cholesterol of 180 mg/dL or more, and the pattern held in smokers and nonsmokers, with or without high blood pressure.
[Stamler 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3773199/)

In 1,017 young men (average age 22) followed for about 30 years, a 36 mg/dL higher cholesterol at baseline meant 72% more cardiovascular disease ([[Glossary#RR|RR]] 1.72) and twice the coronary heart disease (RR 2.01).
[Klag 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8419817/)

In 36,375 adults at low 10-year risk (median age 42) followed for 27 years, LDL-C of 160–189 mg/dL and 190 mg/dL or more were linked to 70% and 50% higher cardiovascular mortality after adjusting for other risk factors. Before adjustment, even 100–129 mg/dL carried 40% more risk than under 100.
[Abdullah 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30571575/)

Pooling cohorts, international comparisons and trials, a 10% (0.6 mmol/L) lower cholesterol meant 54% less ischemic heart disease at age 40, 39% at 50, 27% at 60 and about 20% at 70–80. In trials, the reduction was 7% in the first 2 years, 22% in years 2–5 and 25% after that, matching the long-term cohort estimate.
[Law 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8043072/)

## Trials: lowering LDL lowers risk

Across 49 trials (312,175 people), each 1 mmol/L drop in LDL-C cut major vascular events by 23% with statins and 25% with other treatments that work through LDL receptors (diet, bile acid sequestrants, ileal bypass surgery and ezetimibe). PCSK9 inhibitors did as well as expected for their LDL drop. Because such different treatments give the same benefit per mmol/L, the benefit comes from lowering LDL itself.
[Silverman 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27673306/)

A review of the statin trials found statins cut major vascular events by about a quarter per mmol/L for each year they're taken. Lowering LDL by 2 mmol/L for 5 years in 10,000 people prevents about 1,000 events in people with heart disease and 500 in people at raised risk without it. The same treatment causes about 5 cases of myopathy, 50–100 cases of diabetes and 5–10 hemorrhagic strokes. Placebo-controlled trials show that most muscle symptoms blamed on statins aren't caused by them.
[Collins 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27616593/)

The benefit holds at very low LDL. In patients starting at a median LDL-C of 70 mg/dL or less, each 1 mmol/L reduction cut major vascular events by 21% (RR 0.79), for statins and nonstatin drugs alike, down to achieved levels around 21 mg/dL. Serious adverse events, muscle problems, diabetes, hemorrhagic stroke and cancer didn't increase. The authors have many ties to companies that make these drugs.
[Sabatine 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073316/)

## How low is normal

Hunter-gatherers, newborns, wild primates and other wild mammals, none of which develop atherosclerosis, have LDL-C around 50–70 mg/dL. Trials suggest plaque progression and heart events are lowest below 70 mg/dL, with no safety problems in the 50–70 range.
[O'Keefe 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15172426/)

The 2019 European guidelines set LDL-C goals by risk, down to under 55 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L) for people at very high risk.
[Mach 2020](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/41/1/111/5556353)

## Which marker to measure

In 4,462 adults followed for 19 years, non-HDL cholesterol predicted cardiovascular death a little better than LDL-C: each 30 mg/dL meant 19% vs 15% more risk in men and 11% vs 8% in women.
[Cui 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11386890/)

In a meta-analysis of 12 studies (233,455 people), [[Glossary#ApoB|ApoB]] was the strongest marker of cardiovascular risk (risk ratio per standard deviation 1.43), non-HDL cholesterol was next (1.34) and LDL-C was weakest (1.25).
[Sniderman 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21487090/)

Small, dense LDL particles are closely linked to coronary disease and are raised in type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome, even when LDL-C isn't high. Methods for measuring them haven't been standardized.
[Hirayama 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22989852/)

## Fasting or not

In 27,331 women, fasting LDL-C predicted cardiovascular disease equally well whether calculated or measured directly, but LDL-C measured directly in nonfasting samples didn't predict it.
[Mora 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19395440/)

A later study measured the same 8,270 people both fasting and nonfasting, 4 weeks apart. Each 40 mg/dL of LDL-C predicted coronary events similarly either way (HR 1.32 nonfasting, 1.28 fasting), and 94.8% landed in the same risk category. The lead author has received grants and fees from Pfizer, which funded the trial.
[Mora 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31135812/)

A European consensus statement found that after normal meals, LDL-C changes by at most about 8 mg/dL, while HDL, ApoB and Lp(a) don't change. It recommends nonfasting tests by default, and fasting only when nonfasting triglycerides are above 440 mg/dL (5 mmol/L).
[Nordestgaard 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27122601/)

## Modified and oxidized LDL

Atherosclerosis starts when LDL builds up in the artery wall and gets modified. Modified LDL activates the surrounding cells and triggers inflammation, which modifies more LDL and keeps the cycle going.
[Pentikäinen 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10762453/)

In 504 patients having coronary angiography, oxidized phospholipids on ApoB particles closely tracked Lp(a) (r = 0.83). In patients 60 or younger, the top quartile had about 3 times the odds of coronary artery disease of the bottom quartile.
[Tsimikas 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16000355/)

LOX-1, the main receptor for oxidized LDL on blood vessel walls, also captures white blood cells. Blocking it in rats prevented death from endotoxin shock.
[Honjo 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12538855/)

## LDL vs inflammation

It's often claimed that LDL isn't causal for [[Glossary#ASCVD|ASCVD]] rather it's inflammation. We have epidemiological data that divides a population into quartiles related to [[Glossary#AMI|AMI]]. These quartiles have varying levels and combinations of exposures to either high LDL, high [[Glossary#CRP|CRP]], high LDL and high CRP, and high LDL and high CRP, low LDL and high CRP, and low LDL and low CRP.
![[Pasted image 20261027192118.png]]
We see risk is lowest when both LDL and CRP are both low, but risk goes up in context of low LDL and high CRP as well with the worst being high CRP and high LDL. So, it seems high CRP are an independent risk factor for AMI, and presumably ASCVD. We also see high LDL and low CRP carries and independent risk on its own as well.
[Mo 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7343474/)

We also see in subgroup analyses on the FOURIER trial has also been done investigating the relationship between inflammation and ASCVD endpoints. The results show basically the same thing. We see having both high LDL and high CRP leads to the worst outcomes, but either in isolation raises risk, and having low of both reduces risk the both.
![[Pasted image 20261027192512.png]]
[Bohula 2018](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.034032)

Inflammation isn't a target for therapy, however, compared to LDL, because we have tried that and it was unsuccessful while when we target LDL do work.
[Katsiari 2019](https://www.wjgnet.com/2220-6132/full/v8/i1/1.htm)
[Moore 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30560921/)
