> [!summary] Bottom line
> The rule-following argument says physical facts can't settle which rule someone is following, such as addition versus "quaddition," so thought must be partly immaterial. It works for behavior: writing 2 + 2 = 4 fits many different intentions. But to count against physicalism it needs a case where the same brain state fits two different intentions, and nobody has shown one. Not being able to prove there's no hidden rule is a problem about knowing, and getting from there to an immaterial mind takes an extra premise.

## The argument

Kripke's skeptic says nothing about your past use of "plus" shows you meant addition rather than "quus." Quus gives the same answers as addition when both numbers are below 57, and gives 5 otherwise. If you've never added numbers that large, all your past answers fit both rules.
[Wikipedia: Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittgenstein_on_Rules_and_Private_Language)

Ross and Feser turn this into an argument for an immaterial intellect: thinking can be exact (you really are adding, not quadding), no physical process is exact in that way, so thinking isn't purely physical.
[Ross 1992](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026790)
[Feser 2013](https://doi.org/10.5840/acpq20138711)

## Behavior versus brain states

**Behavior underdetermines intention.** Writing "2 + 2 = 4" on a board fits doing addition, doing quaddition, or just trying to scare someone. You can check this in your own experience, since you know you can write it with different intentions.

**The brain-state step isn't established.** To count against physicalism, the same premise has to hold for brain states: one brain state compatible with two different intentions. Nothing in experience shows that, and nothing rules out each brain state going with one intention. Moving from the board to the brain is an analogy that still needs an argument.

**The direction matters.** Two different brain states giving the same intention is fine for physicalism. What the argument needs is the reverse: the same brain state with two different intentions.

**It needs the totality of physical facts.** Showing that some physical facts, like the writing on the board, fit two intentions doesn't get there, because other physical facts, like the brain's, might settle it. The argument needs a case where all the physical facts together are compatible with two inconsistent intentions. That's the same as two possible worlds that are physically identical but differ in what someone intends, which is what physicalism rules out.
[Rule-following discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ARJfYCpE5So&t=153s)

## The empirical evidence

Brain imaging can't settle it either way yet. Perfusion MRI has a lot of variability within and between observers and many spurious results, and different areas light up from frame to frame even for the same report. Studies where the same area lights up for different reported beliefs haven't shown the beliefs were exactly the same rather than similar. Instruments being too coarse to find a one-to-one mapping doesn't show there isn't one, and the correlations found so far don't establish physicalism either.
[Rule-following discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ARJfYCpE5So&t=40s)

## Infinite thoughts, finite brains

One version says there are infinitely many propositions, so infinitely many possible beliefs, but only finitely many brain states, since a brain has a finite number of particles. So some beliefs couldn't be brain states. But infinitely many propositions doesn't mean a human can believe all of them. Some may be too complex for any arrangement of a human brain, and no one holds them all at once.
[Rule-following discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ARJfYCpE5So&t=966s)

## Knowing versus being

There are two kinds of underdetermination:

- **Epistemic:** we can't tell from the physical facts which rule is being followed.
- **Ontological:** the physical facts don't fix which rule is being followed.

Not being able to prove there's no hidden rule in addition is the epistemic kind. Some people use the argument only that way, to show physical facts can't justify physicalism. Concluding that physicalism is false needs a further premise taking you from "can't be known from the physical facts" to "isn't fixed by them," such as a verificationist principle.
[Rule-following discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ARJfYCpE5So&t=2114s)

## Demanding a justification

A common follow-up is to ask how you know addition has no hidden rule without appealing to something immaterial or to God. One reply is to grant that some basic beliefs, like the reliability of logic or that a rule applies the same way every time, can't be justified without circularity. Then ask how exactly the immaterial intellect or God solves that. Saying thought is "exact and unambiguous" restates the claim in question rather than justifying it.
