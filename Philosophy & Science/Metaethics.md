> [!summary] Bottom line
> Metaethics asks what morality is and where it comes from, while normative ethics asks what we should do. A moral system doesn't need moral realism: every system of knowledge bottoms out in assumed axioms, so you can bootstrap a moral axiom and reason from it. But facts alone don't give you that axiom. Pain existing doesn't show it ought not to, and something being unnecessary doesn't make it bad.

## Metaethics vs normative ethics

Normative theories are claims about what to do, like consequentialism, deontology and virtue ethics. Metaethics is about the status of those claims, like whether moral statements can be true and whether moral facts exist. Saying "pain is bad" is a normative claim. Asking how anyone could know that is a metaethical one.

"Ethics" and "morals" aren't separate things in philosophy, where the field is called ethics or moral philosophy. Morality can describe the code of conduct of a society, a group, or an individual, or it can mean a code all rational people would endorse.
[SEP: The definition of morality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/morality-definition/)

A definition only says what a word means. It doesn't claim that anything it describes is true, so defining moral reasoning as reasoning about what one ought to do doesn't derive an ought from anything.

Consequentialism is the view that what's right depends only on consequences, and utilitarianism is the main example of it, where the consequence that counts is utility. So every utilitarian is a consequentialist, but not the other way around.
[SEP: Consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/)

### Bad vs wrong

"Bad" and "wrong" aren't the same thing. A volcano killing people is bad, but it would be strange to call the volcano wrong, because wrongness applies to moral agents. Likewise, something can be bad or undesirable without anyone being to blame for it. A harmful trait that someone didn't choose and can't change can be something we'd rather didn't exist, while the person is only morally responsible for what they do about it. This follows the common principle that people are only morally assessable for what's under their control.
[SEP: Moral luck](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-luck/)

So when someone says a trait is "wrong," it's worth asking which they mean: that it would be better if it didn't exist, or that the person is culpable for it. People can agree on the first and disagree on the second.

## Moral realism and anti-realism

Moral anti-realism denies that moral properties exist objectively. It comes in three forms:

| View            | Claim                                                                                        |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Noncognitivism  | Moral statements don't state facts, so they aren't true or false                             |
| Error theory    | Moral statements try to state facts, but none are true, like claims about gods to an atheist |
| Non-objectivism | Moral facts exist, but they depend on minds                                                  |

Minimal moral realism only rejects noncognitivism and error theory. Robust moral realism also says moral facts are objective.
[SEP: Moral anti-realism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-anti-realism/)

So a moral statement having a truth value means it can be true or false, and it isn't a contradiction for all of them to be false.

### Objective isn't the same as universal

Objective vs subjective is about whether something depends on minds. Universal vs relative is about whether it applies to everyone. These are separate questions, so something subjective could hold for everyone, and something objective could differ between groups. Moral relativism isn't even a form of anti-realism, since it could rest on objective facts about those groups.
[SEP: Moral anti-realism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-anti-realism/)

So pointing out that nearly everyone dislikes pain doesn't make pain's badness objective.

### Mind-dependent vs mind-internal

"Depends on minds" can mean more than one thing. Something can be mind-independent in one sense and mind-dependent in another: cars are designed by minds, but a car doesn't need anyone's mind to keep existing.
[SEP: Moral anti-realism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-anti-realism/)

So objective might be better defined as external to minds, not just independent of them. Imagine a tree that vanishes the moment I die. It depends on my mind, but anyone could still see and touch it, and it doesn't seem right to call it subjective. If that's correct, the line that matters is whether something is inside a mind, not whether it needs a mind to exist.

This also closes off one common move. Asking whether morality would stop existing without minds doesn't settle whether it's subjective, because a realist can say it's an objective thing that only exists given minds.

### Is God-based morality objective?

Theists often say atheists can only have subjective morality. But if the good is whatever God desires, that seems to depend on a mind too. Saying the good is what God desires looks no different from saying it's whatever my uncle Charlie desires, unless something explains why God's desires count and his don't. This is a version of the arbitrariness problem for divine command views.
[SEP: Theological voluntarism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/voluntarism-theological/)

The theist's way out is the distinction above. If morality depends on God's mind but exists outside it, while the atheist's morality is inside human minds, then the theist's view could count as objective and the atheist's wouldn't. That only works if the theist actually holds that morality is external to God's mind, which a view where everything is within God's mind can't say. It also doesn't help against an atheist who doesn't think morality is mind-internal.

## Theories of truth

Whether something counts as true can depend on your theory of truth:

- **Correspondence:** true if it matches a state of affairs in the world.
- **Coherence:** true if it fits with a system of beliefs or axioms.
- **Pragmatist:** true if it's useful to believe.

[SEP: Truth](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/truth/)

Pragmatism can also be used as an axiom of its own. Whether to adopt that axiom is a separate question from what it means.

## Axioms and bootstrapping

Any attempt to justify a belief ends in a circle, an infinite regress or an assumed starting point. This is the Münchhausen trilemma.
[Wikipedia: Münchhausen trilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%BCnchhausen_trilemma)

Saying you're confident rather than certain doesn't get around this. If you're 90% confident in evolution and 90% confident in that confidence, multiplying gives 81%, and it keeps falling toward zero the further you ask. So at some level you have to assume something rather than know it.

That doesn't mean evidence is useless. Evolution is accepted because the fossil record, endogenous retroviruses and homologous genes fit base axioms like logic and mathematics. Doubting those axioms in a debate doesn't mean we can't convict murderers or accept evolution, since we can act on what follows from them.

Morality can work the same way. Bootstrap an axiom, like the goal of optimizing well-being and suffering, and logical relations follow from it. It's then a moral system, even for someone who doesn't claim the axiom is objectively true.

### Combining axioms

A system can hold more than one axiom. Threshold deontology says duties hold up to a point, and consequentialism takes over once the consequences become bad enough.
[SEP: Deontological ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-deontological/)

This isn't inconsistent, because the switch point follows from the values you assign. If killing has a value of −10,000 and saving a life has a value of +1, you wouldn't kill one person to save 5 or 1,000, but you would to save 10,001. Those exact numbers are arbitrary, like any axiom, but the answers follow consistently from them, and they'd be the same for trait-equalized humans and animals.

### Problems with other forms of utilitarianism

Negative utilitarianism only counts reducing suffering, so a world of zero suffering and infinite well-being comes out the same as an empty void.

Preference utilitarianism only counts satisfying preferences, so a strong enough preference for something horrible could justify it. Saying people never have preferences that strong is only an empirical escape, and it doesn't help in principle.

Hedonic utilitarianism has a similar problem, which is one reason to prefer a threshold view.

## Is and ought

The is-ought problem comes from Hume, and on the usual reading it says no ought can be inferred from premises that only state what is.
[SEP: Hume's moral philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-moral/)

So pain being a product of evolution doesn't show it ought not to exist. Evolution explains that pain exists, but getting to "pain is bad" takes an extra axiom.

### Unnecessary doesn't mean bad

"Unnecessary pain is unnecessary" is true by definition, but it isn't an argument. Plenty of things are unnecessary without being bad, like lifting a pen or an asteroid hitting a lifeless planet. Whether something is necessary is a separate question from whether it's good or bad, so calling pain unnecessary doesn't show it's wrong.
