> [!summary] Bottom line
> Young Earth creationism holds that the universe is around 6,000–10,000 years old. But parallax puts the typical star in our galaxy about 9,300 light years away, and 1% of stars more than 24,000 light years away. Light from those stars took that many years to reach us, so the universe must be older than a young Earth allows. Extrapolating claimed historical declines in the speed of light back 6,000 years still only lets light travel about 7,000–19,000 light years.

## Star distances

As Earth orbits the Sun, nearby stars shift slightly against more distant ones. This is parallax, and the size of the shift gives a star's distance directly, without assumptions about what the star is like.

Using the Gaia satellite's parallaxes for 1.33 billion stars:
- The median estimated distance was 2,841 parsecs, about 9,300 light years.
- 1% of stars were closer than 319 parsecs (about 1,000 light years).
- 1% were farther than 7,317 parsecs (about 24,000 light years).
- The farthest estimate was 32,979 parsecs, about 108,000 light years.

The very smallest estimates, down to 0.54 parsecs, come from faulty parallax measurements.
[Bailer-Jones 2018](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-3881/aacb21)

A light year is the distance light travels in a year, so the light we see from a typical star left it about 9,300 years ago, and for 1% of stars more than 24,000 years ago. That's already beyond 6,000–10,000 years using only stars in our own galaxy.

## Speed of light decay

One young Earth response is that light used to travel much faster, so starlight could cross these distances in a few thousand years. It rests on a compilation of about 200 historical measurements of the speed of light from 1675 to 1983, some of which came out higher than today's value.
[Dolphin & Montgomery data sets](http://www.ldolphin.org/cdata.html)

Even taking the trend in those measurements at face value doesn't give enough time. Fitting a curve of speed against year (as a multiple of today's speed) and extrapolating it back 6,000 years to about 4000 BC gives how far light could have travelled since then:
- A quadratic fit to the data gave about 19,100 light years, and 19,000 when measurements after 1967 were excluded. That was the year the second was redefined by atomic clocks, which some argue changed how the speed of light was measured.
- Weighting each measurement by its precision, the standard way to fit data with different error bars, gives only 7,300–9,800 light years.

Either way, that's short of the 24,000 light years or more to the farthest 1% of the stars Gaia measured in our own galaxy. The fits also swing widely depending on which measurements are included, because the old measurements are few and imprecise, and extrapolating a curve thousands of years past the data isn't reliable to begin with.

## The argument in premises

At an average speed under 15c, light takes more than 5,996 years to cover 89,944 light years. So:

**1. Main argument**
- P1: If light travelled over 89,944 light years at an average speed of less than 15c, the universe is more than 6,000 years old. (P → Q)
- P2: Light travelled over 89,944 light years at an average speed of less than 15c. (P)
- C: The universe is more than 6,000 years old. (Q)

**2. Support for P2:** light travelled over 89,944 light years (R), and did so at an average speed under 15c (S). (R ∧ S → P)

**3. Support for R**
- If a star is over 89,944 light years away (T), and its light travelled from the star to us through ordinary space-time rather than a wormhole (U), then light travelled over 89,944 light years. (T ∧ U → R)
- T: stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, are about 162,000 light years away. Its distance, 49.59 kiloparsecs, was measured geometrically to 1% precision using 20 eclipsing binary stars. [Pietrzyński 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30867610/)

**4. Support for U:** the stars have existed (V), we observe their light reaching us (W), and that light travelled to us through ordinary space-time (Y). (V ∧ W ∧ Y → U)

**5. Support for S:** either the speed of light is constant at c (Z), or the speed-of-light data and their fitted curves describe it accurately (X). If c is constant, the average speed is c. If the fitted curves are right, the average since 4000 BC is about 1.2–3.2c (see above). Either way it's under 15c. (X ∨ Z → S)
