> [!summary] Bottom line
> The fine-tuning argument infers a designer because life-permitting constants seem improbable by chance. If God's own nature counts as finely tuned, it leads to an infinite regress. And our universe only barely permits life, and only for a sliver of its history, which an imperfect designer predicts better than a perfect one. Appealing to an infinite universe doesn't make life any less rare. So to whatever degree the argument supports a designer, it supports a non-God designer at least as strongly.

## Who designed the designer?

If something finely tuned must have a designer, it's not clear that God's constitution wouldn't count as finely tuned, so to the extent it does, then they must have a designer and it would be infinitely regressive from there.

## The undertuning objection

The fine-tuning argument says life-permitting constants are a tiny slice of the possible values, which is improbable without a designer. The same reasoning can be applied within that slice, and there it favors an imperfect designer over God.

**Our universe barely permits life.** A perfect being setting the constants for life would be expected to tune them well, not leave the universe near the edge of the life-permitting range. But a review of fine-tuning constraints concludes that our universe "is not fully optimized for the emergence of life." Less dark energy, larger primordial density fluctuations, more baryons per photon, a smaller fine-structure constant, stable beryllium-8 or weaker gravity could all give more galaxies, stars or habitable planets. With our universe's fluctuation amplitude, the vacuum energy density can't be much larger than observed and still let galaxies form, so we sit close to that edge.
[Adams 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.03928)

**Life is fleeting.** Stars shine from about a million to 100 trillion (10^14) years after the Big Bang. After that come eras of dead stellar remnants and evaporating black holes lasting until about 10^100 years, then a dark era. So the life-permitting part of the universe's history is a tiny sliver of its timeline. This holds even if the universe is infinite and contains infinitely many living things, because the time during which life can exist is still a sliver.
[Adams 1997](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9701131)

**An infinite universe doesn't remove the rarity.** One reply is that if the universe is infinite, life isn't rare at all, since there would be infinitely many living things however thinly spread. But the same move would explain away any observation of a small quantity. A theory predicting abundant iron and one predicting very little would both predict infinite iron in an infinite universe, so finding little iron could never count against either. It also clashes with how physics handles an infinite universe. If quantities are judged by their totals, the energy available for work is infinite at every moment and can never fall, so the second law and heat death couldn't even be stated. Physics compares amounts per volume instead, and by that measure life is rare again. Comparing proportions across an infinite universe is itself the unsolved measure problem: proposed ways of handling the infinities conflict with each other or with observation, and some produce paradoxes, such as the youngness paradox, where nearly all observers should find themselves in very young regions. Even granting infinitely many living things, more could always be created, so the question of why there isn't more life remains.
[Fine-tuning debate, part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q10oCAav42I&t=41s)
[Freivogel 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.0244)
[Guth 2007](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0702178)

**An imperfect designer predicts this.** If the only planes physics allowed were like the Wright brothers' first plane, which barely flew, nobody would conclude that a perfect being designed the universe for planes. A designer who just barely got a working prototype explains it better. In the same way, an imperfect designer explains a barely life-permitting universe without extra assumptions, while a perfect designer needs added ones, such as not caring how long life lasts.

**Evidence about designers points to imperfect ones.** A theistic hypothesis can be written for any set of constants, such as a god who happens to prefer those values. So the argument relies on a further step: agents like to create things, so a designer would probably pick life-permitting constants. But every agent we've observed is imperfect and has no divine properties, so that same step raises the probability of an imperfect, non-divine designer.

This isn't the problem of evil. It concerns how little life the universe allows and for how long, not suffering, and it also counts against an evil god who wanted to create life in order to torment it. Counting every non-ideal feature of the universe as part of the problem of evil would make that argument stronger rather than absorb this one.
