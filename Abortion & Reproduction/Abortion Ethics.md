> [!summary] Bottom line
> Thomson argues a right to life doesn't include a right to use someone else's body. The debate centers on the responsibility objection (whether causing a dependent fetus to exist creates a duty to sustain it), with Boonin and Liebegott defending Thomson and Beckwith and Lang pushing back.

Thomson argues that even if a fetus is granted personhood and a right to life, this does not automatically give it a right to use another person’s body. Her violinist analogy illustrates why sustaining someone’s life can exceed what morality requires. She defends abortion in some circumstances, while distinguishing the right to end bodily support from a right to ensure death if the fetus could survive independently.
[Thomson 1971](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2265091)

Boonin-Vail argues that knowingly risking pregnancy through consensual sex does not itself constitute consent to continued bodily support, even without contraception. He also distinguishes causing a dependent person to exist from making an existing person dependent through injury. He concludes that responsibility for conception alone does not defeat Thomson’s argument; if it succeeds for pregnancies resulting from rape, the responsibility objection does not prevent its extension to consensual sex.
[Boonin-Vail 1997](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2381949)

Beckwith criticizes Boonin’s defense of abortion, arguing that a right to life rests on what kind of being someone is, rather than whether they have developed consciousness or desires. He also challenges the distinction between causing existence and causing dependency: because fetuses are dependent by nature, he argues that creating one makes parents responsible for its needs. On his view, parental obligations can arise without consent to parenthood.
[Beckwith 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/03605310600588723)

Lang argues that consensual sex need not imply consent to pregnancy for responsibility to arise. Voluntarily causing a fetus to exist in a dependent state may create a special obligation to support it, even if conception did not make it worse off than nonexistence. He concludes that Thomson’s violinist analogy does not defeat this responsibility objection, so the fetus’s moral status remains relevant to whether abortion is permissible.
[Lang (JSTOR)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/24354976)

In his dissertation, Liebegott argues that the responsibility objection is circular: establishing responsibility for a fetus’s needs does not by itself establish that continuing pregnancy is the only permissible response. His argument treats eliminating a need as one way of meeting it, so rejecting abortion on this basis requires an independent argument that abortion is wrong. He also challenges arguments based on human nature, valuable futures, and giving fetuses the benefit of the doubt. Nuts guy
[Liebegott dissertation](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI3444693/)

> [!question] Objections to prepare for
> - The violinist is a stranger, but a fetus is the woman's own child, and parents have special duties to their children.
> - Abortion usually kills the fetus directly rather than just withdrawing support (killing vs. letting die).
> - Consenting to sex is consenting to its foreseeable consequences, including pregnancy.
