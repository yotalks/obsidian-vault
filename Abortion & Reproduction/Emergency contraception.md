> [!summary] Bottom line
> Emergency contraceptive pills work mainly by delaying ovulation; the evidence argues against them preventing implantation.

Emergency contraceptive pills primarily prevent pregnancy by delaying ovulation, with evidence against an implantation-blocking mechanism.
A review found that ulipristal mainly works by delaying ovulation, including after luteinizing hormone begins rising. Most evidence argued against effects after fertilization at the emergency contraceptive dose, although some uncertainty remained.
[Rosato 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4709420/)
→ [[Menstrual cycle]]

In a small levonorgestrel study, women taking it before or on the estimated day of ovulation had no pregnancies versus four expected. Those taking it after ovulation had three pregnancies versus three to four expected, supporting little or no effect after ovulation.
[Novikova 2007](https://europepmc.org/article/med/17241840)

Another levonorgestrel study found no pregnancies among 87 women taking it before ovulation, versus 13 expected. Among 35 taking it on or after ovulation, six pregnancies occurred versus seven expected. This supports an effect before fertilization rather than prevention of implantation.
[Noé 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20399948/)

In a study recruiting 700 women, ulipristal significantly reduced pregnancies compared with the expected number when taken before ovulation, but no significant reduction was detected after ovulation. This supports ovulation delay as its main mechanism without proving that every possible post-ovulation effect is absent.
[Li 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27052501/)
