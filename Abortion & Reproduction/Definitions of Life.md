> [!summary] Bottom line
> There's no agreed definition of life. Proposals like 'self-reproduction with variations' work as research tools rather than firm boundaries.

Many definitions of life combine an individual system’s ability to maintain itself with a population’s capacity to evolve. However, there is no universally accepted definition, and these criteria are disputed. Gayon argues that definitions should function as tools for research and remain open to revision as knowledge improves.
[Gayon 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11084-010-9204-3)

Trifonov proposes that “life is self-reproduction with variations,” based on an analysis of 123 definitions of life. He argues that self-reproduction already implies the necessary materials, energy, and metabolism, while inherited variations allow evolution. This is a proposed minimal definition, rather than proof that these features alone are sufficient for life.
[Trifonov 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/073911011010524992)

Trifonov clarifies that “self-reproduction with variations” requires changes that are inherited in subsequent copies, rather than just copying errors. His definition does not require cells and would include computer viruses that reproduce with inherited variations. He presents it as a tentative guide for creating and studying minimal life, while acknowledging that its wording and method remain debatable.
[Trifonov 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22208269/)

Bich and Green argue that definitions of life can be useful without providing a universal boundary between living and nonliving things. “Operational definitions” identify features researchers can build, manipulate, or test, helping guide experiments and interpret results. Their value depends on their usefulness for research, and they should remain open to revision as evidence changes.
[Bich 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-017-1397-9)
