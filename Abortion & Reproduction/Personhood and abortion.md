> [!summary] Bottom line
> If only beings with psychological continuity are persons, abortion is permissible, but so is painlessly killing infants and severely disabled people who were never persons. Widening the definition to escape that runs into sperm rights, and narrowing it runs into killing people in dreamless sleep. Both sides face a reductio, so the debate comes down to which conclusion is harder to accept.

## The psychological continuity view

One view of what gives a being the right not to be killed is that it's a person, meaning it has a continuing sense of itself over time, with psychological connections between its past and future. A fetus doesn't have this, so abortion doesn't kill a person.

## Infants and severe disability

The problem is that a one-month-old doesn't clearly have this either. So the view implies that painlessly killing infants is permissible, and it isn't only infants. Children and adults whose disability means they've never met the definition would be in the same position.

Some bioethicists accept this for newborns, arguing that they have the same moral status as fetuses, so what's permissible for one is permissible for the other.
[Giubilini 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22361296/)

This can't be softened by calling it non-treatment, or by imagining a painless method. Controlling for violence shows the intuition isn't only about how gruesome it is.

## Defining continuity

The definition faces a dilemma:

- **Too narrow:** If continuity has to be unbroken, it wouldn't be wrong to kill someone in dreamless sleep, a coma or under anesthesia, because their psychology is interrupted. Saying the psychology is stored in a physical disposition avoids this, but only for as long as it's actually stored.
- **Too broad:** If any causal connection between conscious states counts, a sperm has a causal connection between one consciousness and a future one, which leads to sperm rights.

Adding that the being must have been a person at some point blocks sperm rights, but it brings back infants and people with severe disabilities who never were.

## The reductio on the other side

The view that sentient fetuses have a right not to be killed has a cost too. If abortion really were like killing an infant, defensive force against it would seem justified in principle, and most people find that unacceptable. In practice, the answer is changing the law, not violence.

This view also doesn't imply that women who have abortions are evil, any more than eating a hamburger makes someone evil, since most people haven't examined the ethics.

## One person's modus ponens

This is a case of one person's modus ponens being another's modus tollens. One side argues that abortion is permissible, so infanticide must be too. The other argues that infanticide is impermissible, so abortion can't be justified that way.

Pointing out that an intuition comes from culture doesn't settle it. Infanticide has been common in many societies, so the opposition to it isn't universal.
[Wikipedia: Infanticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infanticide)

But every intuition has some cultural source, including contrarian ones, so this cuts both ways. All intuitions bottom out in something unjustified, and both sides are using the same method of checking their judgments against each other, known as reflective equilibrium.
[SEP: Reflective equilibrium](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/reflective-equilibrium/)

## Miscarriage

One argument for the fetus having less value is that a miscarriage isn't treated as being as tragic as the death of an adult. But most miscarriages happen early, with about 80% in the first 12 weeks, when the fetus likely isn't sentient. A stillbirth is far more devastating, which fits value rising with sentience.
[Wikipedia: Miscarriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscarriage)
