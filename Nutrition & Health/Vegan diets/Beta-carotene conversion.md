> [!summary] Bottom line
> Vegans get vitamin A by converting beta-carotene to retinol. Conversion varies a lot between foods and people, from about 4:1 to 28:1 by weight, and gets less efficient as the dose rises. Even so, at the 6:1 ratio measured for synthetic beta-carotene, 20 mg a day would cover the 900 µg RDA for someone converting only 27% as well as average, and the lowest converter in a dose study converted about a third as well as average. Beta-carotene supplements above about 10 mg a day are linked to slightly higher mortality, though, so food sources are the safer first choice. Rare mutations that cripple conversion can be handled with preformed vitamin A supplements.

## Conversion ratios

Studies measuring how much dietary beta-carotene becomes retinol find conversion factors of 3.6–28:1 by weight, with wide variation both between studies and between individuals in the same study. The food matrix, dietary fat and genotype all affect it.
[Tang 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20200262/)

In Bangladeshi men on a low vitamin A diet, 60 days of 750 µg retinol equivalents a day raised total-body vitamin A stores. Estimated equivalency factors were about:
- **Sweet potato:** 13:1
- **Indian spinach** (*Basella alba*): 10:1
- **Synthetic beta-carotene:** 6:1
[Haskell 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15321812/)

## Dose and individual differences

In a randomized crossover study, 7 volunteers took single 20 mg and 40 mg doses of labeled beta-carotene, with blood sampled for 37 days after each. Doubling the dose doubled the beta-carotene area under the curve ([[Glossary#AUC|AUC]]) but raised the retinol AUC by only 36%, so conversion becomes less efficient as the dose increases. This also explains why high beta-carotene intakes don't cause vitamin A toxicity.

After the 20 mg dose, retinol AUC averaged 18.3 µmol/L·h but ranged from 6 to 40, so the lowest converter made about 33% as much retinol as average. Variability was smaller at the 40 mg dose (18 to 33).
[Novotny 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20237064/)

## Enough for the RDA?

The adult male RDA for vitamin A is 900 µg of retinol. At the 6:1 ratio for synthetic beta-carotene, 20 mg of beta-carotene gives about 3,333 µg of retinol, so even someone converting 27% as well as average (73% impairment) would still meet the 900 µg RDA. The lowest converter in the dose study, at about 33% of average, would get about 1,090 µg.

The 6:1 ratio was measured at about 4.5 mg of beta-carotene a day, and conversion falls at higher single doses, so 20 mg at once converts less efficiently than smaller doses spread through the day. Food sources convert at lower ratios, but carotenoid-rich foods reliably improve vitamin A status, as covered in [[Vegan diets]].

## Supplement safety

A meta-analysis of 53 low-risk-of-bias trials (241,883 people) found:
- **Beta-carotene above 9.6 mg a day** (the RDA in retinol equivalents) increased mortality: RR 1.06 (1.02–1.09), across 20 trials.
- **9.6 mg or less** had no significant effect: RR 0.90 (0.69–1.17), but only 6 trials had 14,285 people, about 5% of the sample needed for a firm answer.
- **In meta-regression**, the daily dose of beta-carotene wasn't significantly associated with mortality (P = 0.12), while the dose of preformed vitamin A was.

The median beta-carotene dose in these trials was 19.5 mg, and doses ranged from 1.2 to 50 mg.
[Bjelakovic 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0074558)

A meta-analysis of primary prevention trials found beta-carotene supplements overall had no significant effect, but beta-carotene given alone increased all-cause mortality by 6% (RR 1.06, 1.02–1.10).
[Schwingshackl 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28096125/)

## Rare poor converters

The first step of conversion is done by the enzyme BCO1 (also called CMO1). In one person with high blood carotenoids and mildly low vitamin A, a single copy of a T170M mutation cut the enzyme's activity by 90%. Symptoms like this appear when someone eats carotenoids but very little preformed vitamin A. Cases like this are very rare, and for them, a carefully dosed preformed vitamin A supplement (synthetic retinyl palmitate) can be used with a doctor's help.
[Lindqvist 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17951468/)
