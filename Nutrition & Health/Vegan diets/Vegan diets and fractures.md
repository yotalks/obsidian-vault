> [!summary] Bottom line
> In EPIC-Oxford, UK vegans had about 2.3 times the risk of hip fracture of meat eaters and about 43% more fractures overall, after adjusting for BMI. That's about 15 more hip fractures per 1,000 people over 10 years. Fish eaters and vegetarians had a smaller rise in hip fractures only. These vegans ate little calcium and were lean, and the adjustments for both were probably too coarse to remove their effect, so the estimates may overstate what a diet with enough calcium and a healthy BMI would do. A later cohort found no excess risk in vegans who took both calcium and vitamin D.

→ [[Vegan diets]]

## The study

EPIC-Oxford followed about 55,000 UK adults for an average of 17.6 years, with 3,941 hospital-recorded fractures. Diet was recorded at baseline in 1993–2001 and again around 2010 for those who answered. The comparison was against meat eaters.

| Fracture | Fish eaters | Vegetarians | Vegans | Vegan cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Total** | 0.95 (0.86–1.05) | 1.09 (1.00–1.19) | 1.43 (1.20–1.70) | 147 |
| **Hip** | 1.26 (1.02–1.54) | 1.25 (1.04–1.50) | 2.31 (1.66–3.22) | 41 |
| **Leg** | 1.07 (0.77–1.48) | 1.01 (0.76–1.34) | 2.05 (1.23–3.41) | 18 |
| **Clavicle, rib, vertebra** | 0.98 (0.73–1.33) | 0.99 (0.77–1.27) | 1.59 (1.02–2.50) | 23 |
| **Arm** | 0.92 (0.69–1.22) | 1.25 (1.00–1.56) | 1.56 (0.99–2.44) | 22 |
| **Wrist** | 0.90 (0.73–1.11) | 1.00 (0.83–1.19) | 1.24 (0.84–1.82) | 29 |
| **Ankle** | 0.84 (0.63–1.12) | 1.01 (0.80–1.27) | 1.03 (0.58–1.82) | 13 |

These are hazard ratios adjusted for BMI and lifestyle. Among clavicle, rib and vertebra fractures, only vertebral fractures were higher in vegans (HR 2.42, 1.31–4.48, 13 cases).
[Tong 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-020-01815-3)

In absolute terms, that's per 1,000 people over 10 years:

| Group | Hip fractures | Total fractures |
|---|---|---|
| **Meat eaters** | 11.5 | 47.2 |
| **Vegetarians** | 14.4 | 51.3 |
| **Vegans** | 26.5 | 66.6 |

The excess was clearer in women (total fractures HR 1.53, 1.24–1.88) than men (1.18, 0.85–1.62), but only 47 fractures were in vegan men.

## Who these vegans were

At baseline, the vegans differed from meat eaters in ways that matter for bone:

| | Meat eaters | Vegans |
|---|---|---|
| **Dietary calcium (mg/day)** | 1,005 | 591 |
| **BMI** | 24.5 | 22.1 |
| **BMI under 20** | 8.3% | 23.8% |
| **Protein (% of energy)** | 17.0 | 13.3 |
| **Hormone therapy use (women)** | 26.7% | 5.6% |
| **One or more children (women)** | 75.2% | 34.4% |

Calcium in this group was well below the UK guideline of 700 mg a day that the authors used. Fiber wasn't low, though: vegans averaged 26.8 g of fiber a day in men and 25.7 g in women, not 20 g.

Diets also changed. Of 880 vegans with a repeat measure, only 56% were still vegan about 14 years later, and 61% had been free of eggs and dairy for at least 5 years at baseline.

## How much is calcium, protein and BMI

Associations were stronger before adjusting for BMI, and they shrank a little more after calcium and protein:

| Vegans vs meat eaters | Total | Hip |
|---|---|---|
| **Without BMI** | 1.50 (1.26–1.78) | 2.64 (1.90–3.67) |
| **With BMI** | 1.43 (1.20–1.70) | 2.31 (1.66–3.22) |
| **With BMI, calcium and protein** | 1.30 (1.08–1.56) | 1.94 (1.35–2.77) |

Results held among people who met guidelines for calcium and protein (hip HR 2.43, 1.38–4.28), but only 13 vegans in that group had a hip fracture. So taken at face value, the risk isn't fully explained by these factors. The authors suggest muscle strength, falls, vitamin D, B12 or IGF-1 could also play a part, but they didn't test those. They couldn't adjust for calcium supplements at all.

### Why the adjustment may be too coarse

Adjusting by category only removes confounding between categories. Within a category, one group can still sit closer to the risky end. → [[Adjusting for confounders]]

**BMI.** Hip fracture risk rises steeply at low BMI and is fairly flat above about 25. A BMI of 20 carries about twice the hip fracture risk of a BMI of 25 (RR 1.95, 1.71–2.22).
[De Laet 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15928804/)

The study's categories were under 18.5, 18.5–19.9, 20–22.4 and 22.5–24.9, so the steepest part of the curve is covered by wide bands. Since vegans cluster toward the low end, some effect of low BMI likely remains in the estimate. Splitting by BMI points the same way: vegans under 22.5 had a hip fracture HR of 3.17 (2.13–4.71), while those at 22.5 or above had 0.94 (0.38–2.29), though the second group had only 5 cases.

BMI isn't only a confounder, though. If the diet itself keeps people lean, adjusting for BMI removes part of the diet's effect, so neither model is simply the true one.

**Calcium.** The same problem may apply here, to a lesser degree. In 61,433 Swedish women followed for 19 years, the lowest quintile of calcium intake had a higher risk of hip fracture (HR 1.29, 1.17–1.43), and intakes above that didn't lower risk further. The highest quintile had a slightly higher rate of hip fracture (HR 1.19, 1.06–1.32).
[Warensjö 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21610048/)
![[Warensjo 2011 calcium and hip fracture.png]]

So risk falls steeply up to about 800 mg a day and is roughly flat after. Reading the curve, the hazard ratio is about 1.75 at 200 mg and about 1.3 at 500 mg.

The EPIC-Oxford categories were under 525, 525–699, 700–899, 900–1,199 and 1,200 mg or more. Everyone under 525 mg was one category, even though that's where the curve is steepest, and vegans averaged 591 mg (SD 237) against 1,005 mg in meat eaters. So some effect of low calcium likely remains. It's a smaller problem than BMI because the calcium curve doesn't rise as far.

Intake from food questionnaires also has real error, which leaves more confounding behind.

**Protein.** This looks like much less of a problem. In a meta-analysis of 12 cohorts with 407,104 people, the highest total protein intakes were linked to less hip fracture than the lowest (RR 0.89, 0.82–0.97, 6 studies). There was no clear difference for animal (1.04, 0.70–1.54) or vegetable protein (1.00, 0.53–1.91) alone.
[Wu 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep09151)
![[Wu 2015 protein and hip fracture.jpg]]

The total protein curve is nearly flat from about 45 to 110 g a day, sitting a little below 1, and its confidence bands are wide. There's no steep section like there is for BMI and calcium.

The EPIC-Oxford categories were under 13, 13–14.4, 14.5–15.9, 16–18.0 and 18.1% or more of energy. From their average energy and protein shares, vegans ate about 58 g a day and meat eaters about 84 g, and both sit on the flat part. So coarse protein categories likely left little behind, which fits adjustment for protein moving the vegan hip fracture HR only from 2.31 to 2.21, while calcium moved it to 1.97.

### Breastfeeding

Vegans here were much less likely to have had children, which raises the question of breastfeeding. Breastfeeding is linked to less hip fracture (RR 0.64, 0.43–0.95), and the link flattens out by about 25 months.
[Xiao 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6966889/)

The study adjusted for number of children (none, 1–2, 3 or more), so this is mostly accounted for, even though breastfeeding itself wasn't measured.

## Limits

- Only 1,982 vegans took part, and many subgroups had few cases.
- Hospital records miss fractures that didn't need admission, and the causes of fractures weren't recorded, so fragility fractures and accidents can't be separated.
- Past osteoporosis and bone medication weren't recorded.
- The cohort was mostly white and 77% women, and these diets were recorded from 1993 on, so they may not match vegan diets today.

Excluding the first 5 years of follow-up didn't change much, so low-BMI or sick people changing their diet doesn't seem to explain it.

## Calcium and vitamin D

In Adventist Health Study 2, vegan women had a higher risk of hip fracture than nonvegetarians (HR 1.55, 1.06–2.26), with no link in men. Vegans who took both calcium and vitamin D had no excess risk.
[Thorpe 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33964850/)

That's still observational, but it fits calcium and vitamin D being a big part of the problem. So the practical takeaway isn't that vegan diets break bones. It's that a vegan diet low in calcium and at a low BMI is a risk worth fixing.
