> [!summary] Bottom line
> Natural water and soil aren't realistic sources of B12. Lakes and seas mostly have a few nanograms per liter, so meeting the 2.4 µg RDA would take hundreds of liters a day. The only water close to drinkable amounts was a New York pond during a seasonal Euglena bloom, and most of its readings were still far too low. Soil has far more B12 by weight, but you'd need to eat tens of grams a day at the very least. B12 rises in water around algal blooms, which is the water you'd least want to drink, and B12 in blue-green algae and marine mud is often an inactive form.

→ [[Vegan diets]]

## How much water it would take

The adult RDA is 2.4 µg, or 2,400 ng.
[NIH: Vitamin B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/)

| Water | B12 (ng/L) | Liters a day at the highest value |
|---|---|---|
| **English Lake District, 9 lakes** | Mostly under 15, up to about 20 | About 120 |
| **Lake Biwa, Japan** | 0.19–6.06 | About 400 |
| **Lake Kojima, Japan** | 2.1–36 | About 67 |
| **Lake Kinneret (Sea of Galilee)** | Up to about 100, at 40 m deep | 24 |
| **Mediterranean Sea** | About 0.7–8.4 | About 290 |
| **Pond in New York, Euglena bloom** | 100–2,000 | 1.2 |

Liters are my calculation from the highest value recorded, so they're the best case for each. At typical levels it takes far more. Seawater is measured in picomolar, where 1 pM is about 1.36 ng/L.
[Daisley 1969](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1969.14.2.0224)
[Kurata 1981](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jnsv1973/27/4/27_4_301/_pdf/-char/ja)
[Nishijima 1977](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/suisan1932/43/12/43_12_1403/_article/-char/en)
[Cavari 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/907339/)
[Bonnet 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3677149/)

These are peaks, and B12 swings with the seasons. In Lake Biwa and Lake Kojima it was highest in late summer or autumn. Lake Kinneret's peak of 100 ng/L was deep in the lake in November and December, not at the surface. In Lake Tahoe, vitamins were found almost only in the sunlit upper layer.
[Carlucci 1972](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1972.17.5.0774)

## The 2,000 figure

The claim that natural water had 100 to 2,000 of B12 is a units problem. That range is in µµg/ml, an old unit for picograms per ml, which works out to 0.1–2 µg/L. It's the highest figure in natural water here, and it comes from a small pond on the grounds of the New York Botanical Garden, which gets a coating of Euglena every year. It isn't from the English lakes, which were much lower.

Nine readings from November 1949 to August 1950 gave:

| Date | µg/L |
|---|---|
| Nov 23 | 2.0 |
| Jan 23 | 1.0 |
| Feb 2 | 0.2 |
| Mar 20 | 0.1 |
| Jun 9 | 0.4 |
| Jun 29 | 0.1 |
| Jul 11 | 0.6 |
| Jul 13 | 0.2 |
| Aug 21 | 0.2 |

[JSTOR: Euglena pond water](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2482180)

So only 2 of 9 readings reached 1 µg/L. Most readings would take 4–24 liters a day, from one pond with a bloom on it.

## Blooms

Water with more B12 tends to be water with algal blooms. Nearly all harmful bloom species need B12 from their environment: all but one of 27 species tested. Lake Biwa had dinoflagellate blooms, and in the ocean, dissolved B12 reached 61 pM (about 83 ng/L) during a dinoflagellate bloom in Todos Santos Bay, compared with 0.2–2 pM in the North Atlantic.
[Tang 2010](https://www.pnas.org/content/107/48/20756)
[Panzeca 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecss.2009.08.016)

Upper Klamath Lake is the best-known case. During an *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae* bloom, its phytoplankton held up to 5 µg of B12 per gram of dry material, and sediment up to 1.8 µg per gram.
[Gillespie 1972](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC380342/)

That doesn't make it a good source, for two reasons:

- **Most of it is inactive.** *Aphanizomenon* tested at 616 µg per 100 g by a microbial assay, but only about 5% of that by an intrinsic factor assay. The purified compound was pseudovitamin B12, which is inactive in humans.
[Miyamoto 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17147452/)
- **It can be contaminated.** Toxic *Microcystis* grows alongside *Aphanizomenon* in the lake. Microcystins were found in 85 of 87 blue-green algae supplement samples, and 72% were above Oregon's limit of 1 µg/g.
[Gilroy 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10811570/)

So the same caution as duckweed applies: finding B12 in or around something doesn't make it a reliable source. → [[Vegan diets]]

## Mud

Coastal marsh mud and suspended solids can look rich in B12, but those figures are per gram of solids, with the water taken out of the calculation. That isn't what's in a mouthful of muddy water.
[Burkholder 1956](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1956.1.3.0202)

The bigger problem is the form. Marine bacteria make cobalamins in culture and in marine mud, but a substantial part of what they produce under natural conditions seems to be pseudovitamin B12 and related forms. Older tests counted these as B12, even though pseudovitamin B12 is inactive in humans.
[Ayers 1960](https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.80.6.744-752.1960)

Inactive forms may not just be useless. In B12-deficient children eating algae and fermented plant foods, plasma B12 rose, but red blood cell size, a sign of deficiency, got worse. It improved in children given fish or a B12 supplement. That shows a raised B12 reading doesn't mean B12 status improved, though it doesn't show the algae caused the decline, since the children were deficient to begin with.
[Dagnelie 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2000824/)

## Soil

Various soil B12 measurements have been made, including in natural forest soil, which we wouldn't expect to be affected by pesticides or herbicides. So low soil B12 isn't just a result of modern farming. Reported values run from 0.2 to about 70 µg/kg.
[Mozafar 1994](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00007957)
![[Mozafar 1994 soil B12 table.png]]

The 98 µg/kg figure from Kirakosyan 1970 in that table appears to be an error. The highest value of B12 concentration reported in that study was actually 70 µg/kg, in the top layer of a carbonate chernozem.
![[Kirakosyan 1970 soil B12 table.webp]]

B12 falls with depth and varies by soil. Forest soil was 15–42 µg/kg near the surface and 1–18 µg/kg in the deepest layer, and semi-desert soil was 0–9 µg/kg, with none in some samples.

| Soil | B12 (µg/kg) | Soil for 2.4 µg |
|---|---|---|
| **Highest reported** | 70 | About 34 g |
| **Forest topsoil, high end** | 42 | About 57 g |
| **Forest topsoil, low end** | 15 | About 160 g |
| **Lower reported values** | 2 | About 1.2 kg |

For scale, a common default for how much soil and dust adults swallow by accident is 100 mg a day. Even at the highest value, that's 0.007 µg, or 0.3% of the RDA.
[ATSDR: Soil and sediment ingestion](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/pha-guidance/resources/ATSDR-EDG-Soil-Sediment-Ingestion-508.pdf)

These figures may also overstate active B12. The table counts B12 "or biologically active corrinoids," and in foods, as much as 80% of the activity measured by some methods comes from inactive analogues.
[Herbert 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3046314/)

### Unwashed vegetables

A common claim is that strict vegetarians in Iran avoided B12 deficiency because their vegetables were grown in human manure and not washed well. It's repeated in that same review, but the study behind it doesn't show this. These 23 villagers ate milk products once or twice a week and a little meat or chicken up to once a month, so this wasn't a vegan group. Some who ate no animal protein were still deficient, and how long anyone had eaten this way wasn't reported, even though B12 deficiency can take years to develop.
[Halsted 1960](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14399173/)

Manure can raise B12 in some crops, but not by much. Cow dung raised barley kernels from 2.6 to 9.1 ng/g and spinach leaves from 6.9 to 17.8 ng/g dry weight, and it didn't change soybeans. Even the manured spinach would take about 135 g of dry leaves for 2.4 µg.
[Mozafar 1994](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00007957)

## Feces

Some vegans bring up human feces as a source for B12...

98% of B12 from poo is pseudo B12
1309 ng cobalamin equivalents/g wet weight of feces. Cobalamin was around 1.4% of this.

So 1.3ug * 0.014% = 0.0182ug B12 per g feces.

So you need 131 grams of poo a day to reach the 2.4ug RDA.

This is a little over a quarter pound of poo you'd have to eat a day.
[Allen 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18469256/)

### Animal feces

Consuming animal poo for B12 also has several considerations.

1. Evaluating B12 content in animal poo requires a pseudo B12 percentage.
2. Dry weight don't exist in nature, so they need to be converted into wet weight.
3. Evaluated animals may get B12 supplements and B12 feed supplements as part of farming and invalidates the measurements as it's not what naturally happens.
4. Many animal species didn't exist for the overwhelming majority of our evolutionary existence.

No study on B12 content of animal poo has met the above criteria.

## Watercress

This study is often used to support the idea that watercress can be a plant source of B12 in nature.
[Lawrence 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chembiol.2018.04.012)

Firstly, this was grown in agar, not soil. Agar doesn't exist in nature. This can be important as it's generally accepted that nutrients (and just about any solute in general) have a much higher diffusivity in agar compared to soil.
![[Pasted image 20261105194952.png]]
[Watt 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2803425/)

The concentration of B12 that the watercress was grown in was higher than any of the measurements of soil B12 we have available. Even in natural soil sources. Even favoring the density of soil (which is usually lower in natural sources).
![[Pasted image 20261105195057.png]]

Even assuming we somehow found a B12 soil source with real B12 that managed to have a concentration of 0.1mg/L bioavailable B12 (which has never been found in soil). It still wouldn't be realistic to get B12 from watercress.

Even if watercress grew in 100ug/L soil, where it was bioavailable B12, this would lead to a B12 concentration of 1pg/mg in watercress. That's 1ug/kg. This means you'd need 2.4kg of watercress a day to meet the B12 RDA. This is more of a volume issue than a mass issue. 34g of watercress chopped up takes up around 1 cup of volume.
![[Pasted image 20261105195323.png]]

This means 2400g of chopped up watercress takes 70.588 cups, which is 16.7 liters, or 4.41 gallons.

And if more realistic values of B12 concentration in soil were used (around 30ug/kg), you'd end up needing around 5 kg which would take around 147 cups or 34 liters or 9.18 gallons.
You'd probably end up in the ER before you'd get enough watercress to meet the RDA.
The FSRG database has watercress containing 0ug of B12.
[USDA: FNDDS](https://www.ars.usda.gov/northeast-area/beltsville-md-bhnrc/beltsville-human-nutrition-research-center/food-surveys-research-group/docs/fndds-download-databases/)

## Fermented foods

We wouldn't expect P. freudenreichii alone, nor P.freudenreichii and its co-lactobacillus to be the only fermenters in nature, let alone the most prevalent fermenters on whatever native grain products we consumed to such a degree that the role of other fermenters would be inconsequential. This setup will almost certainly require artificial conditions as employed by this paper (isolating and growing it for inoculation to ensure it's the dominant microbe fermenter). I'm not aware of any natural conditions where this wouldn't just be polymicrobial. The reason this is important is because other fermenters produce pseudo B12, which is dangerous and makes your B12 status worse. P. freudenreichii is the only microbe for this purpose that is generally recognized as safe by the FDA. And even in this study, with all the artificial conditions trying to make this work, one of their batches still had pseudo B12. 
[LWT 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0023643824007990)
