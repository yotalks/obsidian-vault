> [!summary] Bottom line
> Most studies find vegan and vegetarian children grow within the normal range, and vegetarian children can be as tall as or taller than meat-eaters. The largest recent study of young vegan children found them about 3 cm shorter, with lower bone mineral content and more B12 and iron deficiency, alongside better cholesterol. A well-planned vegan diet with supplements, especially B12 and vitamin D, seems able to support normal growth, but how often families plan it well is unknown. After correcting that study for multiple comparisons, the height, bone and iron differences are no longer significant, while lower cholesterol and fat mass, and lower B12 and vitamin D in children not supplementing, remain.

## Growth

**The Farm (Tennessee, 1980s).** In 404 children aged 4 months to 10 years from a community that followed a vegan diet based on soybeans, with vitamins A, D and B12 added to soy milk, most heights and weights were between the 25th and 75th percentiles of the US reference. They were slightly below the median: 0.2–2.1 cm shorter and 0.1–1.1 kg lighter depending on age, with the biggest gap at 1–3 years. By age 10 they averaged only 0.7 cm and 1.1 kg below the median (0.1 and 0.3 SD).
[O'Connell 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2771551/)

**British vegan children.** Children born to vegan mothers, breastfed for at least 6 months and raised vegan, mostly grew and developed normally, but tended to be smaller and lighter than population standards. Energy, calcium and vitamin D intakes were usually below recommendations, and a few had low riboflavin and B12, though most parents knew to supplement B12.
[Sanders 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3414589/)

**Lacto-ovo vegetarians.** Among 1,765 children aged 7–18 in Southern California, Seventh-day Adventist vegetarians (meat less than once a week) were taller than their meat-eating classmates, by 2.5 cm for boys and 2.0 cm for girls. This held after adjusting for other foods, and for parental height and socioeconomic status in a subsample.
[Sabaté 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1855500/)

**German toddlers (VeChi Diet).** Among 430 vegetarian, vegan and omnivorous children aged 1–3, energy intake, energy density, weight and height didn't differ between groups. Omnivores ate the most protein and vegans the most carbohydrate.
[Weder 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31013738/)

**German children and teens (VeChi Youth).** Among 401 participants aged 6–18 (149 vegetarian, 115 vegan, 137 omnivore), height, weight and BMI z-scores didn't differ between groups.
[Alexy 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34069944/)

**Indonesian children.** Over 6 months, 22 vegan and age- and sex-matched omnivorous children had similar IGF-1 (105.5 vs 102.7 ng/mL), IGFBP-3 and growth velocity (3.0 vs 3.2 cm), though the sample was very small.
[Purwana 2021](https://ejournal2.undip.ac.id/index.php/jbtr/article/view/5474)

**Polish children.** In a cross-sectional study of 187 children aged 5–10 (63 vegetarian, 52 vegan, 72 matched omnivores), vegans were on average 3.15 cm shorter than omnivores (−0.57 height z-score). Vegetarians were 1.9 cm shorter, but that wasn't significant. Both had lower bone mineral content, and in vegans it stayed lower after accounting for body size (3.7% less in the total body excluding the head, 5.6% at the lumbar spine). Vegans had less body fat but similar lean mass.
[Desmond 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33740036/)

## Nutrient status

In the Polish study, vegans had better cardiovascular markers than omnivores: LDL cholesterol 24 mg/dL lower and lower C-reactive protein, though HDL was also 12 mg/dL lower. But they had more deficiencies:

| | Omnivores | Vegetarians | Vegans |
|---|---|---|---|
| Probable B12 deficiency | 3% | 4% | 13% |
| Possible B12 deficiency | 16% | 19% | 40% |

Vegans also had more iron-deficiency anemia and low ferritin, and lower vitamin D in those not taking supplements. Vegetarians, unexpectedly, had a less favorable cardiometabolic profile, with higher glucose, VLDL and triglycerides.
[Desmond 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33740036/)

The German VeChi Youth study found no specific nutritional risks for vegetarian or vegan children compared with omnivores. Hemoglobin, vitamin B2, vitamin D, HDL and triglycerides didn't differ between groups, though ferritin was higher in omnivores and vegans had the lowest LDL. Over 30% of every group had low vitamin D and vitamin B2. Supplement use was common among vegans: 88% took B12 and 54% vitamin D.
[Alexy 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34069944/)

A review concluded that, apart from a few outliers, vegan children grow normally and are less often obese. B12, calcium and vitamin D are the biggest risks of a poorly planned vegan diet, and a well-planned diet with supplements likely provides enough of the critical nutrients for normal growth. But it's unknown how often vegan children actually get a well-planned diet, and data on omega-3s, zinc, iodine and selenium are lacking.
[Sutter 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34130207/)

## How child growth is judged

Growth is assessed by plotting repeated measurements on growth charts and comparing them over time.
![[CDC boys stature and weight for age 2-20.png]]
[CDC: Growth charts](https://www.cdc.gov/growthcharts)

Children are defined as stunted if their height-for-age is more than 2 standard deviations below the WHO growth standard median.
[WHO: Stunting in a nutshell](https://www.who.int/news/item/19-11-2015-stunting-in-a-nutshell)

A child's expected adult height can be estimated from the midparental height: the average of the parents' heights, after adding 13 cm to the mother's height for boys or subtracting 13 cm from the father's for girls (13 cm is the average height difference between men and women). Normal growth velocity is about 5 cm a year from age 5 until puberty, faster in early childhood and during the pubertal growth spurt. Referral to a pediatric endocrinologist is suggested for height more than 3 standard deviations below the mean, growth velocity under 5 cm a year, or a projected height more than 2 standard deviations (about 10 cm) below the midparental height.
![[Normal growth rates in children.png]]
[Barstow 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26132126/)

## Interpreting the Polish study

### Height

Vegan children's height z-score was 0.55 lower than omnivores' with diet alone in the model, and 0.57 lower after adjusting for parental height, birth weight, breastfeeding, activity, parental education and other factors, about 3.15 cm. That's statistically significant (95% CI −1.02 to −0.12), but far from the −2 SD that defines stunting. Vegans also had lower BMI and fat mass z-scores, with similar lean mass.
![[Desmond 2021 anthropometry table.png]]
[Desmond 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33740036/)

### Correcting for multiple comparisons

The study made 47 comparisons, or 34 after counting different measures of the same thing once. A Bonferroni correction for 34 comparisons gives a threshold of P < 0.00147 → [[Multiple comparisons]]. P values can be worked out from the reported confidence intervals.
[Altman 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22803193/)

For vegans compared with omnivores in the most adjusted models, these differences stay significant after the correction:

| Outcome | Difference | P |
|---|---|---|
| Fat mass index z-score | −0.72 | 0.0004 |
| Total cholesterol | −32.1 mg/dL | 0.0000014 |
| HDL cholesterol | −12.7 mg/dL | 0.0000075 |
| LDL cholesterol | −20.5 mg/dL | 0.0004 |
| Vitamin B12, no supplements or fortified foods | −217.6 pmol/L | 0.0000013 |
| Homocysteine, no supplements or fortified foods | +50% | 0.00003 |
| Vitamin D (25(OH)D), no supplements | −13.3 nmol/L | 0.0002 |

Height (P ≈ 0.013), all bone measures (P ≈ 0.01–0.03), hemoglobin, ferritin and the rates of anemia and depleted iron stores don't pass the corrected threshold.

### Bone

Vegans had 16.4% less total body bone mineral content (excluding the head) and 10.5% less at the lumbar spine with only age and sex adjusted. Bone mineral content depends on body size, and only the third model adjusted for height, weight and bone area. With that, the gap shrank to 3.7% and 5.6%. Bone mineral apparent density (BMAD) adjusts for bone volume, so it accounts for height, but not weight. Vegans' BMAD z-score was 0.62 lower.
![[Desmond 2021 bone table.png]]

Weight matters for weight-bearing bones in particular: in adolescent boys, weight, lean mass and BMI were linked to higher BMAD at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, but not for the whole body or forearm.
[El Hage 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21188608/)

For scale, total body bone mineral content in 112 healthy 12-year-old girls ranged from 799 to 2,083 g, with a mean of 1,276 g and an SD of 272 g (about 21% of the mean). A 3.7% difference is small next to the normal spread between children.
![[Lloyd 1992 bone mineral content table.png]]
![[Lloyd 1992 bone mineral content histogram.png]]
[Lloyd 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1597730/)

### Iron

Vegans had lower red blood cell counts, hemoglobin (−0.37 g/dL) and ferritin (25% lower). The hematocrit differences are reported as −83% to −105%, which can't be right for a measure that's normally around 40%. They look like they were multiplied by 100 by mistake, which would make the difference about 1 percentage point, leaving vegans within the normal range.
![[Desmond 2021 iron table.png]]

No child had severe anemia. Moderate iron-deficiency anemia affected 0% of omnivores and about 2% of vegetarians and vegans, and mild anemia 0%, 7% and 6%. Depleted iron stores (ferritin under 15 µg/L) affected 12.8% of omnivores, 18.3% of vegetarians and 30.2% of vegans.
![[Desmond 2021 deficiency prevalence table.png]]

### B12

Vegans who took neither supplements nor fortified foods had much lower B12 (−217.6 pmol/L) and 50% higher homocysteine than omnivores. But vegetarians eating B12-fortified foods, and vegetarians and vegans using both supplements and fortified foods, didn't differ from omnivores in B12, homocysteine or red cell size. Nearly a third of the vegetarian and vegan children got neither.
![[Desmond 2021 B12 by supplementation table.png]]
[Desmond 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33740036/)
