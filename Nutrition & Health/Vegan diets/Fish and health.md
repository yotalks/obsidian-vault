> [!summary] Bottom line
> Eating fish looks healthy, but how healthy may depend on how contaminated it is. Older cohorts found neutral or mixed results for fish, while newer ones mostly find benefits, as levels of mercury and dioxins in fish have fallen. Pesco-vegetarians had the lowest mortality in the Adventist Health Study 2, and that gap with vegans isn't explained by B12, omega-3s or vitamin D, since trials of those don't lower heart disease.

## Contaminants and changing results

Cohort studies from around the 1990s or earlier show positive, neutral or negative health effects of eating fish, while cohorts from the 2000s onward show mostly benefits. That matches falling levels of mercury, methylmercury and dioxins in fish over the same period. So eating fish is healthy, but at some point its benefits can be outweighed by the toxins that come with it.

In the EPIC cohort, over 500,000 people in ten European countries filled in diet questionnaires in 1992–1999 and were followed until 2010. Fish intake wasn't associated with overall, ischemic heart disease or cancer mortality, though there was a U-shaped trend for fatty fish and total mortality, and for total fish and cancer mortality.
[Engeset 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4356893/)

In the Adventist Health Study 2, recruited in 2002–2007, pesco-vegetarians had the lowest mortality of any diet group (HR 0.81, 0.69–0.94, vs nonvegetarians) → [[Vegetarian diets and mortality]].
[Orlich 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4191896/)

In the same cohort, pesco-vegetarians had 43% fewer colorectal cancers than nonvegetarians (HR 0.57, 0.40–0.82), the biggest reduction of any vegetarian group. Vegans' risk wasn't significantly lower (HR 0.84, 0.59–1.19).
[Orlich 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25751512/)

In a Chinese case-control study (1,189 cases, 2010–2014), people eating the most freshwater fish had 53% lower odds of colorectal cancer than those eating the least (OR 0.47, 0.36–0.60), and sea fish was also inversely associated (OR 0.79, 0.62–0.99). Dried or salted fish and shellfish weren't associated.
[Xu 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26264963/)

In over 2,300 samples of Norwegian farmed salmon from 1999 to 2011, levels of mercury, arsenic, dioxins, dioxin-like PCBs and DDT all fell. By 2011, dioxins and dioxin-like PCBs, the limiting contaminants, allowed up to 1.3 kg of salmon a week before reaching the tolerable weekly intake.
[Nøstbakken 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25454244/)

In a systematic review of 12 cohorts, mostly exposed to dioxins at work, the highest-quality studies found consistent, dose-related increases in ischemic heart disease mortality with dioxin exposure and smaller increases in all cardiovascular mortality. Most didn't adjust for the main cardiovascular risk factors.
[Humblet 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2592261/)

## Pesco-vegetarians vs vegans

Several nutrients have been suggested to explain why pesco-vegetarians might do better than vegans. Trials don't support any of them for heart disease.

### B12

B vitamins lower homocysteine, but trials don't show that lowering it prevents heart disease:
- In 3,096 Norwegian patients having coronary angiography, folic acid plus B12 lowered homocysteine 30% but didn't reduce deaths or cardiovascular events over a median 38 months (HR 1.09, 0.90–1.32). [Ebbing 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18714059/)
- In 5,442 high-risk women, a daily pill of folic acid, B6 and B12 for 7.3 years lowered homocysteine 18.5% but didn't reduce cardiovascular events (RR 1.03, 0.90–1.19). [Albert 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18460663/)
- In a meta-analysis of 8 trials with 37,485 people, folic acid lowered homocysteine 25% but didn't change major vascular events (RR 1.01, 0.97–1.05), coronary events or stroke over 5 years. [Clarke 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20937919/)

In a 29-year Australian cohort of 2,950 adults, blood folate and B12 levels weren't associated with death from coronary heart disease or cardiovascular disease after adjusting for age and standard risk factors.
[Hung 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12531843/)

### Omega-3s

There's no conclusive evidence that omega-3s would explain the difference, because meta-analyses of omega-3 supplements are mixed. Most found no effect on mortality or cardiovascular events, and only high doses in people with heart disease showed fewer cardiac deaths and heart attacks → [[Omega-3 supplementation]].

### Vitamin D

In a trial sequential meta-analysis, vitamin D supplements, with or without calcium, didn't change the risk of heart attack or ischemic heart disease (9 trials, 48,647 people) or stroke (8 trials, 46,431 people) by 15% or more, and future trials of similar design are unlikely to change that.
[Bolland 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24703049/)
