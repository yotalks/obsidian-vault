> [!summary] Bottom line
> Soy doesn't change testosterone or estrogen in men: two meta-analyses of clinical trials, the latest covering 41 studies, found no effect at any dose or duration. In premenopausal women it doesn't change estrogen either, and its small effects on FSH, LH and cycle length aren't clinically meaningful. Soy is linked to lower risk of prostate, lung and gastrointestinal cancer, and isn't linked to mortality either way. Several of these meta-analyses have soy industry ties.

## Hormones in men

A small cross-sectional study of 69 Japanese men found that those eating more soy had lower estradiol, and borderline lower total and free testosterone. This is observational, and the clinical trials below don't show it.
[Nagata 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10798211/)

Early intervention studies in men, giving 40–70 mg a day of isoflavones from soy foods or supplements, found few effects on blood hormones or semen quality.
[Kurzer 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jn/article/132/3/570S/4687378)

A meta-analysis of clinical studies found no effect of soy protein or isoflavones on total testosterone, free testosterone, SHBG or the free androgen index in men.
[Hamilton-Reeves 2010](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0015028209009662)

An updated meta-analysis of 41 studies (total testosterone measured in 1,753 men) also found no effect on total or free testosterone, estradiol, estrone or SHBG, regardless of isoflavone dose or study duration.
[Reed 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0890623820302926)

These meta-analyses measured hormones, not fertility. One co-author on both, Mark Messina, consults for companies that sell soy products. The 2021 analysis had no funding source.

## Hormones in women

In premenopausal women, a meta-analysis of trials found soy or isoflavones didn't change estradiol, estrone or SHBG, the primary outcomes. [[Glossary#LH|FSH and LH]] fell by about 20%, and menstrual cycle length increased by about 1 day (1.05 days, 95% CI 0.13–1.97). In postmenopausal women there were no significant effects on estradiol, estrone, SHBG, FSH or LH.
[Hooper 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2691652/)
→ [[Menstrual cycle]]

A 1-day longer cycle isn't clinically meaningful. Cycles normally range from about 21 to 35 days.
[Cleveland Clinic: menstrual cycle](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/10132-menstrual-cycle)

The FSH and LH drops are also shaky. They were significant only with the [[Mean difference vs standardized mean difference|standardized mean difference]] (SMD). With the plain mean difference, neither was significant:
- FSH: −0.52 (95% CI −1.15 to 0.11)
- LH: −1.26 (95% CI −3.30 to 0.78)

The authors chose SMD because baseline hormone levels varied between studies, treating that as if the studies used different scales. But that's a difference between populations, not between measurement scales, so the mean difference is the better choice here. When only low-risk-of-bias trials were kept, no trials were left for these outcomes, and the authors called the results tentative.

The review was partly funded by the Soy Nutrition Institute, and Messina was a co-author.

## Cancer

- **Prostate:** in a meta-analysis of 2 cohort and 6 case-control studies, soy food was linked to 30% lower prostate cancer risk ([[Glossary#RR|RR]] 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.83). The first author worked for Solae, a soy ingredient company. [Yan 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15945102/)
- **Lung:** in a meta-analysis of 8 case-control and 3 cohort studies, soy was linked to 23% lower lung cancer risk (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.65–0.92). The link was strongest in never smokers (0.62). Soy was measured differently across studies, so better-designed cohorts or trials are still needed. [Yang 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3252551/)
- **Gastrointestinal:** in a meta-analysis of 22 prospective studies, soy was linked to lower overall GI cancer (0.857, 95% CI 0.766–0.959) and gastric cancer (0.847, 95% CI 0.722–0.994), but not colorectal cancer. By sex, the link held in women (0.711, 95% CI 0.506–0.999) but not men. [Lu 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-03692-y)

These are associations, mostly from case-control and cohort studies (see [[Observational study designs]]).

## Mortality

In a meta-analysis of 7 cohorts (627,209 people, 39,250 deaths), high soy intake wasn't linked to all-cause mortality (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.90–1.02), cardiovascular mortality (0.95) or cancer mortality (0.98). Only 3 of the cohorts reported all-cause mortality.
[Namazi 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29666853/)
