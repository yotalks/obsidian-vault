> [!summary] Bottom line
> The '84% quit' claim misreads the study, and vegans can cover nutrient gaps: B12, calcium, iodine and vitamin D through supplements or fortification, vitamin A from carotenoids, and no demonstrated harm from lower taurine, carnosine or omega-3 levels.

## Adherence

This study is often cited to claim that 84% of vegans eventually quit their diet and therefore it is not good. Firstly, this is incorrect because it found a 70% dropout, not 84%. And people stop doing all kinds of health promoting things for all kinds of reasons. They also state the reasons of quitting and the craving of animal products was only in a minority.
[Faunalytics 2014](https://faunalytics.org/how-many-former-vegetarians-and-vegans-are-there/)

## Nutrients

B12 is indeed not found in plants, however, supplements are widely available.

Carnosine is also not found, but RCTs show omnivores taking on a vegetarian diet didn't find decreased carnosine levels in muscle. Only observational cross sectional studies found this.
[Harris 2007](https://faseb.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1096/fasebj.21.6.a944-a)
[Blancquaert 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29569535/)

This was for 3 months, is that enough to see a difference? Human muscle half life for carnosine isn't fully known, but we do have data in rats that it's about 28 days.
[Blancquaert 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5009790/pdf/TJP-594-4849.pdf) [Tamaki 1980](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jnsv1973/26/2/26_2_127/_pdf/-char/en)

With vitamin a, there is no outcome based evidence that vegans have a higher rate of vitamin A deficiency or diseases associated with vitamin A deficiency. There are two studies that assessed retinol status of vegans after excluding those who supplement, bot found they had statistically significantly lower levels than omnivores, but the differences were not clinically significant.
[Li 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24394311/)
[Schüpbach 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26502280/)

Even mechanistically, accounting for difficulties with beta carotene to retinol conversion, vegans have multiple ways of easily converting enough beta carotene to the 900ug RDA of retinol. 

When we try to correct vitamin A deficiency with dietary carotenoids in humans, we see eating whole foods rich in carotenoids reliably improves and/or normalizes vitamin A status. Even foods that have been genetically modified to have more carotenoids improve vitamin A status reliably.
[van Jaarsveld 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15883432/)
[Ribaya-Mercado 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17413103/)
[de Pee 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9808223/)
[Tang 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10584052/)
[Tang 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10584052/)
[Tang 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16210712/)

We even have a study that found no differences in retinol status between BCO1 genotypes consuming low amounts of preformed retinol.
[Graßmann 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32560166/)

What's likely happening is that these genetic variants aren't changing the total retinol being converted from carotenoids rather the conversion curve changes shape but the area under the curve remains unchanged, so they convert the same amount just at a slower rate.

Sweet potatoes have a baseline conversion ratio of about 13:1 
 [Tang 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2854912/)
1 cup of mashed sweet potatoes is ~328 grams.
 Beta carotene content of sweet potatoes have been estimated to be 55-124ug/g wet weight. Taking an average value of 89.5ug/g wet weight.
 [Müller 1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s002170050042)
That gives us 29,356ug of b-carotene per cup of mashed sweet potato. A conversion rate of 13:1 gives us 2258ug of retinol. That means that even if one were to have a 60% conversion impairment, they would still get over 900ug of retinol just with 1 cup of mashed sweet potatoes. And if one were to have a 80% conversion impairment, they would still get over 900ug of retinol with 2 cups of mashed sweet potatoes.

You can also supplement with synthetic beta carotene as is found in multivitamins, which has a conversion ratio of 6:1. Meaning that even if one has a 75% impairment of conversion, they will still get more than 900ug RDA retinol just with a 25mg dose of beta carotene. 
[Novotny 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2855261/)
[Haskell 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15321812/)

The only cases of vitamin A toxicity in the literature involved the consumption of preformed retinol from liver, where even a child died from consuming chicken liver pate sandwiches.
[Homma 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25850632/)
[Dewailly 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21902932/)
[Nagai 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10424294/)
[van Stuijvenberg 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31089689/)
[Carpenter 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3655980/)

Vegans can supplement with calcium to reach the RDA without any issue.

Creatine is not necessary (though can of course be supplemented like bodybuilders do).

Iodine can be achieved with iodized salt

Vitamin D can be achieved with the sun or supplemented.

With taurine, we have data that shows in 12 strict vegan males who had maintained a vegan diet for 53 +/- 26 months and in 14 male nonvegetarian control subjects, plasma taurine levels differed. However, an objective standard to establish a deficiency for taurine does not exist, so there's no basis this would be negative for health.
[Rana 1986](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/taurine-concentrations-in-the-diet-plasma-urine-and-breast-milk-of-vegans-compared-with-omnivores/57EF109C7DCF9067E72C751B6F6D8874)
[Laidlaw 1988](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(23)16501-4/abstract)

Heme iron, even though not essential, can be achieved with impossible burgers.

EPA/DHA can be found in seaweed, algae, algae oil, and converted from ALA. The differences between circulating omega-3s, EPA, and DHA between vegans, vegetarians, and omnivores are mixed. Some find large differences, while some find it's similar. Though this study had low sample sizes of vegans and the measurement of blood omega-3 levels had an even lower vegan sample size. Even then, lower levels don't necessarily mean deficiency so there's no basis to say there would be worse health outcomes.
[Welch 2010](https://academic.oup.com/ajcn/article/92/5/1040/4597496)
[Sarter 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261561414000764)
One limitation of the Rana paper is that they centrifuged the blood samples for 1,500g for 15 minutes which leaves a small amount of platelets behind, which have high taurine levels intracellularly. It's likely not a lot as there's data that 20 minutes at 1,500g removes 95% of platelets in platelet rich plasma, but it's possible this limitation can shift the absolute taurine levels. 
[Chandler 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23249614/)
This limitation applies to both omnivore and vegan samples, but it does change the scale of the comparison so in order to meta analytically summate these two studies, we would need to use the standard mean differences rather than the mean difference. A meta analytic summation of these two studies barely misses statistical significance P=0.06

> [!note] My take
> If good data came out that vegans would be better off with higher levels of taurine, then I would recommend supplementation.

> [!question] Objections to prepare for
> - Lower taurine, carnosine or EPA/DHA levels might matter even without a defined deficiency cutoff.
> - Relying on supplements and fortified foods is a weakness where access or adherence is poor.
> - High dropout may show the diet is hard to sustain, whatever the stated reasons.
