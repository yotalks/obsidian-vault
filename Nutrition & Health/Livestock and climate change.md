> [!summary] Bottom line
> Livestock caused about a fifth of global warming up to 2010, and dominate agriculture's environmental impacts in Europe. Vegans' diets produce about 40% of the emissions of heavy meat eaters'. Grass-fed beef doesn't help: it has a larger carbon footprint than conventional beef. But following the US dietary guidelines would slightly raise emissions, because they recommend more of some resource-heavy foods.

## Why it matters

Without adaptation, deaths from heatwaves are projected to rise most in tropical and subtropical countries near the equator, with smaller percentage increases in Europe and the United States. Adaptation would shrink the increase, but deaths would still rise almost everywhere.
[Guo 2018](http://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1002629)

## Livestock's share

Direct non-CO2 emissions from livestock (mainly methane and nitrous oxide) caused about 19% of the 0.81 °C of human-caused warming by 2010. CO2 from clearing land for pasture added at least another 0.03 °C, bringing livestock's share to 23%. By 2100 their share could be anywhere from about 5% to 18%, depending on how much other sectors cut their emissions.
[Reisinger 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/m/pubmed/29105912/)

In Europe, livestock account for most of agriculture's environmental impact: 78% of its terrestrial biodiversity loss, 80% of its soil acidification and air pollution (ammonia and nitrogen oxides), 81% of its global warming, and 73% of its nitrogen and phosphorus water pollution. These are shares of agriculture's impact, which itself ranges from 12% of Europe's global warming to 59% of its nitrogen water pollution.
[Leip 2015](http://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/10/11/115004/meta)

## Diet

In the UK, the greenhouse gas emissions of a standard 2,000-calorie diet, in kg of CO2 equivalents per day, were:

| Diet | kg CO2e per day |
|---|---|
| High meat eaters (100 g+ a day) | 7.19 |
| Medium meat eaters (50–99 g) | 5.63 |
| Low meat eaters (under 50 g) | 4.67 |
| Fish eaters | 3.91 |
| Vegetarians | 3.81 |
| Vegans | 2.89 |

So heavy meat eaters' diets produce about twice the emissions of vegans'.
[Scarborough 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-014-1169-1)

Following the US dietary guidelines wouldn't necessarily lower emissions. Cutting calories to reach a normal weight, without changing the food mix, lowered energy use, blue water use and emissions by about 9%. But switching to the USDA's recommended food mix raised them: by 43%, 16% and 11% at current calorie intake, and by 38%, 10% and 6% with fewer calories. That's mainly because the guidelines recommend more fruits, vegetables, dairy and seafood, which use relatively more resources per calorie.
[Tom 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10669-015-9577-y)

## Biomass

Humans now weigh about 10 times as much as all wild mammals combined, and livestock, mostly cattle and pigs, outweigh wild mammals by even more.
[Bar-On 2018](http://www.pnas.org/content/115/25/6506)
