> [!summary] Bottom line
> Vegetarians have about 25% less ischemic heart disease and slightly less cancer. Their lower all-cause mortality is less consistent: it shows up in Adventists, especially men, and in UK vegetarians who stuck to their diet, but not in a meta-analysis of cohorts or in UK vegetarians overall. More plant protein in place of animal protein, especially processed meat, is linked to lower mortality. Major dietetics bodies consider well-planned vegan diets healthy at every life stage, with a reliable B12 source.

## All-cause mortality

In the Adventist Health Study 2 (73,308 Seventh-day Adventists, about 6 years of follow-up), vegetarians overall had 12% lower all-cause mortality than nonvegetarians (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.80–0.97). The links were stronger in men:

| Vs nonvegetarians | Men | Women | Both |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vegans | 0.72 (0.56–0.92) | 0.97 (0.78–1.20) | 0.85 (0.73–1.01) |
| All vegetarians | 0.82 (0.72–0.94) | 0.93 (0.82–1.05) | 0.88 (0.80–0.97) |

So vegan men had significantly lower mortality, but vegan women, and vegans overall, didn't differ significantly from nonvegetarians.
[Orlich 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4191896/)

In a pooled UK cohort (60,310 people, including EPIC-Oxford), all-cause mortality before age 90 didn't differ between vegetarians and regular meat eaters (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.94–1.10), and vegans didn't differ significantly either. Many of the meat eaters were friends and relatives of the vegetarians, and earlier EPIC-Oxford work found the cohort's overall death rate was only half that of the UK as a whole, so the meat eaters here aren't typical. The authors suggest UK vegetarians may also be motivated less by health than Adventist vegetarians are.

For deaths before age 75, vegetarians and vegans had lower mortality only after excluding people known to have switched diet group during follow-up (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.97). Without that exclusion there was no difference (0.97, 0.87–1.08).
[Appleby 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4691673/)

## Heart disease and cancer

A meta-analysis of 10 prospective cohorts found that vegetarians had:
- 25% lower incidence or mortality from ischemic heart disease ([[Glossary#RR|RR]] 0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.82)
- 8% lower total cancer incidence (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.87–0.98)

There was no significant difference in total cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, all-cause mortality, cancer mortality, or any specific cancer. Vegans had 15% lower total cancer incidence (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.75–0.95), but only a few studies looked at vegans, and that was their only significant result.

Cross-sectional studies in the same meta-analysis found vegetarians and vegans had lower BMI, total and [[Glossary#LDL|LDL]] cholesterol, and glucose.
[Dinu 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26853923/)

## Plant vs animal protein

In two large US cohorts (131,342 health professionals followed for up to 32 years):
- Animal protein wasn't linked to all-cause mortality, but was linked to slightly higher [[Glossary#CVD|cardiovascular]] mortality (HR 1.08 per 10% of calories).
- Plant protein was linked to lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.90 per 3% of calories) and cardiovascular mortality (0.88).

The links held only in people with at least one unhealthy lifestyle factor (smoking, heavy drinking, overweight or inactivity), not in those with none. Replacing 3% of calories from animal protein with plant protein was linked to lower mortality: by 34% when it replaced processed red meat, 12% for unprocessed red meat, and 19% for eggs.
[Song 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5048552/)

## Food groups

In a meta-analysis of prospective studies, each daily serving of these foods was linked to lower all-cause mortality:
- whole grains (RR 0.92)
- vegetables (0.96)
- fruits (0.94)
- nuts (0.76)
- fish (0.93)

Red meat (1.10) and processed meat (1.23) were linked to higher mortality. See [[Red meat]].
[Schwingshackl 2017](https://academic.oup.com/ajcn/article/105/6/1462/4569801)

## Dietetics position

The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics considers appropriately planned vegetarian and vegan diets healthful, nutritionally adequate and suitable for every stage of life, including pregnancy, infancy and for athletes. It credits their low saturated fat and high intake of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, soy, nuts and seeds for lower cholesterol and better blood glucose. Vegans need a reliable source of B12, such as fortified foods or supplements; see [[Vegan diets#Nutrients|Vegan diets]].
[Melina 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27886704/)
