> [!summary] Bottom line
> Per unit of food, organic farming has lower and less stable yields, equal or higher greenhouse gas emissions and energy use, and mixed effects on erosion. Gains in biodiversity and soil carbon don't offset the extra land needed.

## Soil erosion

Soil erosion is multivariable and some organic practices worsen it, some organic practices improve it. It's also land type (hilly vs flat land) and plant type variable depending on the various practices.

"Organic farming reduced soil loss for radish by 18% as a result of a high weed biomass density and cover at the end of the growing season. For potato, organic farming increased soil loss by 25% due to a reduced crop coverage, which is suspected to have been a consequence of crop–weed competition or increased herbivory associated with the absence of agricultural chemicals."
[Geoderma 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016706114000020)

"However, on hilly soils, erosion was found to be higher in OFSs than in CFSs."
[Agric Ecosyst Environ 2002](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167880902000919)

1) When the studies show improved soil erosion rates with organic, the percent difference in rate isn't that large.

2) When studies show reduced greenhouse gas emissions with organic, they commit at least 1 of the following fallacies:
- per land [[Glossary#GHG|GHG]] instead of per food GHG
- per food GHG instead of per type of food GHG
- Not accounting for the increased economic demand of cattle/dairy industry from manure and the increased methane that would cause when calculating CO2 equivalent emissions
→ [[Organic is not vegan]]

## Yields

Here we perform a meta-analysis to assess temporal yield stability of three major cropping systems: organic agriculture and conservation agriculture (no-tillage) vs. conventional agriculture, comparing 193 studies based on 2896 comparisons. Organic agriculture has, per unit yield, a significantly lower temporal stability (−15%) compared to conventional agriculture.
[Knapp 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-05956-1)

Organic farms promoted biotic abundance, biotic richness, soil carbon, and profitability, but conventional farms produced higher yields. Compared to conventional farms, organic farms had lower variability in abundance and richness but greater yield variability. 
[Smith 2019a](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2019.00082/full#T1)

## Emissions and land use

Direct GHG emissions are reduced with organic farming, but when increased overseas land use to compensate for shortfalls in domestic supply are factored in, net emissions are greater. Enhanced soil carbon sequestration could offset only a small part of the higher overseas emissions. 
[Smith 2019b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12622-7)

Contrary to dairy farming, energy use and GHG emissions in organic crop production are higher than in conventional crop production. Energy use in organic arable farming is 10-30% and in organic vegetable farming 40-50% higher than in their respective conventional counterparts. GHG emissions in organic arable and vegetable farming are 0-15% and 35-40% higher, respectively.
[NJAS 2013 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1573521413000705)

Organic farming is worse for the environment in multiple facets such as land use.
[Tuomisto 2012](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301479712004264)

"overall, organic yields are typically lower than conventional yields. But these yield differences are highly contextual, depending on system and site characteristics, and range from 5% lower organic yields (rain-fed legumes and perennials on weak-acidic to weak-alkaline soils), 13% lower yields (when best organic practices are used), to 34% lower yields (when the conventional and organic systems are most comparable)"
[Seufert 2012](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11069)

Per unit of KG food/calorie produced, organic farming DOES NOT reduce greenhouse gas emissions.
Per acre is a bad metric to compare because organic farming is inherently inefficient and uses more land in the first place. So it's greenhouse gas impact will be artificially deflated.
[Mondelaers 2009](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Joris_Aertsens/publication/235407512_A_meta-analysis_of_the_differences_in_environmental_impacts_between_organic_and_conventional_farming/links/00b49522e169faa120000000/A-meta-analysis-of-the-differences-in-environmental-impacts-between-organic-and-conventional-farming.pdf?origin=publication_detail)
