> [!summary] Bottom line
> Averaged across statin trials, death was postponed by only a few days, or about 1–1.5 days per year on a statin. That figure undersells statins. It only covers the trials' running time, often in comparison with controls who were also treated, and an average hides how the gain is distributed: in a model of a treatment like a statin, most people gain nothing while a minority gain years.

## Postponement of death

An analysis measured the area between all-cause survival curves in 11 statin trials with follow-up of 2.0–6.1 years. Death was postponed by −5 to 19 days in primary prevention trials and −10 to 27 days in secondary prevention trials, with medians of 3.2 and 4.1 days. The authors said withholding statins should be considered for people with limited life expectancy or side effects.
[Kristensen 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26408281/)

Dividing each trial's result by its length gives an average of about 1.5 days per year, or about 1.4 days when weighted by trial size. There's a lot of variation behind that, from −2.2 days per year in GISSI-HF to 4.7 in 4S.

## Why this undersells statins

**It stops when the trial stops.** The authors note it only estimates survival gain within the trials' running time, while real treatment often continues for much longer.

**Controls were often treated.** In ALLHAT-LLT, 32% of usual care participants with heart disease and 29% without it started lipid-lowering drugs, so LDL fell 28% with pravastatin but also 11% with usual care. That trial found no significant effect on mortality (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.89–1.11).
[ALLHAT 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12479764/)

**Averages hide the distribution.** Death isn't delayed by a few days for everyone. Modeling UK mortality data for men aged 50 at average risk, an intervention that cuts cardiovascular mortality by 30% gives a mean lifespan gain of 7 months. But 93% of these identical men gain nothing, and the other 7% gain a mean of 99 months.
[Finegold 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27042321/)

This is a model of a hypothetical intervention, not trial data, but it shows why a small average can still come from a large benefit for a few people. In a survey in the same paper, 33% of people preferred a 2% chance of gaining 10 years to a certain gain of 1 year, even though that's a fifth of the expected gain.

Any treatment that prevents events in a minority will look small when spread over everyone. The same 11 trials still showed fewer deaths overall (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.84–0.93).
[Kristensen 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26408281/)
