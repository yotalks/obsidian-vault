> [!summary] Bottom line
> In stable heart disease, routine revascularization doesn't lower deaths, but it does reduce spontaneous heart attacks and angina, at the cost of more heart attacks during the procedure. In trials from the stent era, total heart attacks were slightly lower (RR 0.89). Saying stents don't work also misses where most of them go: about 80% of PCIs in the US are for acute coronary syndromes, and in acute coronary syndromes invasive treatment does lower deaths.

## Stable heart disease

A meta-analysis of 14 randomized trials and 14,877 people compared routine revascularization with initial medical therapy, followed for a mean of 4.5 years.
[Bangalore 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32794407/)

| Outcome | RR (95% CI) |
| --- | --- |
| Death | 0.99 (0.90–1.09) |
| Nonprocedural heart attack | 0.76 (0.67–0.85) |
| Procedural heart attack | 2.48 (1.86–3.31) |
| All heart attacks | 0.93 (0.83–1.03) |
| Unstable angina | 0.64 (0.45–0.92) |
| Freedom from angina | 1.10 (1.05–1.15) |

There was no difference in heart failure or stroke. The evidence on death was strong enough to rule out a reduction of 10% or more.

Angina relief isn't just a placebo effect. In a trial of 301 people with stable angina on little or no antianginal medication, PCI gave a lower angina score than a placebo procedure at 12 weeks (2.9 vs 5.6, OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.41–3.47).
[Rajkumar 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38015442/)

Over longer follow-up of ISCHEMIA, the largest trial, there was still no difference in all-cause mortality, but an invasive strategy had fewer cardiovascular deaths and more non-cardiovascular deaths.
[Bolognese 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37091658/)

## Stent trials vs older trials

If the claim is about stents, the trials to pool are the ones that used stents, not ones that used balloon angioplasty alone, which is a procedure cardiologists barely perform anymore. Trials also shouldn't be picked or dropped without a reason, so this means separating every trial by whether it used stents, not choosing the ones with the preferred result.

Split that way, the all-trial result for total heart attacks hides a difference between eras:

| Trials | Number of trials | RR for heart attack (95% CI) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No stents | 6 | 1.42 (0.97–2.07) |
| Stents | 8 | 0.89 (0.80–0.998) |
| All trials | 14 | 0.93 (0.83–1.03) |

The test for interaction was P = 0.02, and fixed and random effects models gave the same subgroup results. The stent trials were BARI 2D, COURAGE, FAME-2, ISCHEMIA, ISCHEMIA-CKD, JSAP, MASS-2 and TIME.
[Bangalore 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32794407/)

So in stent trials, total heart attacks fell even when procedural heart attacks are counted, though only slightly and with a confidence interval that nearly reaches 1.

The stent group isn't stents alone. Trials were sorted by how much they used stents, and the revascularization groups in BARI 2D and MASS-2 include bypass surgery.
[Hueb 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20733102/)

## Where stents actually go

Most stents aren't placed for stable disease. In the US registry in 2017, 81.2% of PCIs were for acute coronary syndromes: 19.1% for STEMI, 25.3% for NSTEMI and 36.8% for unstable angina. Only 18.8% were for other reasons.

The share classed as elective was higher, at 33.8%, since not every acute coronary syndrome is treated urgently. Japan was the reverse, with 72.7% elective.
[Inohara 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32912447/)

## Acute coronary syndromes

A meta-analysis of 46 randomized trials and 37,757 people compared PCI on top of medical therapy in unstable and stable disease:

| Outcome | Unstable disease RR (95% CI) | Stable disease RR (95% CI) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Death | 0.84 (0.75–0.93) | 0.98 (0.87–1.11) |
| Cardiac death | 0.69 (0.53–0.90) | 0.89 (0.71–1.12) |
| Heart attack | 0.74 (0.62–0.90) | 0.96 (0.86–1.08) |

[Chacko 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32063040/)

In heart attacks with ST elevation, primary angioplasty beat thrombolytic drugs across 23 trials and 7,739 people. Short-term deaths were 7% vs 9%, and nonfatal reinfarction was 3% vs 7%. Stents were used in 12 of the trials.
[Keeley 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12517460/)

In non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndromes, early invasive therapy lowered deaths compared with a more conservative approach across 7 trials and 8,375 people, at 4.9% vs 6.5% over 2 years (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.63–0.90). Nonfatal heart attacks were 7.6% vs 9.1%.
[Bavry 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17010789/)

That benefit isn't the same in everyone. In long-term patient-level data from three trials, routine invasive treatment lowered cardiovascular death or heart attack in men (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.63–0.86) but not women (HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.89–1.43).
[Alfredsson 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25262262/)

It also didn't help people under 65 (HR 1.11, 95% CI 0.90–1.38), mainly because of more early heart attacks, with no difference between age groups in cardiovascular death.
[Damman 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21930723/)

## Left main disease

Left main disease has traditionally been an argument for bypass surgery, but in pooled data from 4 randomized trials and 4,393 people considered suitable for either, 5-year mortality didn't differ between PCI and bypass, with or without diabetes (HR 1.11 and 1.08). PCI had less early stroke, but more spontaneous heart attacks and repeat procedures. These patients had largely low to intermediate disease complexity.
[Gaba 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38465592/)
