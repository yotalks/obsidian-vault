> [!summary] Bottom line
> Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowers heart disease in cohorts, randomized trials (about 30% in trials, similar to statins) and Mendelian randomization. The risk rises in an S-shape around 25 g/day, which is why cohort meta-analyses comparing intakes above or below that range look null, and trials show the effect runs through total cholesterol. The trials that seemed to show harm from vegetable oils (Sydney, Minnesota) likely used trans-fat margarines, and Lyon's benefit can't be credited to its small cut in linoleic acid. The meta-analyses that found no link had problems of their own: adjusting for cholesterol, data extraction errors, or leaving out cardiovascular events. Even after you account for factors that could plausibly influence, most saturated fats have been shown to consistently increase the risk of morbidity or mortality, strongly suggesting that they directly contribute to worse health outcomes, particularly WRT heart disease and NAFLD.

There are many systematic reviews and meta analyses including RCTs or prospective cohort studies or both that show saturated fat intake is linked with negative health outcomes particularly with respect to morbidities like [[Glossary#CVD|CVD]] events, or mortality like CVD deaths.

## Effect on blood cholesterol

In a meta-analysis of 395 metabolic ward experiments, where everything participants ate was controlled, replacing 10% of calories from saturated fat with complex carbohydrates lowered LDL-C by 0.36 mmol/L (14 mg/dL). Replacing another 5% of calories from carbohydrate with polyunsaturated fat lowered it a further 0.11 mmol/L, while monounsaturated fat had no significant effect. In a typical British diet, replacing 60% of saturated fat with other fats and cutting dietary cholesterol by 60% would lower total cholesterol by about 0.8 mmol/L (10–15%), four fifths of it LDL.
[Clarke 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9006469/)
→ [[LDL and heart disease]]

A regression analysis of the combined experimental data found that saturated fat raises serum cholesterol and is its main dietary determinant, polyunsaturated fat actively lowers it, monounsaturated fat has no independent effect, and dietary cholesterol raises it. Changes in LDL-C roughly paralleled changes in total cholesterol, but changes in HDL-C couldn't be predicted well.
[Hegsted 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8503356/)

A WHO regression analysis of 84 controlled feeding trials (2,353 volunteers, diets fed for 13 to 91 days) found that each 1% of energy moved from saturated fat lowered LDL-C by 0.055 mmol/L when replaced with polyunsaturated fat, 0.042 mmol/L with monounsaturated fat and 0.033 mmol/L with carbohydrates. LDL-C predicted from each group's starting level and its diet agreed closely with the LDL-C actually measured, across 165 diets from 69 studies, so diet explains most of the differences in LDL-C between these diets.
![[WHO 2016 observed vs predicted LDL.png]]
[Mensink 2016 (WHO)](https://web.archive.org/web/20230410125402/https://apps.who.int/iris/bitstream/handle/10665/246104/9789241565349-eng.pdf)

## Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat

### Cohort studies

During a 4-10 year follow up, 5249 coronary events and 2155 coronary deaths occurred among 344,696 people. For a 5% lower energy intake from [[Glossary#SFA|SFAs]] and a concomitant higher energy intake from [[Glossary#PUFA|PUFAs]], there was a significant inverse association between PUFAs and risk of coronary events.
This was a pooled analysis of 11 American and European cohorts. Coronary events were 13% lower (HR 0.87, 0.77–0.97) and coronary deaths 26% lower (HR 0.74, 0.61–0.89). Replacing saturated fat with carbohydrates instead was linked to slightly more coronary events (HR 1.07, 1.01–1.14), and monounsaturated fat wasn't associated with CHD. It's large, with 4–10 years of follow-up and many events, but observational.
[Jakobsen 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19211817)

A 5% of energy increment in [[Glossary#LA|LA]] intake replacing energy from saturated fat intake was associated with a 9% lower risk of [[Glossary#CHD|CHD]] events.
That's 13 cohorts with 310,602 people and 12,479 CHD events, including 5,882 CHD deaths. The 5% swap also cut CHD deaths 13% (RR 0.87, 0.82–0.94), with CHD events at RR 0.91 (0.87–0.96). Comparing the highest with the lowest linoleic acid intakes, CHD events were 15% lower and CHD deaths 21% lower.
[Farvid 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4334131/)

Meta analysis of prospective cohort studies found a significant association between SFA intake and CHD mortality.
The meta-analysis pooled 29 cohorts with 1,164,029 people, and the association with CHD mortality was RR 1.10 (1.01–1.21). PUFA intake was linked to lower all-cause and stroke mortality, but not to CVD or CHD mortality.
[Mazidi 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261561420301461)

### Randomized trials

Cardiovascular mortality was reduced by 9% and cardiovascular events by 16%, which was attenuated in a sensitivity analysis that excluded a trial using fatty fish. Trials with at least two years' follow up provided stronger evidence of protection from cardiovascular events.
This covered 27 trials (30,902 person-years). Cardiovascular mortality was RR 0.91 (0.77–1.07) and cardiovascular events RR 0.84 (0.72–0.99), attenuated to 0.86 (0.72–1.03) without the oily fish trial. Trials of at least two years had RR 0.76 (0.65–0.90) for cardiovascular events. Total mortality barely changed (RR 0.98, 0.86–1.12).
[Hooper 2001](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC30550/)

Replacing saturated fats with mixed omega 6 and omega 3 fatty acid PUFA diets lowered the risk of non-fatal heart attack and death from CVD by 22%. The 13% increased risk when omega 6 fatty acids alone were substituted were not statistically significant.
The mixed omega-3 and omega-6 diets had RR 0.78 (0.65–0.93) for non-fatal heart attack plus CHD death, and the omega-6-only diets RR 1.13 (0.84–1.53). In all eight datasets, the PUFA replaced a mix of trans fat and saturated fat.
[Ramsden 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21118617)

Looking at over 13,614 participants with 1,042 CHD events, the average rated PUFA consumption was 5% energy in controls. The overall pooled risk reduction was 19% corresponding to a 10% reduced CHD risk for each 5% energy of increased PUFA, without evidence for statistical heterogeneity. Meta regression identified study duration as an independent determinant of risk reduction, with studies of longer duration showing greater benefits.
These were eight randomized trials. PUFA averaged 14.9% of energy in the intervention groups, and the overall result was RR 0.81 (0.70–0.95), or RR 0.90 (0.83–0.97) per 5% of energy. Mozaffarian reported speaking honoraria from Unilever, among others.
[Mozaffarian 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20351774/)

This updated review suggested that reducing saturated fat by reducing and/or modifying dietary fat reduced risk of CVD events by 14%
That's RR 0.86 (0.77–0.96) across 24 comparisons with 65,508 people, 7% of whom had a cardiovascular event. The benefit showed up in trials that modified fat rather than just cut it, trials of at least two years, and trials in men. Total and cardiovascular mortality didn't clearly change. As a Cochrane review, its protocol, search methods and outcomes were set out before the review began. The 2016 paper is a summary of the 2012 Cochrane review.
[Hooper 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/27224282)
[Hooper 2012 (Cochrane)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22592684/)

### Reviews of both

There was found to be convincing evidence that partial replacement of SFA with PUFA decreases risk of CVD, especially in men.
[Schwab 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4095759/)

RCTs that lowered intake of dietary SFA and replaced it with PUFA vegetable oil reduced CVD by around 30%, similar to the reductions achieved by statins.
This was an American Heart Association advisory. Replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrates and sugars didn't lower CVD.
[Sacks 2017](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510)

Replacement of SFA with unsaturated fat lowers CHD events and replacement with LA lowers CHD deaths as well.
This systematic review covered meta-analyses since 2010 plus newer cohorts and trials. Replacing saturated fat with carbohydrate didn't lower CHD events or CVD mortality.
[Clifton 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0939475317302375)

## Meta-analyses that found no link

### Siri-Tarino 2010

Serum cholesterol lies on the causal chain between saturated fat intake and CHD and CVD, so adjusting for it in a meta-analysis obscures the effect of saturated fat. Yet 7 of the 16 studies in the meta-analysis of CHD events, and 4 of the 8 in the meta-analysis of stroke, were adjusted for serum cholesterol, and these studies accounted for nearly half of all the events. That adjustment inevitably biases the estimates toward the null, and a meta-analysis of unadjusted data would have given different, more informative results.
[Scarborough 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20534750/)
[Siri-Tarino 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20071648/)

If serum cholesterol didn't cause heart disease, there would be even less reason to control for it, since it wouldn't be a confounder. Either way, the lower relative risks from cholesterol-adjusted studies aren't justified.

### Chowdhury 2014

This meta-analysis found no clear link between coronary disease and saturated fat (RR 1.02, 0.97–1.07) or omega-6 PUFA (RR 1.01, 0.96–1.07) in observational studies, and concluded the evidence didn't clearly support guidelines encouraging high PUFA and low saturated fat intake.
[Chowdhury 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24723079/)

Two of the six studies in its omega-6 analysis were wrong. The Nurses' Health Study and Kuopio Ischemic Heart Disease Study relative risks were retrieved incorrectly and listed as above 1.0. In the Nurses' Health Study's 20-year follow-up, the highest versus lowest quintile had RR 0.77 (0.62–0.95), and the authors seem to have used the RR for alpha-linolenic acid from a paper on sudden cardiac death. In the Kuopio study, the RR was 0.39 (0.21–0.71), and where the meta-analysis's number came from is unclear. It also left out data from two other studies and didn't mention the pooled cohort analysis that found PUFA replacing saturated fat lowered CHD.
[Willett 2014 (Harvard Nutrition Source)](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2014/03/19/dietary-fat-and-heart-disease-study-is-seriously-misleading/)

After a correction, the summary estimate for long-chain omega-3s from dietary intake changed from RR 0.93 (0.84–1.02), which wasn't significant, to RR 0.87 (0.78–0.97) from 16 studies with over 422,000 people, which is. The authors said the corrections didn't affect their main conclusions.
[Calder 2017](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/406397/1/Calder_Editorial_J_Pub_Health_Emerg_Key_words_added.pdf)

### Harcombe 2015

This meta-analysis of trials published before 1983, when the US (1977) and UK (1983) dietary fat guidelines were introduced, found no difference in all-cause mortality (RR 0.996, 0.865–1.147) or CHD mortality (RR 0.989, 0.784–1.247) across 6 trials of 2,467 men.
[Harcombe 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25685363/)

Its problems:
- It only included data published before 1983, leaving out everything since.
- It only looked at mortality, not cardiovascular events like heart attacks. Reanalyzing the pre-1983 trials in the Cochrane review showed 21% fewer cardiovascular events on a low saturated fat diet (RR 0.79, 0.69–0.91), a number needed to treat of about 14.
- It didn't appear to have a pre-defined, published protocol, so readers can't tell whether its two outcomes were the ones it set out to examine. Publishing protocols reduces outcome reporting bias, and Cochrane reviews are required to.
- Some of its data extraction differed from Cochrane's. For the Rose olive oil trial, it gave all-cause mortality as RR 3.00 versus 1.50 in the Cochrane review.
[Mahtani and Nunan 2016 (CEBM)](https://web.archive.org/web/20191113020141/https://www.cebm.net/2016/10/evidence-really-not-support-introduction-low-fat-dietary-guidance-1983/)

The review first stated the authors had no competing interests. The journal later published a correction noting that the corresponding author earns income from The Harcombe Diet Co. and Columbus Publishing, which sell diet books and dietary advice, with an editor's comment that these should have been declared from the start.
[Open Heart 2015 correction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4336863/)

The Harcombe Diet's first phase allows unlimited meat, fish and eggs, and vegetables except potatoes and mushrooms.
[The Harcombe Diet: What can I eat?](https://theharcombediet.com/2014/03/what-can-i-eat/)

### Pimpin 2016

This meta-analysis of butter found no significant association with CVD (RR 1.00, 0.98–1.02), CHD or stroke, and a small inverse association with diabetes. But each daily tablespoon (14 g) of butter was associated with slightly higher all-cause mortality (RR 1.01, 1.00–1.03, P = 0.045), across 9 country-specific cohorts. Mozaffarian reported consulting for the Haas Avocado Board and GOED, among others.
[Pimpin 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27355649/)

The analysis wasn't comparative. What butter replaces matters: if it replaces doughnuts or sugar-sweetened drinks, it could even look beneficial. That's why studies of replacing saturated fat with PUFA are the ones that answer the question.

## The threshold effect

It's often claimed that PUFAs increase risk for CVD and SFA may reduce risk.

Several meta-analyses have looked at this but come with interpretive challenges.
The relationship between SFA and CVD is nonlinear, and significantly influenced by degree of replacement with PUFA. So, the linear summation of prospective cohorts can hide the nonlinear effect. Like this study which looked at relationship between SFA and CVD in prospective cohorts. The aggregated results are null but the heterogeneity is 41% with no looking into why. 
![[Siri-Tarino 2010 stratified by saturated fat intake.png]]
After stratifying the included studies by intake range, we can see a statistically significant 30% increase in risk in subgroup one (around 25g/day) and null results for subgroup two and three (around 35g/day and 45g/day). Which is what's expected if the risk threshold presupposed by the Dietary Guidelines was correct. The McGee et al. study had to be removed because SFA intake ranges couldn't be determined from the provided data.
[Siri-Tarino 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20071648/)

We have many other meta analyses on this and applying the same methodology to all of the available meta analyses gives the same result. This time subgroup two representing the intake threshold presupposed by the Dietary Guidelines (around 25g/day). We see similar increases in risk in the same subgroup across all published meta analyses investigating the relationship between SFA and CVD. The reason why the other subgroups are null is simple, the ranges are not crossing the threshold of effect. The relationship between SFA and CVD is sigmoidal. Comparing intakes on the floor or ceiling of risk curve will usually produce null results, it's only middle intake range that'll make the increase in risk observable.
[Harcombe 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27697938/)
[Zhu 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30954077/)
[Mazidi 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32307197/)
[Skeaff 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19752542/)

## Randomized trials (Cochrane 2020)

This is the same relationship we see in RCTs. In this massive meta analysis and meta regression, their analysis showed substituting SFA for PUFA, and crossing the 10% of energy threshold of SFA, increases CVD risk, especially for CVD events, CVD mortality, CHD mortality, and non-fatal acute myocardial infarction.
![[Cochrane 2020 saturated fat cut-offs.png]]

They also performed multiple meta regression analyses on the data which is a tool used to investigate the relationship between two variables when they are controlled by a moderator variable, in this case, the two variables are SFA intakes (or PUFA-SFA substitution) and CVD. The only statistically significant moderator variable was total cholesterol. This suggests the chain of causality is SFA to total cholesterol to CVD. So this means the extent that a dietary intervention reduces total cholesterol, there should be reductions in CVD.
→ [[LDL and heart disease]]

They also did a meta analysis on SFA reductions lowering total CVD events and the best replacement being PUFA from seed oils and the effect is likely through lowering total cholesterol which perfectly aligns with the cohorts.
[Hooper 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32827219/)

## The Sydney and Minnesota trials

Two studies often brought up to show seed oils increase CVD are the Sydney Diet Heart Study and the Minnesota Coronary Experiment. They were designed such that SFA was to be replaced with PUFA in the form of seed oil based margarines and PUFA was to be replaced with SFA in the control groups.
Both trials achieved significant differences in total cholesterol, but the SDHS didn't achieve a significant difference in the magnitude of reduction and the final difference in total cholesterol was only 12mg/dL. While statistically significant, it's not clear that it is clinically significant. The other trial however did see significant reductions.
Neither study saw a statistically significant difference in CVD mortality or total mortality. But in the aggregate, there is a significant increase in CVD mortality between the two trials. 
[Woodhill 1978](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/727035/)
[Frantz 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2643423/)

These studies are often touted as the best designed trials to answer this question other than the LAVAT study. Though, the MCE design allowed participants to enter and exit the trial whenever and led to a mean follow up time of just 1.5 years. It should also be noted the SDHS was a secondary prevention trial, which means the subjects had already at least one CVD event at the time of enrollment, so it's questionable how meaningful the findings were.
[Dayton 1969](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.CIR.40.1S2.II-1)

It's been shown that SFA intakes don't always change CVD outcomes in high-risk populations.
[Laguzzi 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-86324-w)

## Trans fat in the margarines

The reason why we didn't see the effect in those trials that we see in nearly every other is likely due to the types of fat-based products they gave to the seed oil groups. They were primarily corn oil based margarines. Margarines are typically made from unsaturated fats, which are liquid at room temperature. In order to make it solid and spreadable, they need to be hardened. This is either done by hydrogenation or through emulsification. Non-hydrogenated margarines were not common during this time prior to the 70s though.
[SoyInfo Center: history of margarine](https://www.soyinfocenter.com/HSS/margarine2.php)

Both trials were done in the 60s and hydrogenation was the preferred method to harden these margarines, this has the secondary effect of creating trans fatty acids.

[[Glossary#TFA|TFAs]] are associated with an increased risk of many diseases, including CVD. TFAs are the only known fatty acid type that has been associated with increased CVD mortality when replacing SFA on a population level.
[Zhuang 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30636521/)

The corn oil based margarine used in SDHS was the brand Miracle.
[Ramsden 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23386268/)

Miracle brand margarine was up to 15% TFA when that study was done. That would mean they were eating about 5.7g/day of TFA.
[Australian Food News 2013](https://www.ausfoodnews.com.au/2013/02/11/heart-foundation-takes-swipe-at-butter-and-new-study-on-margarine.html)

The margarine used in the MCE trial was likely Fleischmann's Original, due to availability and popularity at the time. As well as the product being developed in direct response to the pro-PUFA research done at the time. The only two other margarines potentially available at the region then was Imperial and Mazola margarine. Some Imperial were still high in TFA until pretty recently. Mazola was the only non-hydrogenated margarine on the market at the time.
[ABC News: margarine history](https://abcnews.go.com/Business/story?id=8182555&page=1)

Given the options, there was about a 2/3 chance the margarine they were using was potentially confounded with TFA in the seed oil diet. Though these researchers remain skeptical that this confounding was likely.
[Ramsden 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27071971/)

Even if MCE wasn't confounded with TFA, they still had the ability to join and leave all willy nilly with a low follow up time which gives it way less power than the other trials on this.

## Ramsden's counterargument

The Ramsden study argued that the control group was likely more confounded with TFA than the seed oil group which would mean LA is worse than TFA. They claimed the vegetable shortening in the control group added to boost SFA content and shortening being hydrogenated would mean it must've had a lot of TFA.

This is doubtful because fully hydrogenated oils have very little TFA.
[Kadhum 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26048727/)

But partially hydrogenated oils like most soft margarines during this time had lots of TFA. A tablespoon of partially hydrogenated soybean oil margarine had 22x as much TFA as a tablespoon of fully hydrogenated soybean oil shortening.
![[Trans fat in soybean oil by hydrogenation.png]]
[USDA SR28 nutrient database](https://www.ars.usda.gov/northeast-area/beltsville-md-bhnrc/beltsville-human-nutrition-research-center/methods-and-application-of-food-composition-laboratory/mafcl-site-pages/sr11-sr28/)

Ramsden also acknowledge that the seed oil group's diet used soft margarines that likely had TFA, but they didn't say much about fully hydrogenated vegetable shortening containing pretty much the same amount of TFA as its unadulterated, non hydrogenated counterpart. This is why fully hydrogenated shortenings are still on the market despite the TFA ban in most developed countries.

Given we can infer that the SDHS study was confounded by TFA, and the MCE study was likely confounded by TFA and had short follow ups, the results make more sense. The effect sizes and direction of effect look like what we see in epidemiological research when TFA replaces SFA, risk rises. When PUFA replaces SFA, risk drops. 

## Lyon Diet Heart Study

The Lyon Diet Heart Study is noteworthy as it's the only experimental investigation of the diet-heart hypothesis that actually tried to reduce LA along with SFA. The trial saw one of the largest drops in CVD ever seen with a 73% drop in CVD risk over 4 years. 
[de Lorgeril 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7911176/)

The trial did achieve statistically significant differences in LA intake between groups, with 5.3% of energy as LA in the control group and only 3.6% of energy as LA in the intervention group. If we calculate absolute differences in LA for both groups using each groups total daily calories, we see the absolute differences in LA intake was about 4.5g/day, which is about 1.5 teaspoons of soybean oil which is nothing.
[de Lorgeril 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9989963/)

The groups achieved a difference of about 10g/day of SFA and the between group differences in SFA cross the 25g/day threshold. The intervention was consuming far fewer foods that are positively associated with CVD, like whole meat, processed meat, and butter. And the intervention group was consuming far more fruit and vegetables, as well as unsaturated fats, which are strongly inversely associated with CVD. So there's a lot in these diets that can strongly modify CVD risk and it's very unlikely that the 73% reduction in CVD was because of the 4.5g/day difference in LA and not seen anywhere else in the literature. Basically all other studies that were properly controlled found the opposite effect of increasing LA, and given that, their reductions were likely in spite of the reduced LA intake.

## Mendelian randomization

The relationship between LA and CVD in [[Glossary#MR|MR]] trials has been tested too. Mendelian randomization are basically between an RCT and epidemiological study. They find people with gene variants that influence a particular exposure (like higher tissue LA) and compare the outcomes to other people without those gene variants. The study methods of MR assume genes are randomly distributed in the population which should significantly mitigate the potential for certain types of confounding like healthy user bias. It's generally accepted this is a stronger form of evidence than epidemiology as it's giving you the best idea of magnitude of an effect, without confounding, without a lifelong RCT.

We don't even need MR data here as we have RCTs which are stronger forms of evidence, but data from the UK Biobank which is a huge prospective cohort with over 500,000 subjects found genetically elevated serum LA was inversely associated with acute myocardial infarction, where genetically elevated arachidonic acid was positively associated. The gene variants looked at were related to the function of fatty acid desaturase enzymes. It seems those less able to convert LA to [[Glossary#AA|AA]] have a reduced risk of [[Glossary#AMI|AMI]], and vice versa for those who can.
[Park 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33924952/)

Since AA conversion is tightly regulated, as well as dietary LA being almost unavoidable, it's unlikely that modulating dietary LA would be a reliable way to modulate the conversion of LA to AA. This supports increasing dietary LA for CVD prevention.
[Park 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33924952/)

## Verdict on heart disease

Overall, the evidence overwhelmingly supports the inclusion of seed oils especially when replacing SFA for reducing CVD risk and the evidence to shift this would need to be massive.
