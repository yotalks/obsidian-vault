> [!summary] Bottom line
> The lung cancer concern rests on an IARC review of case-control studies of cooking fumes, many in Chinese women, which didn't adjust for cooking fuel like wood and coal stoves, and in the best-adjusted study the risk only showed up in smokers. Pooling the studies that looked at the type of cooking oil gives a non-significant lower risk with higher PUFA oils, and prospective cohorts show no link between PUFA intake and lung cancer.

## The IARC case-control studies

The primary evidence used for the claim seed oils increase lung cancer risk is an IARC report from 2010. It includes 24 case-control studies and a lot of mechanistic speculation.
[IARC 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK385523/)

Firstly, going from a high PUFA to high MUFA oils in these case-control studies pretty consistently increases the relative risk for lung cancer which would contradict the hypothesis that MUFA would be better than PUFA for this endpoint.

### Cooking fuel and smoke

Boiling food was also associated with an increased risk of lung cancer. But this is because a lot of people in these studies were using things like wood and coal burning stoves.

It's possible that risk is just tracking cooking frequency, and people that cooked at home more were more likely to be exposed to wood smoke.

Some case-control studies didn't even report relative risks relevant to seed oils. Some only report relative risk for years spent cooking in a bedroom, and the type of cooking fuel used wasn't adjusted for. There are many more ways to produce smoke than heating seed oils in a pan, you can use butter!

Most of the remaining case-control studies report relative risks as a function of deep frying with no adjustment for anthropometrics or confounders like ventilation or use of wood/coal burning stoves. One study failed to report the confidence intervals for their relative risk and had no adjustments.

Here, they perform multiple sensitivity analyses that test for the effect of a few variables that may be confounding like windows, ventilation, SES, but the cooking fuel wasn't adjusted for.
![[Pasted image 20261030162827.png]]
[Ko 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10645816/)

A significant increase in risk was found only in those eating 2 or more meals a day cooked at home, which wood smoke would be confounding. Even if the association was tracking a real effect, there are no dietary guidelines in the world that encourage anyone to eat the majority of their meals fried in a pan.

### Smoking

This has the best adjustment model but they found no statistically significant increase in risk among non-smokers. It raises the possibility that in-home cooking may be a correlate for cigarette smoking.
![[Pasted image 20261030163020.png]]
[Seow 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11097230/)

Case control studies are also retrospective in nature and can't be used to assess temporality. This means they're badly equipped to form a causal inference. The associations are interesting, but not necessarily useful. And it's not known if the fumes are from seed oils, it could be cigarette smoking.

## Pooling the seed oil data

We can aggregate the data that was specific to cooking with seed oils and we find that cooking with higher PUFA oils results in a non-significant decrease in lung cancer risk. There's a good chance these case control studies are tracking some other exposure.

## Prospective cohort studies

We also have a meta analysis of prospective cohort studies looking at the relationship of PUFA and lung cancer and it showed a non-significant decrease in risk with higher intakes.
[Zhang 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24925369/)
→ [[Seed oils and cancer]]

Across 8 prospective cohorts with about 1.27 million people, high PUFA intake had little or no effect on lung cancer risk (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.78–1.06), and each extra 5 g a day of PUFA made no significant difference (RR 0.98, 0.96–1.01).

Many included risk ratios were specific to fish, so if we limit the risk ratios to just those that look at total PUFA, and not fish specifically, there's no association with lung cancer risk.
![[Pasted image 20261030163456.png]]

## Conclusion

The evidence, all in all, for seed oils increasing lung cancer risk is very poor quality and has high risk of confounding and higher quality evidence shows no significant effect of PUFA on lung cancer risk.
