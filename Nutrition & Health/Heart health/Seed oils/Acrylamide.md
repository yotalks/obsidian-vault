> [!summary] Bottom line
> Acrylamide forms from sugars and amino acids during high-heat cooking, not from the oil itself, though unsaturated oils produce more of it than saturated fats. Humans detoxify it faster than the rodents in which it causes cancer, meta-analyses find little or no link between dietary acrylamide and most cancers, and heated soybean oil is still linked to less heart disease than heated palm oil.

## Where acrylamide comes from

There are hypotheses that the passive peroxidation of LA in human tissues and hypotheses specific to certain byproducts produced from certain high heat cooking methods that often involve seed oils. Acrylamide is one of the compounds formed the Maillard reaction, which is responsible for the browning of food and occurs between temperatures of 120-140 C.

ACR is not formed from the oils themselves, rather the sugars and amino acids during heating, it's just that seed oils are a common medium of cooking and can uniquely retain higher levels of it after cooking.
[Daniali 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27374529/)

Heating asparagine at 180°C produced 2,447 ng/g of acrylamide in soybean oil and 1,442 ng/g in palm olein, versus 366 ng/g in lard and 211 ng/g in ghee, and the amount tracked how oxidized each oil was.

## Rodents vs humans

A food having a higher amount of a compound doesn't mean it's dangerous to eat the food, however. It's true ACR has shown to be carcinogenic in rodents, it's also the case rodents and humans digest and metabolize dietary ACR differently. The rate of excretion of ACR detoxification metabolites is much higher in humans than in rats, which suggests that the total area under the curve exposure to ACR and its toxic metabolites are also much lower in humans than in rodents. Because of this, it's likely that the carcinogenicity of ACR is also attenuated in humans.
[Fuhr 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16492914/)
[Gargas 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19166901/)

## Fried food and cancer

In this analysis, it's found that when comparing weekly intake of fried foods to zero intake, and found no statistically significant increase in cancer mortality after adjustment for a a bunch of confounders, including diet quality.
[Sun 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30674467/)

There's also an analysis of another prospective cohort study that showed an increased risk of gastric cancer with increasing fried food consumption from less than twice per week to greater than twice per week.
[Guo 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29429272/)

But looking back to the analysis of LAVAT trial, the intervention didn't see an increase in risk of digestive cancers despite the fact they had heated seed oils in their diet.
[Pearce 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4100347/)

## Dietary acrylamide and cancer

There's also a meta analysis that investigated the association between dietary ACR and eight different cancer subtypes. They only found a non-significant increase in risk for kidney cancer with increasing dietary ARC.
[Pelucchi 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25403648/)
→ [[Seed oils and cancer]]

Across 14 cancer sites, the summary risk ratios for high versus low intake ranged from 0.87 to 1.20, and the only borderline result was kidney cancer (RR 1.20, 95% CI 1.00–1.45).

There's also a later meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies that looked at this relationship in more depth and found no increase in renal cell carcinoma risk with more dietary ACR.
[Jiang 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077494/)

Across 8 studies, the risk ratio for the highest versus lowest intake was 1.12 (95% CI 0.97–1.28), and 1.11 (0.93–1.32) in never smokers.

This study also made multiple dose-response curves investigating the relationship between ACR and the risk of breast, endometrial, and ovarian cancer. They found no increased risk of any of the investigated cancers with increasing ACR, they also found an inverse association between ACR and breast cancer risk.
[Adani 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32169997/)

## Heated oils and heart disease

In this systematic review, the association between heated oils and a number of disease outcomes was thoroughly investigated. Their analysis found that even when highly unsaturated oils were used, there was a consistently lower risk of disease when using PUFA-rich cooking oils compared to SFA-rich oils.
[Sayon-Orea 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26148920/)

We also see heated SU lowers risk of CVD despite being high in ACR.
[Rastogi 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15051601/)

We also see when heated palm oil is primarily replacing heated SO, there's a statistically significant increase in risk of CVD, despite heated SO having up to 70% more ACR compared to PO.
[Kabagambe 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16251629/)

In this Costa Rican case-control study of 2,111 heart attack survivors, palm oil users had more heart attacks than soybean oil users (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.08–1.63).

## Fried foods, weight and diabetes

We also see inconsistent results with fried foods. They seem to increase risk of T2DM and obesity, but not CVD. While this may be expected as these foods are often hyperpalatable, and statistical adjustment for total energy intake may not be sufficient to fully capture potential confounding due to overconsumption. It may not be possible to explore this relationship robustly.
[Bedard 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15251058/)
[Andersen 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12571660/)

## Fried potatoes

To better look at the relationship of ACR and CVD, we can look at the relationship with fried potatoes. This is because they have some of the highest ACR levels of common foods. They're one of the largest sources of ACR in most countries.
[Bušová 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33338370/)
[JECFA 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16708866/)

There isn't data on chips and disease outcomes, but we do have data on french fries. This study of 2 prospective cohorts, it was observed that daily consumptions of either french fries or fried potatoes didn't increase risk of any CVD-related endpoint when compared to weekly consumption.
[Larsson 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27680993/)

In about 69,000 Swedish adults followed for 13 years, each extra 3 servings a week of potatoes (boiled, fried or French fries) didn't change the risk of major CVD events (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.97–1.02).

## Conclusion

ACR increasing risk of any particular disease is weak at best and often null. There is no robust evidence for the hypothesis that heated seed oils increase risk of cancer. There is evidence that despite a ton of dietary ACR, seed oils still are inversely associated with many diseases and if ACR is so dangerous, this isn't expected.
