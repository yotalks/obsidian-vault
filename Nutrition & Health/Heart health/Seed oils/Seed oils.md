> [!summary] Bottom line
> Human outcome evidence reviewed here does not support the main claims that seed oils cause chronic disease. Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated oils appears beneficial for heart health, while findings for other outcomes range from neutral to potentially beneficial. The linked outcome notes cover the studies, mechanisms and uncertainties.

## Mechanisms aren't outcomes

Negative attitudes to seed oils have gone crazy recently, despite this, almost all claims regarding negative health effects of seed oils are rooted in mechanistic or ecological research. Mechanistic studies includes studies like cell culture, animal studies, and in-vitro studies, or some short term human experiments. Ecological research just investigates largely unadjusted associations between population-level exposures and outcomes, such as the association between seed oil availability in the food supply and type 2 diabetes incidence.

These types of research can definitely be useful, but it's wrong to extrapolate from these types of research to population-level health effects. In the absence of corroborating human outcome data, it's very sus to make claims on what outcomes will happen based on speculation about intuitive mechanisms, especially when there's no high internal validity population-level outcome data that actually agrees with the mechanistic speculation to begin with.

Mechanistic studies tell us almost nothing about actual human disease risk.

## Heart disease outcomes

The outcome evidence (the saturated fat threshold, cohorts, the Cochrane trials, the Sydney and Minnesota trans-fat margarine trials, the Lyon Diet Heart Study and Mendelian randomization) is in [[Saturated fat#The threshold effect|Saturated fat]]. In short: replacing saturated fat with seed oils lowers heart disease risk.

## By outcome

- [[Seed oils and LDL oxidation]] — linoleic acid makes LDL slightly easier to oxidize, but antioxidants and diet matter far more, and oxidized LDL doesn't track risk once ApoB is counted
- [[Seed oils and cancer]] — the LAVAT excess was post-hoc and tracked smoking; trials, cohorts and MR are null or protective
- [[Seed oils and obesity]] — the 2-AG appetite mechanism doesn't hold in humans, and PUFA raises energy expenditure
- [[Seed oils and diabetes]] — higher linoleic acid is linked to better insulin sensitivity and lower diabetes risk
- [[Seed oils and fatty liver]] — the infant liver damage comes from soy phytosterols, and PUFA lowers liver fat compared with saturated fat
- [[Seed oils, arthritis and lupus]] — the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio's link to RA pain tracks low omega-3, and MR points to lower risk
- [[Seed oils and macular degeneration]] — the claim rests on ecological data; cohorts are mostly null and MR points to lower risk
- [[Seed oils and cognitive decline]] — no link to Alzheimer's, cognitive decline or dementia; vegetable fat is linked to lower dementia risk
- [[Seed oils and inflammation]] — high and low linoleic acid diets both leave CRP unchanged
- [[Seed oils and lipid peroxidation]] — PUFA-rich diets don't worsen peroxidation or DNA damage markers in trials

## Overall

Seed oils don't seem to be uniquely causal for chronic diseases we face and seem to have a beneficial role for many diseases, especially heart disease, T2DM, and maybe skin cancer. Seed oils could also have a beneficial role WRT macular degeneration, fatty liver disease, and some autoimmune diseases.

There is no evidence of harm with seed oils on any of these endpoints and seem to have no consistent effect on breast cancer, colorectal cancer, obesity, or cognitive decline. There are multiple mechanisms proposed for seed oils being harmful to humans, but the human outcome data do not support the importance of these mechanisms for these endpoints. If the outcomes consistently contradict the mechanisms we speculate with, the mechanisms are likely not interacting in the ways we think.

Seed oils can be a health promoting addition to the diet and seem to have a lot of health benefits with little to no health risks to the general population. Though, they are in many foods that aren't healthy, so it would be a good idea to include them in a healthy way that don't lead to an overconsumption of calories, like in salad dressings or with cooking oils for sauteing vegetables.
