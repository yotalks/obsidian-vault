> [!summary] Bottom line
> Dietary cholesterol raises blood cholesterol along a curve that flattens out: going from 0 to 300 mg/day raises LDL-C by about 10 mg/dL, while 300 to 500 mg/day barely changes it. Because LDL causes heart disease, dietary cholesterol still raises risk, even though studies of typical intakes struggle to show it. LDL causally associates with heart disease in a linear and proportional manner and in all cases of exposures that durably raise LDL over time, it'll produce a likewise increase in heart disease risk. Dietary cholesterol is one exposure.

## A dose response that flattens out

[[Glossary#DC|DC]] intakes that go from average, around 300mg/day, to 500mg/day, would have a negligible effect on total cholesterol levels because its effects exist on a hyperbolic curve.
![[Dietary cholesterol hyperbolic dose response.png]]
[Hopkins 1992](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/1534437)

So, when we study the average population, we are of course not going to notice much on effects on [[Glossary#CVD|CVD]] endpoints.
[Berger 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26109578)

This graph as you can see, again, show no significant difference going from around 300mg/day to 500mg/day in [[Glossary#LDL|LDL-C]]. But going from 0mg/day to 300mg/day increases LDL-C by almost 10mg/dL which is meaningful. DC also does basically nothing to [[Glossary#HDL|HDL-C]].
![[Dietary cholesterol LDL-C meta-regression.png]]
[Vincent 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30596814)

## Egg trials

In the same metabolic ward meta-analysis as above, avoiding 200 mg a day of dietary cholesterol lowered LDL-C by 0.10 mmol/L (4 mg/dL).
[Clarke 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9006469/)

In 49 healthy adults (average LDL-C 125 mg/dL), 2 eggs a day for 6 weeks didn't change total or LDL cholesterol or blood vessel function, while oats lowered LDL-C to 117 mg/dL.
[Katz 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15721501/)

In another trial, 2 eggs a day with meals for 3 weeks raised plasma cholesterol 11% through higher LDL-C and lowered HDL-C 11%. It also made plasma and LDL 34–42% more prone to oxidation.
[Levy 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9001684/)

## Why it still raises heart disease risk

Since LDL causally associates with heart disease in a linear and proportional manner and all exposures that durably increase LDL over time produce likewise increases in heart disease risk and dietary cholesterol is one of those exposures, it seems that dietary cholesterol increases risk for heart disease.
→ [[LDL and heart disease]]

## Why the studies disagreed

Earlier analyses often didn't account for how much cholesterol people already ate. Hopkins's model included both baseline and added dietary cholesterol, so a trial's result depends on where its subjects started. If they already eat a lot, adding more does little and you'd need huge power to see it.
[Hopkins 1992](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/1534437)

The Vincent meta-regression covered 55 randomized studies with 2,652 subjects and intakes from 0 to 1,500 mg/day. Each extra 100 mg/day predicted 1.90 mg/dL more LDL-C on the linear model and about 4.5 mg/dL on the nonlinear models, which fit best.
[Vincent 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30596814)

Why the curve flattens is still debated. It might depend on baseline dietary cholesterol, since that could affect the transporters that absorb it, or on blood cholesterol, or on something else. All of those positions agree that dietary cholesterol raises blood cholesterol.

## Cutting back

The curve works in both directions. Going from 500 mg/day down to 250 probably won't change much, and any drop may come from cutting the saturated fat that comes with cholesterol-rich foods. Getting most of the benefit means going close to zero.

At around 4.5 mg/dL per 100 mg/day, someone eating the US average of about 300 mg/day could expect roughly 13 mg/dL lower LDL-C at zero. That's a rough estimate, but it's the same ballpark as the graph above. It's less important than saturated fat, but not nothing.

## The LDL to HDL ratio

Some analyses point out that HDL rises along with LDL, so the ratio barely moves. That doesn't cancel the harm, because the two aren't symmetric. Raising LDL causes heart disease, while raising HDL isn't protective on its own, so a higher LDL with the same ratio is still worse. → [[HDL]]

## Low-risk people

A healthy lifestyle lowers your baseline risk, but it doesn't make LDL stop mattering. In a meta-analysis of 27 statin trials, lowering LDL by 1 mmol/L cut major vascular events by a similar proportion at every risk level, including those with under 5% risk over 5 years (RR 0.62) and 5–10% risk (RR 0.69). For people under 10% risk, the absolute benefit was about 11 fewer events per 1,000 over 5 years.
[CTT 2012](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1649027)

Those groups were stratified by risk, not age, so they aren't people in their 20s. The fair position is that the absolute risk from an egg can be small for a healthy person, not that it's zero. "I don't need to avoid this to be healthy" is different from "this has no effect."

How much that matters depends on the person. Someone young with low LDL may reasonably decide it's not worth caring about. Someone with a strong family history of early heart attacks has a higher baseline risk, so the same change is worth more. This is also a reason to get a lipid panel rather than go by how healthy you seem. And being anxious over every food has costs of its own, so it's fine to accept a small risk as long as you don't pretend it isn't there.

## Guidelines

The 2015 advisory report said cholesterol wasn't "a nutrient of concern for overconsumption," and the 2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines dropped the 300 mg limit. But the guidelines also said people should eat as little dietary cholesterol as possible while following a healthy eating pattern, so it was the number that went away.
[AHA News: Q&A on dietary cholesterol](https://news.heart.org/qa-federal-nutrition-panels-advice-dietary-cholesterol/)
[Key excerpts from the 2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines](https://foodpolitics.com/wp-content/uploads/CSPIs-DGA-keyexcerpts.pdf)

The AHA's 2019 advisory set no numeric target and favored healthy dietary patterns overall.
[Carson 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31838890/)

The AHA has also suggested one egg a day for people who eat them as part of a healthy diet. That says an egg can fit in a heart-healthy diet. It isn't advice to eat one, and it doesn't mean removing it wouldn't lower risk.
