> [!summary] Bottom line
> Very low LDL can shrink coronary plaque a little: in the ASTEROID trial, high-dose rosuvastatin cut average narrowing from 37.3% to 36.0% over 2 years. But most of that is small. Using prespecified thresholds for a clinically relevant change (at least 10% in diameter stenosis or 0.2 mm in lumen diameter), most patients stayed stable, and only about 8–12% regressed. Angiography also understates plaque size, because the plaques that rupture and cause heart attacks tend to be large even when they don't narrow the artery much.

## Measuring regression on angiograms

Quantitative coronary angiography ([[Glossary#QCA|QCA]]) measures two things at each narrowing:
- **Percent diameter stenosis:** how much narrower the artery is at the lesion than at a nearby normal reference segment.
- **Minimum lumen diameter ([[Glossary#MLD|MLD]]):** the width of the open channel at the narrowest point.

Clinically relevant regression or progression was defined as a nominal change (from baseline to 2 years) of ≥10% for percent diameter stenosis and ≥0.2 mm (prespecified) for MLD. Smaller changes count as no meaningful change, even when they're statistically significant on average.

## ASTEROID

ASTEROID treated 507 people with coronary disease with rosuvastatin 40 mg a day for 2 years, with no control group. LDL fell 53% to an average of 61 mg/dL, and HDL rose 14%. Of these, 292 people with 613 narrowings over 25% had angiograms at both time points.
- **Average stenosis** fell from 37.3% to 36.0%, and average MLD rose from 1.65 to 1.68 mm (both P < 0.001).
- **Stenosis by the clinically relevant threshold:** 7.5% of patients regressed by at least 10 points, 89.4% changed by less than that, and 3.1% progressed.
- **MLD by the clinically relevant threshold:** 12.1% regressed by at least 0.2 mm, 81.9% changed by less, and 6.0% progressed.
→ [[LDL and heart disease]]

So more patients regressed than progressed, but most were stable. It was the first major statin-only trial to show angiographic regression rather than just slower progression. Across angiography trials, less progression tracked lower on-treatment LDL, and ASTEROID, with the lowest LDL, was the only one below zero.

ASTEROID was run by AstraZeneca, which makes rosuvastatin, and two of the authors worked for the company.
[Ballantyne 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18378607/)
[ASTEROID QCA slides (ACC 2008)](https://www.sisalombardia.it/sisa_novitaletteratura_200804/acc2008/asteroid-Ballantyne.pdf)

## Angiograms understate plaque size

Angiography only shows the lumen, not the artery wall. How narrow the lumen gets depends on both how much plaque there is and how much the artery remodels around it, which angiograms assess poorly. So a large plaque can cause only a mild narrowing on an angiogram. Fishbein and Siegel reviewed how big the plaques that rupture actually are.
[Fishbein 1996](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/01.cir.94.10.2662)

In autopsies of 162 people who died of heart attacks, plaque at the site of rupture with a clot narrowed the artery's cross-sectional area by 91% on average (range 67% to 99%). Fatal plaque ruptures happened at sites of severe narrowing by preexisting plaque.
[Qiao 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2007714/)

Heart attacks often happen at sites that look mild to moderate on angiograms. In a postmortem study of 36 people with fatal heart attacks, the culprit plaques were about twice as large as plaques with the same degree of narrowing elsewhere in the same person's arteries (plaque area 9.6 vs 4.7 mm²). Culprit plaques were also more often positively remodeled (70% vs 31%).
[Bezerra 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11600336/)
