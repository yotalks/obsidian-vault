> [!summary] Bottom line
> The evidence that collagen supplements improve skin aging is very weak. The skin trials compare collagen with protein-free placebos, so any effect can't be separated from simply eating more protein, and the meta-analyses that found benefits contained serious errors. Once they're corrected, there's no evidence of benefit. Large burns are the exception, where a collagen-based supplement sped up wound healing against an isocaloric control.

## Skin aging

### No protein-matched controls

In the randomized trials of collagen for skin, the placebos are non-protein substances like maltodextrin, lactose or water with preservatives and flavorings. Collagen supplements add protein, typically 2.5–10 g a dose in skin studies, and no trial reports equating total protein intake between groups. So these trials can't show that collagen does anything an extra serving of any other protein wouldn't. Most proteins contain meaningful amounts of the same amino acids collagen does.
[Bitterman 2025](https://x.com/AviBittMD/status/1952532151309648128)

Protein-matched designs aren't impossible. Trials of collagen for muscle protein synthesis and joint health have used isonitrogenous controls such as whey, but none exist for skin outcomes.
[Bitterman 2025](https://x.com/AviBittMD/status/1952533487006695885)

### The meta-analyses

A re-analysis of the two main meta-analyses of oral collagen for skin aging found inaccurate reporting of data from the primary studies, and combinations of measures likely to produce spurious results. A meta-regression accounting for differences in dose across the trials found no evidence of benefit.
[Grimes 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43047302/)

The errors included:
- Standard errors treated as standard deviations, which gives a study too much weight
- Incorrect use of percentage difference
- Missing studies, and data left out of studies that were included
- Studies without a placebo control
- A hydration result based on a survey question rather than any objective measurement
- Inconsistent effect sizes and incorrect numbers of subjects

The underlying trials were mostly small and short, typically 8–12 weeks. Many didn't track other skincare, procedures or usual protein intake, some gave little detail about the placebo, and few defined how large a change would matter to patients. Elasticity and hydration readings are short-term proxies, so a change in them doesn't establish a visible or lasting effect on skin aging.
[Bitterman 2026](https://x.com/AviBittMD/status/2106272650377023587)

## Burns

In 31 men aged 18–60 with burns over 20–30% of their body surface, a hydrolyzed collagen-based supplement for 4 weeks was compared with an isocaloric placebo:
- **Wound healing.** The rate of wound healing was 3.7 times higher with collagen (hazard ratio 3.7, 95% CI 1.4–9.5).
- **Pre-albumin.** Serum pre-albumin, a marker of nutritional status, was higher at week 2 (29.7 vs 17.8 mg/dL) and week 4 (35.1 vs 28.3 mg/dL).
- **Hospital stay.** Stays were shorter, 9.4 vs 13.5 days, but not significantly (p = 0.063).
- **Intake.** Body weight and dietary energy and protein intake didn't differ between groups.

[Bagheri Miyab 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31859087/)

A large burn plausibly creates an enormous demand for new collagen during the proliferative phase of healing, which may be why collagen helps here but not for everyday skin.
[Bitterman 2026](https://x.com/AviBittMD/status/2110572557866864649)
