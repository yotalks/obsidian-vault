> [!summary] Bottom line
> The trials reviewed here found strong protection against symptomatic and severe COVID-19, with efficacy varying by vaccine and follow-up period. Side effects were mostly short-lived, and serious adverse events were similar between vaccine and control groups. Detailed results and follow-up findings are organized by vaccine below.

## Pfizer–BioNTech (BNT162b2)

### Choosing the candidate

The phase 1/2/3 protocol tested two candidates built on the same modified mRNA platform: BNT162b1, which encodes the spike protein's receptor-binding domain, and BNT162b2, which encodes the full-length spike, stabilized in its prefusion shape.
[Protocol C4591001](https://cdn.pfizer.com/pfizercom/2020-11/C4591001_Clinical_Protocol_Nov2020.pdf)

In the US phase 1 trial, 195 adults aged 18–55 or 65–85 were split into 13 groups of 15, with 12 getting vaccine and 3 placebo in each group. Both candidates produced similar neutralizing antibody levels, as high as or higher than in people who had recovered from COVID-19. BNT162b2 caused fewer and milder systemic reactions, especially in older adults. After the second 30 µg dose in 18–55-year-olds, fever occurred in 75% with BNT162b1 but 17% with BNT162b2. So BNT162b2 at 30 µg went forward to phase 2/3. Pain at the injection site was the most common local reaction with both.
[Walsh 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33053279/)

### How success was defined

Efficacy was measured as 100 × (1 − the ratio of COVID-19 rates in the vaccine and placebo groups). The protocol planned 164 cases, enough for 90% power if the true efficacy was 60%. Success meant at least a 98.6% probability that efficacy was above 30%, under a pessimistic prior centred on 30% efficacy. Interim analyses after 32, 62, 92 and 120 cases needed a 99.5% probability to stop early for efficacy.
[Protocol C4591001](https://cdn.pfizer.com/pfizercom/2020-11/C4591001_Clinical_Protocol_Nov2020.pdf)

### Phase 3 results

43,548 people aged 16 and over were randomized, and 43,448 got two doses 21 days apart of vaccine (21,720) or placebo (21,728). From 7 days after the second dose, there were 8 COVID-19 cases with vaccine and 162 with placebo: 95% efficacy (95% credible interval 90.3–97.6). The vaccine group's risk was about one twentieth of the placebo group's. Efficacy was 90–100% across age, sex, race, ethnicity, body weight and coexisting conditions. Of 10 severe cases after the first dose, 9 were in the placebo group.
[Polack 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33301246/)
→ [[Vaccine safety]]

Protection started before the second dose. The two groups' case curves split about 12 days after the first dose, and efficacy between the first and second doses was 52%.

**Side effects** in the 7 days after the second dose, vaccine vs placebo:

| | 16–55 years | Over 55 |
|---|---|---|
| Injection-site pain | 78% vs 12% | 66% vs 8% |
| Fatigue | 59% vs 23% | 51% vs 17% |
| Headache | 52% vs 24% | 39% vs 14% |
| Muscle pain | 37% vs 8% | 29% vs 5% |
| Chills | 35% vs 4% | 23% vs 3% |
| Fever | 16% vs 0% | 11% vs 0% |

They were mostly mild or moderate and short-lived, and less common in older adults. Serious adverse events were rare and similar in both groups. Two vaccine recipients died (arteriosclerosis, cardiac arrest) and four placebo recipients died (two from unknown causes, a hemorrhagic stroke and a heart attack). None of the deaths was considered related to the vaccine or placebo.

### Six-month follow-up

Through 6 months, efficacy was 91.3% (95% CI 89.0–93.2), declining gradually, and 96.7% against severe disease. It was 86–100% across countries and groups. In South Africa, where the beta variant dominated, it was 100% (95% CI 53.5–100). Few participants withdrew because of adverse events.
[Thomas 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34525277/)

All three Pfizer papers were funded by BioNTech and Pfizer.

## Moderna (mRNA-1273)

30,415 US adults at high risk of COVID-19 or its complications were randomized to two 100 µg doses 28 days apart or placebo. At the end of the blinded phase, median follow-up was 5.3 months. Efficacy was 93.2% (95% CI 91.0–94.8), with 55 cases on vaccine and 744 on placebo. Against severe disease it was 98.2% (2 vs 106 cases), and against asymptomatic infection 63.0%. Efficacy was consistent across ethnic, racial and age groups and in people with coexisting conditions.
[El Sahly 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34551225/)

Unsolicited adverse events over the whole blinded phase were similar with vaccine and placebo:

| | mRNA-1273 (n = 15,184) | Placebo (n = 15,162) |
|---|---|---|
| Any | 41.6% | 43.0% |
| Serious | 1.8% | 1.9% |
| Fatal | 17 | 16 |
| Severe | 3.0% | 3.2% |
| Serious and judged related to vaccination | 12 | 4 |
| Severe and judged related to vaccination | 0.6% | 0.2% |

No deaths were judged related to vaccination. The trial was funded by the US government (BARDA and NIAID).
[El Sahly 2021 supplementary appendix](https://www.nejm.org/doi/suppl/10.1056/NEJMoa2113017/suppl_file/nejmoa2113017_appendix.pdf)

## Oxford–AstraZeneca (ChAdOx1 nCoV-19)

ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 is a viral vector vaccine, not an mRNA one. It uses a harmless chimpanzee adenovirus to carry the spike gene. Four blinded randomized trials in the UK, Brazil and South Africa compared it with a meningococcal vaccine (MenACWY) or saline.

In the pooled interim analysis (11,636 participants), efficacy from 14 days after the second dose was 70.4% (30 cases among 5,807 vaccinated vs 101 among 5,829 controls). It was 62.1% with two standard doses and 90.0% in a UK subgroup whose first dose was accidentally halved, though that subgroup was small (3 vs 30 cases). From 21 days after the first dose, all 10 COVID-19 hospitalizations, including 2 severe cases and 1 death, were in the control group. Serious adverse events were similar (84 events with vaccine, 91 with control).
[Voysey 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33306989/)

Cumulative COVID-19 cases separated between the groups soon after the exclusion period (the first 14 days after the second dose, or 21 days after the first standard dose) and kept diverging. By the end of follow-up, about 1% of the vaccine group had symptomatic COVID-19 after the second dose, versus about 3.5% of controls, and about 1.5% versus 4.5% from the first standard dose.
![[Voysey 2021 cumulative COVID-19 cases.png]]

In the later primary analysis (17,178 participants), efficacy was 66.7% (95% CI 57.4–74.0), with 84 vs 248 cases and no hospitalizations in the vaccine group after the first 3 weeks, versus 15 in the control group. A single standard dose gave 76.0% efficacy from day 22 to day 90. Two doses gave more protection with a longer gap between them: 81.3% at 12 weeks or more, versus 55.1% at under 6 weeks. Serious adverse events occurred in 0.9% of the vaccine group and 1.1% of controls.
[Voysey 2021b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33617777/)

In the US phase 3 trial (32,451 adults in the US, Chile and Peru, randomized 2:1 to vaccine or placebo), efficacy against symptomatic COVID-19 from 15 days after the second dose was 74.0% (95% CI 65.3–80.5), and 83.5% (54.2–94.1) in people 65 and older. Among fully vaccinated participants there were no severe or critical cases with the vaccine, versus 8 with placebo. Efficacy against any infection, measured by antibody seroconversion, was 64.3%. Serious and medically attended adverse events were similar to placebo. The trial was funded by AstraZeneca and others.
[Falsey 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34587382/)

The trials were funded by UK Research and Innovation, the NIHR, CEPI, the Gates Foundation, other foundations, and AstraZeneca.

## Johnson & Johnson and Delta-era findings

The single-dose Johnson & Johnson (Janssen) vaccine prevented about 56% of moderate to severe COVID-19 and 73% of severe disease. Against the Delta variant, protection against infection fell, more for Pfizer than Moderna, while protection against severe disease stayed high.

### Janssen booster (FDA review)

The Janssen vaccine (Ad26.COV2.S) uses a replication-incompetent adenovirus type 26 to carry the gene for a stabilized spike protein, and was authorized in the US as a single dose in February 2021. In October 2021, Janssen asked the FDA to authorize a booster dose. FDA reviewers mostly couldn't check the analyses themselves, because the datasets arrived too late for independent review.

**Single dose.** In the final analysis of the placebo-controlled trial of about 40,000 adults (COV3001), efficacy from 14 days after the dose was:
- 56.3% (95% CI 51.3–60.8) against moderate to severe/critical COVID-19
- 73.3% (63.9–80.5) against severe/critical COVID-19

Protection against moderate to severe disease fell with time, from 72% at 15–28 days to 42.2% from day 113 onward, while protection against severe disease didn't wane. The fall may be partly due to vaccine-resistant variants in countries outside the US. In the US, efficacy was 72.9%. A real-world study of US use (COV4002) gave similar estimates, with no sign of waning during the Delta wave. Both studies found lower effectiveness than for the two-dose mRNA vaccines.

**Two doses 56 days apart.** In a trial of about 30,000 adults (COV3009), with a median of 36 days of follow-up after dose 2, efficacy was 75.2% (54.6–87.3) against moderate to severe/critical COVID-19, 93.7% (58.4–99.9) in the US, and 100% (32.6–100) against severe/critical disease, though that was based on only 8 cases in the placebo group.

**Antibodies.** A second dose 2–3 months after the first raised neutralizing antibodies about 1.5- to 4-fold, with a 3-month gap looking better than 2 months. A booster at 6 months raised them 10.5-fold against the original strain and 3-fold against Delta by 28 days, but this rested on only 15–17 samples and an unvalidated assay.

**Booster safety.** Across 8,049 people given a second or booster dose, side effects weren't noticeably different from the first dose. Follow-up was short, only 17 people got a booster at 6 months, and the database was too small to pick up rare serious adverse events.

**Post-authorization surveillance.** By October 2021, more than 15 million US doses had been given. VAERS reports, which are passive and voluntary, identified:
- **Thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS):** blood clots, including in the brain's venous sinuses, with low platelets. US use was paused for 10 days in April 2021, and 47 cases had been confirmed.
- **Guillain-Barré syndrome:** about 4.2 times the expected rate, or 6.4 extra cases per 100,000 person-years.
- **Under evaluation:** blood clots with normal platelets (which the European Medicines Agency judged possibly linked), myocarditis and pericarditis, and immune thrombocytopenia.

Both TTS and Guillain-Barré syndrome were added as warnings to the vaccine's fact sheets → [[Vaccine safety]].
[FDA 2021: Janssen booster briefing document](https://www.fda.gov/media/153037/download)

### Health care workers in Israel

In 6,710 health care workers at a Tel Aviv hospital, followed for a median of 63 days with regular PCR screening, symptomatic infection occurred in 8 fully vaccinated workers and 38 unvaccinated ones (4.7 vs 149.8 per 100,000 person-days, adjusted IRR 0.03). Asymptomatic infection was also much less common (11.3 vs 67.0 per 100,000 person-days). Unlike trials, which stop once enough cases have occurred, real-world cohorts like this follow people as the epidemic runs its course, which gives a better sense of the absolute risk reduction. Vaccinated workers were tested less often than unvaccinated ones in some weeks, though a propensity-matched comparison was also run. One author reported Pfizer research grants and another Pfizer consulting fees, outside this work.
![[Angel 2021 health care worker testing.png]]
[Angel 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33956048/)

### Pfizer vs Moderna against Delta

In Qatar, a matched test-negative case-control study compared people testing positive and negative for the Delta variant. From 14 days after the second dose, effectiveness was:

| | Pfizer (BNT162b2) | Moderna (mRNA-1273) |
|---|---|---|
| Any Delta infection | 51.9% (95% CI 47.0–56.4) | 73.1% (67.5–77.8) |
| Severe, critical or fatal disease | 93.4% (85.4–97.0) | 96.1% (71.6–99.5) |

After just the first dose, effectiveness against infection was 45.3% for Pfizer and 73.7% for Moderna. Half the people fully vaccinated with Pfizer had their second dose before 11 May 2021, so some of Pfizer's lower protection may reflect waning over time.
[Tang 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34728831/)

Against asymptomatic Delta infection (people testing positive with no symptoms, for example in surveys, before travel or on arrival in Qatar), effectiveness was 35.9% (11.1–53.9) for Pfizer and 80.2% (54.2–92.6) for Moderna.
[Tang 2021 preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.08.11.21261885v1.full)

In the Mayo Clinic Health System, vaccinated and unvaccinated people in Minnesota were matched on age, sex, race, previous PCR testing and date of full vaccination (25,589 in each group). From January to July 2021:
- Against infection, Moderna was 86% effective (95% CI 81–90.6) and Pfizer 76% (69–81).
- Against hospitalization, Moderna was 91.6% (81–97) and Pfizer 85% (73–93).
- In July, when Delta rose from under 1% to over 70% of Minnesota cases, effectiveness against infection fell to 76% for Moderna and 42% for Pfizer, while protection against hospitalization stayed high (81% and 75%, with wide confidence intervals).

Across Mayo sites in several states, people fully vaccinated with Moderna had about half the rate of breakthrough infection of those vaccinated with Pfizer (IRR 0.50, 95% CI 0.39–0.64), and about 60% less in Florida in July (IRR 0.39). This was an observational preprint.
[Puranik 2021](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.08.06.21261707v2.full)
