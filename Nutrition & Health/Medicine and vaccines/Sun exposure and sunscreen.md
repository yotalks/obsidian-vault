> [!summary] Bottom line
> Sun protection is one of the highest-yield things you can do for your skin. In a randomized trial, daily sunscreen lowered squamous cell carcinoma and slowed skin aging, and may have lowered melanoma. Daily sunscreen didn't change mortality over 21 years, so any health benefits of sunlight probably don't depend on UV reaching the skin, and sunscreen doesn't block infrared. Use a broad-spectrum SPF 30+ that you're comfortable applying consistently, since physical and chemical sunscreens both mainly work by absorbing UV. Everyday sunscreen use doesn't seem to lower vitamin D, and sunbeds raise melanoma risk.

## UVA and UVB

UVB is the main cause of sunburn and damages the outer layers of the skin. Its intensity varies by season, peaking from spring to fall in temperate climates, and glass blocks it. UVA makes up as much as 95% of the UV reaching the earth, goes deeper into the skin, and contributes to aging and wrinkles. It stays at about the same strength through the year and passes through windows and clouds, so it's still there on sunny winter days when UVB is limited. Both can contribute to skin cancer.
[Skin Cancer Foundation: UV radiation](https://www.skincancer.org/risk-factors/uv-radiation/)
[AAD: Sunscreen facts](https://www.aad.org/media/stats-sunscreen)

UVA coming through glass is the likely reason a delivery truck driver of about 28 years had far more wrinkling and skin damage on the side of his face next to the window.
[Gordon 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22512500/)

## What sunscreen prevents

### Skin cancer

The best evidence comes from a randomized trial of 1,621 adults in Nambour, Australia. Those assigned to apply SPF 15+ sunscreen daily for 4.5 years had 39% fewer squamous cell carcinoma tumors than those using sunscreen at their own discretion (rate ratio 0.61, 95% CI 0.46–0.81), but no difference in basal cell carcinoma. The number of people getting a first squamous cell carcinoma didn't differ significantly.
[Green 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10475183/)

Ten years after the trial ended, the daily group had 11 new melanomas versus 22 (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.24–1.02, P = 0.051). Invasive melanomas were 3 versus 11 (HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.08–0.97).
[Green 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21135266/)

That's about 0.3% versus 1.4% for invasive melanoma. The overall result falling just above P = 0.05 and the invasive result just below it doesn't make them conflict, since both point the same way. The trial was primarily funded by Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council, and the sunscreen was donated.

A separate randomized trial in 588 Australians aged 40 or older compared SPF 17 sunscreen with the same cream without active ingredients over one summer. The sunscreen group developed fewer new actinic keratoses, which are skin cancer precursors (rate ratio 0.62, 95% CI 0.54–0.71), and more existing ones went away.
[Thompson 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8377777/)

Observational studies look much less favorable. A meta-analysis of 313,717 people, mostly from 25 case-control studies, found no link between sunscreen use and skin cancer (OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.91–1.28), with very high heterogeneity. Early studies even found a higher risk, which faded over time. But people who burn easily and spend more time in the sun are more likely to use sunscreen, so these studies can't tell us what putting on sunscreen does. Randomized trials can.
[Silva 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620003/)

### Is Nambour's sun exposure representative?

Nambour is subtropical, but people there didn't get as much UV as its latitude suggests. When trial participants wore UV-measuring badges, they received only 2.7% of the available UV in summer and 6.5% in winter, so their summer doses were just 1.35 times their winter doses. An estimated 42% of their exposure across the summer and winter months came in winter.
[Neale 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20113430/)

### Burns aren't the only risk

Melanoma doesn't depend only on sunburns. Intermittent exposure and burns predict most melanomas better than chronic exposure does, but lentigo maligna melanoma arises in chronically sun-exposed skin, commonly on the face. Across more than 170,000 US adults, those who lived in high-UV states from birth through adulthood had a higher risk of lentigo maligna melanoma than those in low-UV states (RR 1.51, 95% CI 0.98–2.32), while UV index wasn't related to other melanoma subtypes. In the same cohorts, lifetime UV was tied to squamous cell (RR 2.07) and basal cell carcinoma (RR 1.30).
[Linos 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5459624/)

### Skin aging

In 903 adults under 55 from the same trial, the daily sunscreen group showed no detectable increase in skin aging after 4.5 years, and aged 24% less than the discretionary group (relative odds 0.76, 95% CI 0.59–0.98).
[Hughes 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23732711/)

For damage that's already there, topical retinoids help. In a 16-week double-blind trial of 30 people, 14 of the 15 who used tretinoin on their face improved, and none of those on vehicle cream did. The side effect was skin irritation.
[Weiss 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3336176/)

## Benefits of sunlight

Sun exposure has real benefits, and avoiding the sun is miserable in practice. The goal isn't to avoid the sun, but to get the benefits while limiting the harm.

Observationally, sun avoiders die earlier. In 29,518 Swedish women followed for 20 years, those who avoided the sun had 0.6–2.1 years shorter life expectancy than the most exposed group, mainly from cardiovascular and other non-cancer deaths, and nonsmokers who avoided the sun had a life expectancy similar to smokers in the highest sun exposure group.
[Lindqvist 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26992108/)

The randomized data don't show that UV is the reason. Over 21 years of follow-up of the Nambour trial, daily sunscreen didn't change all-cause mortality (160 vs 170 deaths, HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.76–1.17), cardiovascular deaths (59 vs 76, HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.55–1.08) or cancer deaths (63 vs 58, HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.76–1.57). The sunscreen was SPF 16 on the head, neck, arms and hands for 4.5 years, and the confidence intervals leave room for modest effects either way.
[Lindstrom 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30885518/)

If sunlight does lower mortality, the likely explanation isn't UV on the skin. Sunscreen doesn't block infrared, and sunlight's effects on circadian rhythm work largely through the eyes, so sunscreen shouldn't take those away. UVA can also lower blood pressure by releasing nitric oxide from the skin, as shown in 24 healthy volunteers, but that's an acute effect.
[Liu 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24445737/)

For skin health alone, the best UV dose is as little as practical. Vitamin D, the one benefit that does need UV, can come from a supplement.

### Phototherapy isn't sunlight

Phototherapy helping a condition doesn't mean sunlight will. In a randomized trial of adults with moderate to severe eczema, narrowband UVB reduced disease activity and extent more than visible light did, while UVA didn't clearly help. Sunlight delivers all wavelengths at once, so only a small share of the dose you can tolerate before burning comes from the most effective wavelengths, and some people's eczema gets worse in the sun.
[Reynolds 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11438134/)

## Choosing a sunscreen

Use a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher. SPF 30 blocks about 97% of UVB, and higher numbers block slightly more. Reapply about every 2 hours outdoors and after swimming or sweating. Up to 80% of UV can pass through clouds, so cloudy days count too.
[AAD: Sunscreen facts](https://www.aad.org/media/stats-sunscreen)

Protection is mainly recommended when the UV index is 3 or higher, so sunscreen isn't necessary for a walk on a low-UV winter day. Lower UV still adds up over time, though, so the benefit is lower, not zero.
[WHO: UV index](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/radiation-the-ultraviolet-(uv)-index)

The most important variable is using one you're comfortable with, since that's what you'll apply consistently. If you don't like how sunscreen looks on your skin, a tinted one helps. People with no hair on their scalp need to protect it too.

### Physical vs chemical sunscreens

Physical (mineral) sunscreens are usually described as reflecting and scattering UV, while chemical sunscreens absorb it. That's a leftover from when mineral particles were large. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide reflect only about 4–5% of UV on average, and the rest of their protection comes from absorbing it, the same basic way chemical sunscreens work. They do reflect long UVA and visible light (up to 60%), and that visible reflection is why they can leave a white cast.
[Cole 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26431814/)

So choosing a physical sunscreen because of a different mechanism doesn't make much sense. Cosmetic feel and ease of use matter more, especially since people may use too little to avoid the white cast, which is more noticeable on darker skin.
[Zundell 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/jvc2.251)

## Safety

In a randomized trial of 48 people using lotions and sprays at maximal use, all 6 chemical filters tested were absorbed into the blood above the FDA's 0.5 ng/mL threshold after one application.
[Matta 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31961417/)

Passing that threshold only means more safety testing is needed. The FDA proposes zinc oxide and titanium dioxide as safe and effective, and says that requesting more data on filters like oxybenzone and avobenzone isn't a conclusion that they're unsafe.
[FDA: Regulatory actions on sunscreen](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/understanding-over-counter-medicines/questions-and-answers-fdas-regulatory-actions-over-counter-sunscreen)

In 2021, Johnson & Johnson recalled five aerosol sunscreen lines after finding low levels of benzene. Benzene wasn't an ingredient, and only aerosols were affected.
[FDA: J&J sunscreen recall](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/johnson-johnson-consumer-inc-issues-voluntary-recall-specific-neutrogenar-and-aveenor-aerosol)

## Vitamin D

Sunscreen blocks vitamin D production in lab experiments with artificial UV, but that doesn't seem to carry over to real life. In a systematic review, two randomized field trials found no effect of daily sunscreen on vitamin D, and most of the 69 observational studies found no link, or higher vitamin D in sunscreen users. The trials used SPF of about 16, and none have tested today's high-SPF sunscreens.
[Neale 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30945275/)

Part of the reason is that vitamin D production saturates. With continued UV, previtamin D3 in the skin plateaus at about 10–15% of its precursor, and further UV turns it into inactive products. Sunlight also breaks down vitamin D3 that has already formed. So past a certain point, more UV doesn't make more vitamin D.
[Holick 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6256855/)
[Webb 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2541158/)

That makes vitamin D a loose proxy for how much UV someone gets. In Nambour, using sunscreen, hats or long sleeves wasn't linked to vitamin D, though tending to stay in the shade was (62.5 vs 68.8 nmol/L).
[Jayaratne 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22634425/)

During a week's holiday in Tenerife at a very high UV index, people using SPF 15 as directed avoided sunburn and still raised their vitamin D, by 13–19 nmol/L. That was less than the 28 nmol/L in people using sunscreen at their own discretion, who did burn.
[Young 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31069787/)

In 95 adults in Rio de Janeiro given a brief, non-burning sun exposure, SPF 30 on the face, neck and chest gave a rise in vitamin D similar to no sunscreen (5.4 vs 4.1 ng/mL), though only 10 people went without sunscreen.
[Pereira 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30726465/)

Vitamin D targets of 30 ng/mL (75 nmol/L) are also hard to reach from sun alone. Even in subtropical Nambour, average levels were about 25–28 ng/mL. In white adults in the UK given 6 weeks of simulated summer sun, 90% reached 20 ng/mL but only 26% reached 32 ng/mL.
[Rhodes 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20072137/)

Vitamin D can be supplemented instead → [[Vegan diets]].

## Indoor light

You don't need sunscreen against indoor lights to protect against skin cancer. UV from indoor lighting is far lower than from the summer sun, and melanoma risk is unlikely to be notably affected, since healthy people don't get sunburned by it. Worst-case modeling suggests it could add some squamous cell carcinoma across a whole population, though the risk to any one person is small.
[SCENIHR: Artificial light](https://ec.europa.eu/health/scientific_committees/opinions_layman/artificial-light/en/l-3/6-exposure.htm)

## Tanning

Sunbeds aren't a safe alternative. Across cohort and case-control studies, ever using a sunbed was linked to 20% higher melanoma risk (RR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08–1.34), and first using one before age 35 to 87% higher risk (RR 1.87, 95% CI 1.41–2.48). Risk rose 1.8% for each additional session per year.
[Boniol 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22833605/)

Melanocortin drugs darken skin without UV. Melanotan II isn't an approved drug, and it's one of the reported causes of eruptive moles. In a review of 179 eruptive nevi cases from all causes, 16% had at least one dysplastic nevus and 5 had melanoma, but the data couldn't show the risk of moles turning malignant.
[Burian 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31119650/)

Bremelanotide (Vyleesi) is approved for low sexual desire in premenopausal women, not tanning. Focal hyperpigmentation affected 1% of patients at up to 8 doses a month and 38% after 8 daily doses, more often in dark skin, and it didn't always resolve. It also raises blood pressure temporarily.
[FDA label: Vyleesi](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=f1d0c1b5-2f39-4bad-a6a4-0066e3ad5dcf)
