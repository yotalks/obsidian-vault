> [!summary] Bottom line
> Early COVID-19 lockdowns weren't meant to eliminate the virus. They were meant to slow its spread so hospitals didn't run out of capacity, buying time until immunity, treatments or vaccines arrived. During the Great Depression, US mortality fell rather than rose. Official COVID-19 death counts in spring 2020 undercounted deaths, so incomplete testing didn't only inflate the fatality rate.

## What lockdowns were for

The "hammer and the dance" strategy split the response into two phases. The hammer is a short, strict lockdown to push the reproduction number below 1 and bring active cases down. The dance is a longer period of loosening and re-tightening targeted measures, area by area, to keep cases low enough that hospitals aren't overwhelmed.
[Pueyo 2020](https://tomaspueyo.medium.com/coronavirus-the-hammer-and-the-dance-be9337092b56)

The goal is time, not elimination. Most people may still get infected eventually, but spreading those infections out keeps hospital demand under surge capacity, the number of patients the system can give inpatient care, intensive care and ventilators to. Once demand exceeds surge capacity, the fatality rate rises in every age group, because people who would have survived with care don't get it. The time bought is used to expand surge capacity and wait for one of the endpoints: enough immunity in the population, effective treatments, or a vaccine.

## Recessions and mortality

US population health didn't decline during the Great Depression. From 1930 to 1933, mortality fell for almost all ages and life expectancy rose by several years in men, women, white and nonwhite Americans. Across 1920–1940, mortality tended to peak in years of strong economic expansion (1923, 1926, 1929, 1936–1937), while the recessions of 1921, 1930–1933 and 1938 coincided with falling mortality. The exception was suicide, which rose during the Depression but made up less than 2% of deaths.
[Tapia Granados 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19805076/)

## Protecting only older people

An alternative to general lockdowns is isolating only older people while everyone else carries on. Two problems:
- **Older people contribute to the economy.** In 2018, Americans aged 50 and older contributed about $8.3 trillion a year, around 40% of US GDP. [AARP 2019](https://www.aarp.org/research/topics/economics/info-2019/longevity-economy-outlook.htm)
- **Households are mixed.** Many older people live with younger relatives, so isolating by age would mean deciding household by household who can see whom, which is harder to define and enforce than a rule that applies to everyone.

## Counting COVID-19 deaths

When testing is limited to sicker patients, mild cases are missed, which makes the case fatality rate look higher. But deaths are missed too: people who die untested, such as in nursing homes, often aren't counted as COVID-19 deaths. If a death is missed for every 100 missed cases and the measured fatality rate is 1%, the estimate is still accurate. If deaths are missed at a higher rate than that, the estimate is too low.

In the US from March to May 2020, there were about 122,300 more deaths from all causes than would normally be expected, while 95,235 deaths were officially attributed to COVID-19. Excess deaths were 28% higher than the official count. In several states, the excess deaths happened before testing became widely available and weren't counted as COVID-19 deaths.
[Weinberger 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32609310/)
