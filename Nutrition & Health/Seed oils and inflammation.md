> [!summary] Bottom line
> In diet trials, high linoleic acid intakes, averaging 27 g a day more than low ones, didn't significantly raise CRP, and low linoleic acid diets didn't lower it. Neither high nor low linoleic acid diets seem to have a major effect on inflammation, so avoiding seed oils because of inflammation is misguided.

## CRP in LA trials

The idea that seed oils are inflammatory is very popular. If we have a look at these studies whose intakes in the high [[Glossary#LA|LA]] diet groups ranged from 22.2g/day to 50.8g/day, with an average of 33.5g/day. The low LA diets ranged from 4.8g/day to 7.8g/day, with an average of 6.4g/day. The average difference was 27g/day across the studies.
Altogether, high LA diets had a non-significant increase in [[Glossary#CRP|CRP]] when compared to control. The results were all in all null. It may be possible that the true effect of LA on CRP is hidden and it's plausible that high LA diets do increase CRP, but don't tend to increase CRP much more than control.
Even the Junker study with the highest LA intake difference had null results. There are other possibilities, though, like the subjects' usual diet being already high in LA so the dosing in the interventions doesn't change anything, or the control diet is anti-inflammatory in some way.
Looking at the effects of low LA diets to baseline saw a non-significant decrease in CRP, though still null. So, it doesn't seem like their diets were that protective compared to high LA diets looking at CRP.

## Non-normal CRP data

The Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test was used to test for statistical significance when distributions were not normal. This test was specifically designed for non-normal distributions, and applying this test is also best practice in this case.

[Junker 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11246548/)
[Freese 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15168040/)
[Vafeiadou 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26016869/)
[Iggman 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25319187/)

We see CRP was reported as median and range, because the distributions of CRP were not normal. We see all 3 groups saw non significant decreases in median CRP from baseline, falling from 0.9 to 0.75. But the upper bounds for CRP during the starting week were unusually high in the OO group which means estimating the mean and standard deviation will skew the variance and the mean a lot higher than it should be. This makes it look like there is a drop in CRP in the OO group that was a lot larger than it actually was which increases the treatment effect seen when using estimated means and standard deviations. This also happened in the SU group. The range is a lot narrower at baseline than after the intervention, which skewed the variance and mean toward showing an increase in CRP that didn't actually happen, so both groups saw a non-significant decrease in CRP
[Hozo 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15840177/)

## Overall

So all in all, both high LA and low LA diets seem to not have a major effect on CRP even when LA is very high, so avoiding LA because of inflammation is misguided.
