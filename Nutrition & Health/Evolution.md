> [!summary] Bottom line
> Evolution optimizes reproduction, not long-term health, and antagonistic pleiotropy means ancestral adaptations can harm us later in life. So when novel and ancestral foods perform equally in the short term, the novel food is the better long-term bet.

## Evolution favors reproduction, not longevity

The common notion that what we evolved with is what we would do best on is based on a fundamental misunderstanding of how evolution works. Evolution doesn't have the goal of keeping your body alive and healthy to maximize your lifespan or health. The goal of your genes is to replicate, propagate, and fixate in the population.

Reducing your lifespan can in fact be the evolutionarily preferable thing to do. Given the trade off between reproductive fitness and lifespan, evolution will favor reproductive fitness any day of the week.
![[Selection shadow survival and reproduction.png]]

In their selection experiment, mainly on fruit flies, they found lifespan diverged by about 30% after 6 generations, and the long living females produced around half as many offspring over their lifetimes as the short living ones.
[Zwaan 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10383679/)

Humans and animals are built as a compromise that ensures survival till reproductive age, because that is the period natural selection acts on. Modern hygiene, medicine, and social conditions now is what lets us live far past reproductive years.
[Wick 2003](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0531556502001614)

## Antagonistic pleiotropy

Wherever [[Antagonistic pleiotropy|antagonistic pleiotropy]] has been seriously looked for it's been found. Daf-2 mutant worms live about 2x as long but reproduce 18-23% less, and when competed against normal worms, they went extinct after 4 generations.
In mice, Ames dwarf mice live 48-67% longer than controls but have reduced fertility or are sterile.
In an outdoor enclosure study of the p66shc gene, the normal mice nearly tripled in number over 13 months, while knockout mice fell 4 fold.
[Austad 2018](https://academic.oup.com/emph/article/2018/1/287/5126836)

Antagonistic pleiotropy is the best accepted theory for why aging evolved.
[Mitteldorf 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1134/S0006297919120058)

Human DNA tends to degrade over time when it doesn't need to, which biologically immortal organisms show. Human DNA repair is also regulated and gene-specific. Given this, the interaction between gene degradation and gene repair is likely adaptive. This gives every gene in our DNA that tends to degrade more with time a single antagonistically pleiotropic trait. Since most DNA in the human genome degrades over time, we can infer over 50% of genes and antagonistically pleiotropic.

## Implications for diet

Perhaps adaptations to sodium and saturated fat consumption, like regulated changes in blood pressure or lipoprotein secretion from the liver, may help carry people to reproductive age without problem, but those adaptations may increase risk of poor long term health outcomes if exposed to too long. This is why natural or evolutionary diets are actually good for long term health in modern humans. These adaptations to diet are likely to be antagonistically pleiotropic.

Without empirical data to inform our attitudes for relative health value of novel foods, the health value of those foods are unknown and due to principle of indifference, we have no reason to believe it's good or bad for us. But if evidence can be shown both an ancestral food exposure and a novel one have same reproductive thriving potential, then we can infer the novel food is to be favor. This is because novel foods aren't in domain of foods that have potential to be antagonistic pleiotropic as we did not evolve consuming them, so the novel food is to be favored over the ancestral one here.
![[Novel vs ancestral food health value.png]]

We can therefore infer that ancestral foods are likely to have both a positive short term health value, but a negative long term health value. But in the absence of scientific evidence, our doxastic attitude toward health value of novel foods should be agnostic. When the thriving potential in reproductive years is the same for both ancestral and novel foods are shown to be the same, then we can infer that the novel foods is less likely to be detrimental for long term health. 

So when you equalize the advantages of a novel and ancestral food, the inherent disadvantages of antagonistic pleiotropy would have us favoring the novel food over the ancestral food for long term health. So we can infer a priori that a diet that maximizes benefits and minimizes risks for the most amount of people will likely be a diet that has some level of unnatural novel foods. This is not to say every novel food is superior to every ancestral food nor saying all ancestral foods are bad, but it's a heuristic.

Some may bring up that some ancestral foods like red meat seem to increase risk of many diseases, while others like fruit seem to decrease risk of many diseases. Both are antagonistically pleiotropic but it's likely that the pathways influenced by foods like fruit are less impactful than foods like meat.

The healthfulness of foods at the end of the day is relative to other substitutions, replacing meat with fruit reduces risk, but that doesn't mean fruit has no long term harms or risks. If we truly want to maximize thriving potential of foods, we should make our own novel foods and artificially manipulating natural foods, and we have evidence this helps like for example Golden rice.

> [!question] Objections to prepare for
> - Many novel foods (trans fats, some additives) turned out harmful; why favor novelty a priori?
> - 'Ancestral diet' isn't one thing; ancestral diets varied widely.
> - Antagonistic pleiotropy is about genes; is the step from genes to whole foods justified?
