> [!summary] Bottom line
> The Agricultural Health Study, the first major prospective cohort on glyphosate, followed more than 54,000 pesticide applicators and found no consistent link between glyphosate and cancer, including non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The case-control studies that suggested a link sit lower on the evidence hierarchy and are prone to recall bias. The cohort's isolated positive findings, for multiple myeloma early on and AML later, came from few cases and didn't hold up or haven't been confirmed.

## The Agricultural Health Study

The Agricultural Health Study (AHS) is a prospective cohort of licensed pesticide applicators in Iowa and North Carolina, enrolled in 1993–1997. Applicators reported their pesticide use before any cancer was diagnosed, which avoids the recall bias of case-control studies, where people with cancer may remember past exposures differently.

Glyphosate exposure was measured three ways:
- **Ever use:** ever personally mixed or applied glyphosate products.
- **Lifetime exposure days:** years of use × days per year.
- **Intensity-weighted exposure days:** lifetime days × an intensity score based on mixing, application method, equipment repair and protective equipment.

Before the AHS, three case-control studies had suggested a link between glyphosate use and [[Glossary#NHL|NHL]].

## First analysis (2005)

The first analysis covered 57,311 applicators, 75.5% of whom had used glyphosate, with a median follow-up of 6.7 years. Glyphosate wasn't associated with cancer overall or with most cancer types, including NHL.

Contrary to case control studies, the clearest dose-response finding was a decreased risk of lung cancer, 0.6 (0.3–1.0), in the highest tertile of intensity-weighted exposure days (P for trend = 0.02).

There was also a suggested association with multiple myeloma. Ever use was linked to more than twice the risk, and compared with people who never used glyphosate, the highest tertile of lifetime exposure days had a higher risk (RR 4.4, 95% CI 1.0–20.2). This rested on only 32 myeloma cases, and the authors said it needed follow-up as more cases occurred.
[De Roos 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15626647/)

## Updated analysis (2018)

The update followed 54,251 applicators through 2012–2013, adding follow-up questionnaires on later pesticide use. 82.8% had used glyphosate, and there were 5,779 cancers among users.

Unlagged, glyphosate wasn't statistically significantly associated with cancer at any site. No cancer had a significant trend with increasing exposure, and no cancer was significantly raised in the highest exposure quartile. A few middle categories were raised without any dose-response, such as lung cancer in the third quartile.
- **NHL:** RR 0.87 in the highest quartile (95% CI 0.64–1.20), with no NHL subtype raised.
- **Multiple myeloma:** RR 0.87 (0.45–1.69), so the earlier signal didn't hold up with more cases.
- **Testicular cancer:** RR 0.57 (0.20–1.67) in the highest tertile (P for trend = 0.07), the lowest P for trend of any cancer in the unlagged analysis.
- **[[Glossary#AML|AML]]:** RR 2.44 (0.94–6.32) in the highest quartile, not significant.

Lagging exposure by 5 years didn't change these results. With a 20-year lag, the highest tertile for AML became significant, RR 2.04 (1.05–3.97), P for trend = 0.04. The lag analyses were only run for blood and lymph cancers, so there's no lagged result for testicular cancer to compare. Apart from a raised estimate for T-cell NHL in the lower exposure group (RR 2.97, 1.20–7.31, from 9 cases), none of the other blood and lymph cancers was significant with the 20-year lag. The authors called the AML finding something that needs confirmation.

The study was funded by the NIH's intramural research program, and most of its authors work at the National Cancer Institute and NIEHS.
[Andreotti 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29136183/)
