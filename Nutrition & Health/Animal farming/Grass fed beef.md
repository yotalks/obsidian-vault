> [!summary] Bottom line
> There's little reason to think grass-fed beef is healthier than grain-fed. Grain-finished cattle already spend most of their lives on grass, and switching US beef to grass-finishing would need roughly 145–475 million more acres of pasture, more than all the land now used for livestock feed. Grass-fed beef also has about 1.7 times the carbon footprint of conventional beef. White Oak Pastures, a showcase regenerative farm, had a lower net carbon footprint than conventional farms once soil carbon was counted, but used 2.5 times as much land and still emitted more per kg than a Beyond Burger.

## Health

It's often claimed grass-fed beef is healthier than grain-fed, though it's unclear why. The only small reason could be that grain-finished beef has more fat, and so more saturated fat, even though the percentage of fat that's saturated is the same. But the people who promote grass-fed beef tend to believe saturated fat is healthy anyway.
[Van Elswyk 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0309174013004944#tf0025)

### Polyphenols in grass-fed beef

If one is committed to the notions that polyphenols in plants are bad for humans, and that grass-fed red meat is the healthiest available type of red meat, then they're pragmatically committed to being pro-GMO to lower the grass-derived polyphenol content of grass-fed beef.
In a comparison of 18 pasture-finished and 18 grain-finished Black Angus cattle, pasture-finished beef had 2.6 times the phenolic antioxidants, along with more alpha-tocopherol and omega-3s. These compounds come from the grasses, forbs and shrubs the cattle graze.
[Evans 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39215122/)

## How US cattle are raised

US beef cattle start on pasture in the cow-calf phase (about 6–10 months), often spend 2–6 months more grazing as stockers, and then differ in the finishing phase. Grass-finished cattle keep grazing for about 6–10 months and are harvested at 20–26 months. Grain-finished cattle spend about 4–6 months in a feedlot and are harvested at 14–22 months.
![[Grass vs grain finished cattle timeline.png]]
[Beef Research: Grass versus grain-finished](https://www.beefresearch.org/resources/beef-sustainability/fact-sheets/grass-versus-grain-finished)

So grain-finished cattle spend most of their lives on grass: between about 57% (8 of 14 months) and 81% (18 of 22 months) of their time, with a middle estimate of about 72%.

## Land use

One common proposal is to replace the corn and soy that go to feedlots with grazing. US cropland is used like this:

|---|---|---|
| Livestock feed | 127.4M | 32.5% |
| Food Americans eat | 77.3M | 19.75% |
| Grain and feed exports | 62.8M | 16% |
| Idle or fallow | 52M | 13.3% |
| Biofuel | 38.1M | 9.7% |
| Wheat exports | 21.5M | 5.5% |
| Non-food crops like cotton | 13.6M | 3.5% |
| **Total cropland** | **391.5M** | |

[Bloomberg 2018](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2018-us-land-use/)

Assuming cattle use the same amount of land at every stage of life (they don't; calves use less), shifting grain-finished cattle to grass-finishing would need this much extra pasture to keep current beef production:
- **Lower estimate:** 144.5M acres
- **Middle estimate:** 250.5M acres
- **Upper estimate:** 473.6M acres

All of these are more than the land used to grow livestock feed. Even converting all 90 million acres of corn and 85 million acres of soy to pasture wouldn't reach the 250 million extra acres needed.

That feed land also feeds other animals. US feed use in 2016 by animal, out of 236,275,155 tons in total:

![[US feed use by animal 2016.png]]
[AllAboutFeed 2018](https://www.allaboutfeed.net/Compound-Feed/Articles/2018/1/Insight-into-the-US-animal-feed-market-242428E/)

Assuming every ton of feed takes the same land, cattle feed uses about 53.1M acres. So even freeing up all of it, the middle estimate would still need 197.4M acres more, an extremely inefficient trade-off.

Some also say the roughly 52 million acres of idle or fallow land should be grazed to regenerate it. There's no evidence fallow land isn't needed to protect against erosion. And even if grazing it worked as claimed, it wouldn't free up enough land: we'd still be 145.4M acres short.

A national analysis reached similar conclusions.
[Hayek 2018](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aad401)

## Carbon footprint

Grass-fed beef has a bigger footprint than conventional beef. To produce 100 million kg of beef, the conventional system needed 56.3% of the animals, 24.8% of the water, 55.3% of the land and 71.4% of the fossil fuel energy of the grass-fed system, and its carbon footprint was the lowest. Grass-fed beef's carbon footprint was about 1.7 times as high.
[Capper 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4494320/)

## White Oak Pastures

A peer-reviewed life-cycle analysis of White Oak Pastures, a multi-species regenerative farm, compared it with conventional (commodity) production. Before counting soil carbon, it emitted more greenhouse gases per kg of carcass weight. Its soils sequestered an average of 2.29 Mg of carbon per hectare per year over 20 years, and counting that cut its net emissions by 80%, to about 2.9 times lower (66% lower) than conventional farms.
![[White Oak Pastures emissions.png]]

But it needed 2.5 times as much land: about 1,500 hectares for its cattle and about 500 for its poultry and pork.
![[White Oak Pastures land use.png]]
[Rowntree 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2020.544984/full#F2)

Counted across the whole farm, it isn't a net carbon sink: net emissions were about 4 kg CO2e per kg of carcass weight. (If all the soil carbon were credited to the cattle alone, the beef would be a net sink of −4.4 kg.) That's still more than a Beyond Burger's 3.4 kg CO2e per kg of product, and the gap is bigger than it looks, since carcass weight includes bone and fat that aren't eaten.
[Heller 2018](https://css.umich.edu/sites/default/files/publication/CSS18-10.pdf)

Getting to that −4.4 means crediting nearly all of the farm's soil carbon to the cattle. Crediting 75% of it to them instead gives net emissions of about 5.1 kg CO2e per kg of carcass weight, which is 1.5 times the Beyond Burger's 3.4 kg.
![[White Oak Pastures 75% sequestration vs Beyond Burger.png]]
Worked from the farm's emissions and sequestration figures and the Beyond Burger footprint.
[Rowntree 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2020.544984/full#F2) · [Heller 2018](https://css.umich.edu/sites/default/files/publication/CSS18-10.pdf)

### Herd productivity

White Oak Pastures' own life-cycle analysis compared its herd with the conventional system modeled by Rotz (2013), which uses rotational grazing for the cow-calf stage and a feedlot for finishing. White Oak's cattle were slaughtered at 24 months and 520 kg, versus 17 months and 580 kg. Its herd of 3,055 cattle produced 990 slaughtered head a year, about 0.32 per animal kept, versus 5,050 from a herd of 12,000 (about 0.42 per animal) in the conventional system.
![[White Oak Pastures vs Rotz 2013 herd.png]]
[Quantis 2019: White Oak Pastures LCA](https://blog.whiteoakpastures.com/hubfs/WOP-LCA-Quantis-2019.pdf)

### Compared with better crop farming

Regenerative grazing is usually compared with conventional crop farming: monocropped, tilled, without rotation or cover. If the goal is farming that cuts emissions or stores carbon per hectare, the fair comparison is regenerative grazing against crop farming that uses improved practices too.

In a 25-year field experiment in Saskatchewan, spring wheat grown with improved practices (fertilizing based on soil tests, less summer fallow, and rotating cereals with legumes like peas and lentils) had a negative carbon footprint, averaging −256 kg CO2e per hectare per year. Each kg of wheat grain stored a net 0.027–0.377 kg CO2e in the soil, so the crop took up more CO2 than its production emitted.
[Gan 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms6012)

## Land unsuitable for crops

The claim that livestock make use of land that can't grow crops mainly comes from an FAO analysis. Livestock feed uses about 2.5 billion hectares, half of all agricultural land. About 2 billion of that is grassland, and 1.3 billion hectares of it was classed as unable to be converted to cropland, so 57% of the land used for feed isn't suitable for food crops. The same analysis found 86% of livestock feed (by dry matter) is material humans don't currently eat, and producing 1 kg of boneless meat takes 2.8 kg of human-edible feed in ruminant systems and 3.2 kg in monogastric systems.

Grassland counted as unconvertible if its actual crop yield was below 25% of its potential yield, the level ranked as "marginal" suitability for crops.
[Mottet 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gfs.2017.01.001)

That definition is a yield cutoff, and grazing systems have yield gaps too. Regenerative grazing at White Oak Pastures needed 2.5 times the land to produce the same carcass weight as conventional production, about 40% of the conventional yield per hectare (see White Oak Pastures above). To compare how much grassland could go to regenerative grazing versus regenerative crops, the same kind of yield standard would have to apply to both.
