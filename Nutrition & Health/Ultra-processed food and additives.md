> [!summary] Bottom line
> The MAHA report blames ultra-processed foods and their additives for children's chronic disease, but many of its specific claims are weak. The omega-6/omega-3 "imbalance" worry isn't backed by outcome trials. The food-dye effect it cites is about a tenth of the ADHD gap and disappears in high-quality studies of FDA-approved dyes. BHT's rodent tumor effects came at 10–200 times human intake. And aspartame shows little to no effect on glucose or insulin in controlled human studies. Titanium dioxide is the exception: Europe's food safety authority no longer considers it safe as an additive.

## The report's claims

The report says ultra-processed foods (UPFs) now make up nearly 70% of children's calories, and singles out three components:
- **Ultra-processed grains:** refining removes the bran and germ, stripping vitamins, minerals and fiber, causing blood sugar spikes and raising the risk of type 2 diabetes.
- **Ultra-processed sugars:** found in 75% of packaged foods. The average American eats 17 teaspoons of added sugar a day (60 pounds a year), and 63% of Americans aged 2 and older get over 10% of their calories from added sugars.
- **Ultra-processed fats:** US dietary fat shifted from butter and lard, rich in vitamins A, D and E, to refined seed oils, which the report says lose vitamin E and create an imbalanced omega-6/omega-3 ratio linked to inflammation.

Reviews of UPFs and health:
[Gearhardt 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37813420/)
[Mescoloto 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37866398/)

## Seed oils and the omega-6/omega-3 ratio

The ratio argument goes back to papers like Simopoulos 2008. But the best outcome evidence for raising omega-3s is unimpressive: in the large VITAL trial, omega-3 supplements didn't lower major cardiovascular events or cancer compared with placebo.
[Simopoulos 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18408140/)
[Manson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415637/)

## Food dyes

The report says food colorings like Red 40 are linked to hyperactivity and ADHD symptoms.

Its first citation shows the effect isn't clinically meaningful, if anything: effect sizes averaged about 0.18. Children with ADHD score about 2 standard deviations higher on hyperactivity than children without it, so an effect of 0.2 is about 10% of the difference between them.
[McCann 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17825405/)

A review often cited for food-dye harms counted a study as showing an "association" if it was statistically significant **or** simply had an effect size of at least 20% (or 0.20 standard deviations), even without significance.
[Miller 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35484553/)

In a meta-analysis, food colors had a small, significant effect across all studies (0.21), but not in high-quality studies (0.13), not for FDA-approved colors (0.13), and none at all in high-quality studies of FDA-approved colors (0.00).
![[Food color meta-analysis effect sizes.jpg]]
[Nigg 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22176942/)

## Titanium dioxide

The report says titanium dioxide may cause cellular and DNA damage. Here the report has support: in 2021 the European Food Safety Authority concluded titanium dioxide (E171) can no longer be considered safe as a food additive, because concerns about genotoxicity couldn't be ruled out.
[EFSA 2021](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/6585)

## Parabens

The report says propylparaben shows estrogenic activity that may disrupt hormones. The human evidence is mixed:
- Higher urinary methylparaben and propylparaben were linked to higher breast cancer risk (ORs 1.31–1.50), but also to lower mortality after diagnosis (HRs 0.68–0.77).
[Parada 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31228785/)
- In a multiethnic cohort, breast cancer risk was weakly *inversely* associated with paraben exposure.
[Wu 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34013527/)
- Some personal care chemicals, including parabens, were associated with altered puberty timing in girls, with little association in boys; most shifts were small and not significant.
[Harley 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30517665/)
[Hager 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35162895/)

## BHT

The report says BHT, a preservative in snacks and cereals, "may be associated with tumor growth in rodent studies". The rodent effects appeared at doses of 10–200 mg/kg, which is 10 to 200 times what humans consume per kilogram.
[JECFA: BHT evaluation](https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v042je24.htm)

## Artificial sweeteners and aspartame

The report says artificial sweeteners disrupt the gut microbiome, and notes aspartame's classification as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B) by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

Sweeteners altering gut bacteria and causing glucose intolerance has mostly been shown in rodents.
[Suez 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25231862/)
[Conz 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37111090/)

In controlled human intervention studies, aspartame had little to no effect on blood glucose or insulin, short or long term (though the certainty of evidence is very low).
[Boxall 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40381807/)

IARC's categories are broad. Group 2B ("possibly carcinogenic") also includes aloe vera extract and traditional pickled vegetables, and Group 2A ("probably carcinogenic") includes red meat, very hot drinks above 65 °C, and androgenic (anabolic) steroids.
