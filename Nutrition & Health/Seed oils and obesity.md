> [!summary] Bottom line
> Seed oils don't drive obesity. The 2-AG appetite mechanism works in mice but not in humans, appetite trials are a wash, LAVAT subjects' weight didn't change while their tissue linoleic acid tripled, and PUFA raises energy expenditure and fat burning more than saturated fat does.

## Obesity: the endocannabinoid (2-AG) hypothesis

Seed oils are often attributed to the obesity epidemic. It's hypothesized that dietary [[Glossary#LA|LA]] increases hunger through production of endocannabinoid known as 2-arachidonoylglycerol ([[Glossary#2-AG|2-AG]]) in this study. They say that this endocannabinoid interacts with cannabinoid receptor type 1 ([[Glossary#CB1|CB1]]) and facilitates obesity by stimulating appetite. So basically seed oils make you hungry similarly to weed.
[Watkins 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25610411/)

This mechanism does work in mice. Mice were fed two different diets with varying fatty acid compositions. They were randomized to 2 diets that contained either moderate fat (35% of energy) or high fat (60% of energy). Within each diet group, there were 3 distinct diet conditions. One of the diet conditions was low in LA, and the other 2 were "high" in LA. with each also being supplemented with long-chain omega-3s.
The mice receiving 8% of their energy from LA had consistently higher body weight, with a slightly mitigating effect of supplemented omega-3s in the mice fed a high-fat diet. The increases in body weight in mice that were fed high-LA diets was commensurate with increases in 2-AG.
[Alvheim 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22334255/)

A study that involved 60 humans, however, explored the correlation between body fatness and a number of markers related to the endocannabinoid system. There ended up being significant correlations between circulating 2-AG and obesity. The problem is that, unlike mice, circulating levels of the 2-AG precursor, ARA, didn't differ between lean and obese subjects. Because of this, the authors express skepticism toward the hypothesis that 2-AG synthesis is driven passively by the supply of precursors in humans. They instead say there's greater evidence that obesity itself acts to inhibit the degradation of 2-AG.
This would be expected, considering that the impaired clearance and/or degradation of metabolic substrates is a well-characterized phenomenon in human obesity.
[Blüher 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17065342/)

Not only that, but there is a large amount of literature divulging that the production of 2-AG from its precursors is regulated, not passive.
[Piomelli 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14595399/)

We've also tested the effects of selective CB1-antagonism in humans with a pharmaceutical called Rimonabant. This drug does appear to reduce energy intake and result in weight loss that is equivalent to 0.25 lbs. a week. But CB-antagonists such as this aren't specifically targeting LA metabolism. All it tells us is that the endocannabinoid system is involved with the regulation of body weight in humans, but not what independent contributions are being made by 2-AG, or even dietary LA.
[Curioni 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17054276/)

## Appetite and long-term weight

There have been a number of short-term fatty acid substitution trials investigating effects of dietary LA on appetite. One trial showed an effect of LA on hunger hormones, and a non-significant reduction in prospective energy intake compared to control and high-oleate diets. But, the authors claim that there was no effect of the high-LA diet in reducing prospective energy intake. This is an example of the interaction fallacy because there was actually non-inferiority between the high-LA diet and the other diet conditions.
[Naughton 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30261617)

Of the trials that actually reported ad libitum energy intake in response to diets of varying fatty acid composition, no consistent effects were observed. 
[Flint 2003](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/14694208)
[Stevenson 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26331956/)
[Lawton 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10953671/)
[French 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11040181/)
[MacIntosh 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23328113/)
[Stevenson 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28760423/)

Altogether, the short-term evidence is mostly a wash. This was remarked upon in this study where they included the aggregated findings across a number of different trials investigating the relationship between fatty acid saturation, satiety, and energy intake. Overall, when there was an effect of LA, it tended to reduce energy intake. In one particularly good trial by Strik et al., participants in the 3 groups were given unlimited access to muffins made using either [[Glossary#SFA|SFA]], [[Glossary#MUFA|MUFA]], or [[Glossary#PUFA|PUFA]]. They would eat these muffins ad libitum one variety at a time, with a washout period between muffin types. At the end, all 3 groups had all muffin types and each time they collected subjective data on satiety and the general satisfaction of the food experience, there were no differences that reached statistical significance. It appeared as though SFA trended toward a decrease in fullness. There were also no observed differences in ad libitum energy intake.
One criticism is that the subjects' diets may have already been so high in LA that the hunger effects were blunted. This supposedly creates the illusion of equal satiety benefits between fatty acids, because the hunger generating effects are marked by a sort of LA tolerance. This is possible, but the researchers specifically used a cross-over like design specifically to minimize this confounder.
It's also proposed that in order to study this effect, researchers may need to run-in the subjects on an LA-depleting diet for months (or even years) leading up to the satiety tests. That isn't necessary, however, as this was also investigated in the LAVAT trial. In this ad libitum double-blind RCT, normal weight subjects were randomized to one of two groups. The first group was placed on a seed oil containing diet that yielded about 42.3g/day of LA, whereas the second group was placed on an animal fat containing diet that yielded only approximately 10.8g/day. The difference in LA intake led to a large increase in adipose LA in the seed oil group, so it's likely safe to say that these people reached a maximal saturation point, as evidenced by the hyperbolic curve in adipose LA representation over time.
![[LAVAT adipose linoleic acid over time.png]]
[Strik 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20492735/)

The median LA representation in adipose tissue increased from around 8-11% of total fatty acids to over 30% of total fatty acids across the 8 year study. The baseline levels are consistent with levels found in traditional populations like the Tsimane, Inuit, and the Navajo aboriginals.
[Martin 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22624983/)
[Hirsch 1965](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cphy.cp050117)

While the data published in this study representing the fatty acid composition of breast milk in the Tsimane, it's also true that the LA contents of both breast milk and adipose tissue correlate very closely.
[Heldenberg 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16829413/)
[Martin 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8237871/)
[Demmelmair 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9684741/)

There was no statistically significant differences between the baseline tissue LA representation in the LAVAT and the measurements taken from either the Navajo or Tsimane. However, tissue LA was statistically significantly higher among Inuit when compared to the baseline measurements observed in the LAVAT. We can likely infer because of this that the subjects in the LAVAT study were largely starting at ancestral levels of tissue LA and increasing to about 3 fold higher levels over the 8 year period of the trial. Despite this, it didn't affect their body weight over time.
![[LAVAT body weight by diet.png]]

Some may say that the 10.8g of LA in the control group was too high and differences could've been masked as they passed a certain threshold. But the LA of the animal fat group was within bounds of preagricultural LA intakes. And this was an ad libitum trial in normal weight subjects and bodyweight remained within 2% of baseline. The LA intakes in the seed oil group universally overshoot the upper bounds of all of those same estimates of preagricultural LA intakes. This means the animal fat group was having levels of LA before the obesity epidemic started and the seed oil group was having levels way higher than preagricultural estimates of LA consumption.
[Kuipers 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20860883/)

We also have a secondary endpoint analysis done and the Oslo Diet-Heart saw a 2.5kg reduction in body weight during their study period, and the Medical Research Council saw no change in body weight.
[Leren 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2607071/)
[Medical Research Council 1968](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4175085/)

It seems that seed oils do not uniquely increase body weight in humans and while they may increase 2-AG and obesity in mice, this doesn't seem to pan out in humans. Large scale RCTs in humans do not support this hypothesis.

## Energy expenditure and fat burning

The effect of varying PUFA and SFA in the diet on measures of energy expenditure has been tested many times and show consistently higher in PUFA and lower in SFA tend to increase [[Glossary#EE|EE]] in humans.
For example, the Lichtenbelt et al. study saw a consistent effect across all 6 subjects with higher PUFA intakes increasing postprandial EE. The same trend was seen for resting metabolic rate. So, high SFA, low PUFA diets tend to lower EE and [[Glossary#RMR|RMR]] compared to high PUFA, low SFA diets. PUFA and MUFA seem to be mixed in results but SFA always end up last.
[Krishnan 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24363161/)
[Jones 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1556946/)
[van Marken Lichtenbelt 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9467221/)

The effects of high PUFA feeding on fat oxidation has also been studied and when the respiratory quotient to compare the effects of diets high in PUFA, MUFA, and SFA on EE and fat oxidation, we see a similar trend again. High PUFA feeding resulted in higher postprandial EE as well as higher thermic effect of feeding. Though the differences in the rate of fat oxidation didn't reach significance between groups, there was an obvious trend that reflected the degree of fatty acid saturation. PUFA was the most thermogenic, SFA was the least, and MUFA was in the middle.
[Casas-Agustench 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19010571/)

The same results were seen in this study, only this time different dietary fats containing labeled carbon isotopes were used. You can measure these isotopes in the breath in order to measure how much of the dietary fat a subject has consumed was burned in the time after a meal. Using this methodology, we see that there is one type of SFA that is preferentially oxidized over all other FAs that were tested, which is lauric acid. But, lauric acid might have ketogenic properties. Other FAs tested, we see that PUFA again, has the highest oxidation rate, followed by MUFA, and SFA again is last.
[DeLany 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11010930/)

Seed oils don't seem to make you fat, and even if they did, it doesn't seem their effects on thermogenesis and EE are likely to mediate that. Seed oils seem to potentially have the opposite effect.
