> [!summary] Bottom line
> This note finds weak support for many of the MAHA report's specific claims about food additives and children's chronic disease. It reviews omega-6 fats, food dyes, BHT and aspartame, while treating titanium dioxide as an exception. It also examines claims about the safety of European versus US infant formula.

## The report's claims

The report says ultra-processed foods (UPFs) now make up nearly 70% of children's calories, and singles out three components:
- **Ultra-processed grains:** refining removes the bran and germ, stripping vitamins, minerals and fiber, causing blood sugar spikes and raising the risk of type 2 diabetes.
- **Ultra-processed sugars:** found in 75% of packaged foods. The average American eats 17 teaspoons of added sugar a day (60 pounds a year), and 63% of Americans aged 2 and older get over 10% of their calories from added sugars.
- **Ultra-processed fats:** US dietary fat shifted from butter and lard, rich in vitamins A, D and E, to refined seed oils, which the report says lose vitamin E and create an imbalanced omega-6/omega-3 ratio linked to inflammation.

Reviews of UPFs and health:
[Gearhardt 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37813420/)
[Mescoloto 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37866398/)

## Seed oils and the omega-6/omega-3 ratio

The ratio argument goes back to papers like Simopoulos 2008. But the best outcome evidence for raising omega-3s is unimpressive: in the large VITAL trial, omega-3 supplements didn't lower major cardiovascular events or cancer compared with placebo.
[Simopoulos 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18408140/)
[Manson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415637/)

## Food dyes

The report says food colorings like Red 40 are linked to hyperactivity and ADHD symptoms.

Its first citation shows the effect isn't clinically meaningful, if anything: effect sizes averaged about 0.18. Children with ADHD score about 2 standard deviations higher on hyperactivity than children without it, so an effect of 0.2 is about 10% of the difference between them.
[McCann 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17825405/)

A review often cited for food-dye harms counted a study as showing an "association" if it was statistically significant **or** simply had an effect size of at least 20% (or 0.20 standard deviations), even without significance.
[Miller 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35484553/)

In a meta-analysis, food colors had a small, significant effect across all studies (0.21), but not in high-quality studies (0.13), not for FDA-approved colors (0.13), and none at all in high-quality studies of FDA-approved colors (0.00).
![[Food color meta-analysis effect sizes.jpg]]
[Nigg 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22176942/)

## Titanium dioxide

The report says titanium dioxide may cause cellular and DNA damage. Here the report has support: in 2021 the European Food Safety Authority concluded titanium dioxide (E171) can no longer be considered safe as a food additive, because concerns about genotoxicity couldn't be ruled out.
[EFSA 2021](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/6585)

## Parabens

The report says propylparaben shows estrogenic activity that may disrupt hormones. The human evidence is mixed:
- Higher urinary methylparaben and propylparaben were linked to higher breast cancer risk (ORs 1.31–1.50), but also to lower mortality after diagnosis (HRs 0.68–0.77).
[Parada 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31228785/)
- In a multiethnic cohort, breast cancer risk was weakly *inversely* associated with paraben exposure.
[Wu 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34013527/)
- Some personal care chemicals, including parabens, were associated with altered puberty timing in girls, with little association in boys; most shifts were small and not significant.
[Harley 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30517665/)
[Hager 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35162895/)

## BHT

The report says BHT, a preservative in snacks and cereals, "may be associated with tumor growth in rodent studies". The rodent effects appeared at doses of 10–200 mg/kg, which is 10 to 200 times what humans consume per kilogram.
[JECFA: BHT evaluation](https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v042je24.htm)

## Artificial sweeteners and aspartame

The report says artificial sweeteners disrupt the gut microbiome, and notes aspartame's classification as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B) by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

Sweeteners altering gut bacteria and causing glucose intolerance has mostly been shown in rodents.
[Suez 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25231862/)
[Conz 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37111090/)

In controlled human intervention studies, aspartame had little to no effect on blood glucose or insulin, short or long term (though the certainty of evidence is very low).
[Boxall 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40381807/)
→ [[Artificial sweeteners]]

IARC's categories are broad. Group 2B ("possibly carcinogenic") also includes aloe vera extract and traditional pickled vegetables, and Group 2A ("probably carcinogenic") includes red meat, very hot drinks above 65 °C, and androgenic (anabolic) steroids.
→ [[Artificial sweeteners#Cancer|Aspartame and cancer cohorts]]

## Infant formula

The report says some American parents have turned to European formula brands, because of supply concerns and questions about the number and types of additives in US formulas.

US infant formula is one of the most tightly regulated foods in the country: the FDA dictates its nutrients and vitamins and sets strict rules for production, packaging and labeling. Importing European formula is technically illegal, and it costs far more (about $1.70 per 6-ounce bottle for one popular brand, versus about 45¢ for top US picks).

A study of popular European brands found they generally meet most FDA nutrition guidelines, but importing them raises safety concerns: instructions may be in Dutch or German, they're mixed at a different scoop-to-water ratio (one scoop per ounce instead of per 2 ounces) so parents can prepare them wrong, there's no way to know whether they were kept at safe temperatures in transit, the FDA doesn't inspect them, and EU recalls are hard to track.

The real differences are small:
- The EU bans some added sugars like corn syrup in standard milk-based formula and requires at least 30% of carbohydrates to come from lactose, but many US formulas meet that standard too.
- US formulas often contain more iron, which the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends to prevent iron deficiency. The EU requires much more of the omega-3 DHA, but research doesn't clearly show benefits from those higher amounts.
- Many US formulas include extra ingredients like lutein, taurine, nucleotides and L-carnitine. These are found in breast milk, though not all have been shown to help when added to formula.
- Europe offers whole-milk and goat's-milk formulas, but infants allergic to cow's milk will likely react to goat's milk too.

Experts don't think the theoretical advantages of European formula are worth the risks and cost.
[Wirecutter 2021](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/blog/us-parents-european-baby-formula/)

## Are UPFs inherently harmful?

It's often claimed the standard Western diet is unhealthy because it's comprised solely of UPFs and these are inherently unhealthy. But the latter doesn't follow necessarily.
![[Pasted image 20261105221809.png]]

All these combinations can be true technically.

Firstly, it's true that the SWD is characterized by UPF consumption and that UPF consumption correlates well with poor health outcomes. This doesn't mean the UPFs themselves are harmful though.
[Vandevijvere 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31099480/)
→ [[Ecological fallacy]]

We can agree fruit is healthy, but if you ate nothing but fruit, that would be incredibly unhealthy, yet this doesn't make fruit itself unhealthy.

Similarly, the SWD is a mono-diet, it's characterized by the exclusive consumption of UPS, which have a very homogenous composition of wheat flour, seed oils, sugar, animal byproducts, salt, etc. So we shouldn't hold a double standard with UPFs.

If one suggests that fruit correlates with positive health outcomes from the lowest to highest intakes in epidemiology, but UPFs correlate with negative health outcomes from the lowest to highest intakes, then we aren't comparing like with like.

In epidemiology looking at UPFs and health, the highest quantiles of intake with be representative of people eating almost exclusively UPFs, basically a mono-diet. But in the epidemiology looking at fruit and health, the upper quantiles of intake will almost never be representative of that.

Imagine we did get people to only eat fruit for decades, they would end up terribly and likely worse off than the UPF only diet.

All in all, diet-health relationships are substitution effects. Every time you add something to your diet, you're removing something else. If one is adding a single food item/category to the exclusion of everything else that could be giving extra benefits, then we'd expect worse health outcomes. So it doesn't matter if the food is healthy or unhealthy.

So there doesn't seem to be any robust evidence that UPFs are inherently harmful.

### Feeding trials

Controlled trials can test processing more directly than diet patterns can. In 20 adults on a metabolic ward, meals matched for presented calories, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fiber led to about 508 kcal a day more intake on the ultra-processed diet. People gained 0.9 kg in 2 weeks on it and lost 0.9 kg on the unprocessed diet.
[Hall 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/)

In 55 adults on two 8-week diets that both followed UK guidelines, weight fell 2.06% on minimally processed food and 1.05% on ultra-processed food.
[Dicken 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-025-03842-0)

These are short trials of calories and weight, so they don't show UPFs are toxic. They do show that processing can change how much people eat even when nutrients are similar.

There are actually some cases where the processing of foods enhances their healthfulness and quality overall.

First example is whey protein isolate. This is a popular protein supplement used to help reach protein requirements and it's helpful because it's high in leucine which is the amino acid responsible for increasing MPS and it is easy to consume and digest due to the processing it goes through. This ease of consumption usually is problematic with foods but here we can use it to our advantage. It's been shown to help lean body mass and resist losses in lean body mass in elderly adults as they often find it difficult to get in adequate protein due to reduced appetite and difficulty chewing, but the processing helps us with this problem.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/22338070
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30289425

Yeast extract is another very processed food that can come from byproducts of beer fermentation. As a liquid, it is salty and a pretty nasty condiment called Marmite/Vegemite. A similar product known as nutritional yeast is also available as dry flakes that can taste like fish food. Researchers have looked at the effects of supplementing some of these products in humans and found marmite has been shown to affect the balance of excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters in the brain and can have clinical benefits for epilepsy. Brewer's yeast, which is the same species used to make nutritional yeast, has also been shown to reduce blood pressure and improve blood lipids in humans with type II diabetes.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28376309
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3744257/

Fruit juices have also been studied many times for being health promoting. Orange juice can improve endothelial function, blood lipids, and inflammatory markers in humans. Carrot juice can reduce lipid peroxidation cascades. And V8, which is a type of tomato based vegetable juice, has the potential to lower blood pressure in pre-hypertensive adults. There are also a lot of similar findings in beet, pomegranate, and grape juice.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4045306/
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3192732/
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2949782/
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5372571/

Some people claim processed foods make us fat, but is this true?

Firstly, processed foods taste very good so when we eat processed foods instead of whole foods, we tend to eat more. The feeding efficiency of calorie dense processed junk food is very high that getting satiating requires more calories usually.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/31105044

Processed foods are also very easy to digest and this impacts our energy expenditure. It takes double the calories to digest and metabolize a whole food meal compared to a processed food meal. So even when calories are equated, a processed food meal is more likely to cause weight gain than a meal of whole foods.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2897733/

Processed foods also usually lack protein which has an independent effect on increasing our energy expenditure and lean body mass. Our lean tissue is a large energy sink and creates a buffer for excess calories. Chronically under consuming protein can reduce our lean body mass and lead to lower energy expenditures over time which can exacerbate the previous points which can lead to a negative feedback loop.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3777747/
