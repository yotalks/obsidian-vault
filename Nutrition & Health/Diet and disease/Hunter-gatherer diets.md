> [!summary] Bottom line
> Hunter-gatherers don't show that an ancestral diet is best. The Hadza weren't mostly meat eaters, and elderly survivors look healthy partly because close to half of children never reached adulthood. Having evolved eating a food also doesn't mean it can't harm long-term health. People who survived childhood could reach about 72, but Americans die later on the same measure.

## The Hadza

The Hadza of Tanzania are often cited as meat eaters, but they didn't eat mostly meat. Their diet falls into five main categories: tubers, berries, meat, baobab and honey. Honey was their most preferred food and tubers their least; tubers act as a fallback food, eaten more when berries are scarce. And what hunter-gatherers ate doesn't settle what's healthiest anyway.
[Marlowe 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19350623/)

## Survivorship bias

Many primal dieters claim that ancestral diets optimally promote the long term health of humans. There are usually two main justifications for this, the fact that they appear to be robustly healthy, and that humans evolved eating these types of diets. While these are technically statements of fact, we should remember correlation doesn't equal causation and their reasoning is flawed.

Would elderly people within a population appear more or less healthy if a population never had access to modern medical technology? They would actually probably appear healthier.

If a population never had access to modern medical technology, one of the things you can't do reliably is save sick or injured children. No matter what culture you look at, a large portion of children will get infectious diseases. These illnesses like bacterial or viral infections, are usually mundane by our current standards, but without modern medicine, many children who developed these types of illnesses would likely not survive.

Those who are more prone to illness likely do not have the same chances of survival as those who are less prone to illness. So without medical intervention, the environment is selecting for the fittest possible individuals of a population. This would likely create the appearance of more robust health in the elderly. If you weed out all the weaker people, of course the remaining population is going to appear stronger.

This creates a massive problem for primal dieters who choose to cite the health of these populations as a justification for their diet, or as a justification for recommending their diet to others. They seem to be aware of the environmental adversity faced by these populations, but not that this creates a massive selection bias problem for their narrative.

Let's say you show that the average life expectancy of these populations was around 30 years, they will immediately say that those estimates are confounded by infant and child mortality. But they can't have it both ways. Indeed, infant and child mortality was incredibly high in these populations.
![[Pasted image 20261105190914.png]]
[Volk 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513812001237)

Around 26.8% of infants and 48.8% of prepubescent children die in these populations. With such a high proportion of children dying, the apparent health of the elderly people would be colored by this. They likely appear healthier because the least resilient members of those populations are already dead. So you can't claim that these populations had high rates of infant and child mortality without also admitting that the resulting population is a very biased sample.

This is called survivorship bias, which is a type of selection bias that can occur when those who did not survive a selection event or process are overlooked in favor for those that did survive. Let's say for example, we wanted to construct better body armor for soldiers to wear in combat. We might conduct a study for the soldiers that returned from combat and collect the bullet wound distribution pattern data to help find where soldiers are most likely to get shot.
![[Pasted image 20261105191330.png]]

But as you can tell, this would exclude all those who shot and didn't survive. It would also overlook the bullet wound distribution patterns that tended to lead to death, which would give us a way better picture for how we should make the best body armor.

Likewise, when primal dieters claim we should eat like our ancestors because they were robustly healthy, they're not appreciating survivorship bias is confounding their appraisal of hunter gatherer health. They're overlooking the other 50% of the population that didn't survive.

Because of this, it's not a good idea to use the apparent good health of these populations as the basis of assumption that their diets are good for modern humans. Until they figure out a way to explain why survivorship bias is not going to be confounding in an evaluation of their health, they can't rely on their apparent good health to determine the applicability of their diets to modern humans. The degree to which their diets are appropriate to modern humans is unclear.

## Lifespan

Hunter-gatherers didn't all die young, but that doesn't show their way of life was better. Across small-scale hunter-gatherer and horticultural populations, the most common age of adult death was about 72, with a range of 68–78. That's the mode among adults, not an average lifespan, so it can only be compared with the same measure:

| Measure | Hunter-gatherers | US |
|---|---|---|
| **Life expectancy at birth** | 21–37 years | 79.0 years (2024) |
| **Most common adult age at death** | About 72 years | 85 years (2002) |

Sweden in 1751–59 also had a modal age of 72. On average, 57% of hunter-gatherer children reached age 15 and 36% reached 45. Illness caused 70% of deaths, violence and accidents 20%, and degenerative disease 9%.
[Gurven 2007](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/GurvenKaplan2007pdr.pdf)
[CDC 2025](https://cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db548.htm)

## Looking healthy and being healthy

Absence of diagnosed disease in a traditional population isn't absence of disease. The Masai are the clearest case: a 1962 survey of 400 men eating almost nothing but milk and meat found virtually no clinical or ECG sign of heart disease, and the finding has been cited ever since as evidence against the diet-heart hypothesis. Autopsies of 50 Masai eight years later found atheroma as widespread as in American men of the same age. Their coronary arteries had simply enlarged enough that the thickening didn't obstruct them.
[Mann 1964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14193818/)
[Mann 1972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5007361/)
→ [[Saturated fat#The Masai|The Masai]]

A clinical survey of living people in a population without medical imaging measures events, not disease. That's a weaker standard than it looks, and it compounds the selection problem above.

The Maasai participants in these studies were pastoralists. The example illustrates a measurement issue that also matters when reading reports about hunter-gatherers. Clinical exams and ECGs can miss plaque, while a sample of autopsies cannot establish the rate of cardiovascular events in the population.

## Evolved foods

Primal dieters also suggest since we evolved eating certain foods, it's absurd to believe any of those foods could pose a long-term health risk to the average person. This reasoning has been used to dismiss robust and well studies diet-disease relationships, such as saturated fat and CVD, red meat and cancer, or sodium and hypertension.
[Hooper 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32428300/)
[Etemadi 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28487287/)
[Mente 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27216139/)
[Olde Engberink 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28655835/)

→ [[Appeal to nature]]

This is a clear appeal to nature fallacy and can be dismissed on that basis alone. It is important to describe precisely why this line of reasoning fails. What we want to know is whether or not foods are necessarily beneficial (or neutral) for long term health just because we evolved eating them.

Firstly, we need to see how Darwinian natural selection works. It's the process by which random gene mutations are selected for by different environmental challenges. Some mutations are better at dealing with certain environmental challenges than other mutations. As a result, these more adaptive mutations increase an organism's chances of producing offspring. These organisms subsequently pass on these adaptive mutations to their offspring too.
![[Pasted image 20261105200710.png]]

Like here we see black mice are less likely to get eaten by the bird than tan mice. Because of this, the random mutations that produces black mice rather than tan mice end up being naturally selected for by the environment. But the environmental challenges like getting eaten aren't quite the same as environmental challenges that affect the long-term health of an organism.
