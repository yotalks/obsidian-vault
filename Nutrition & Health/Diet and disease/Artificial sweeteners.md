> [!summary] Bottom line
> In large, long-running cohorts, artificially sweetened drinks aren't linked to diabetes or heart disease. In trials they don't change insulin sensitivity, glucose, insulin, cholesterol or body weight. Replacing sugary drinks with them leads to slightly lower weight, about as well as water does. A widely reported link to stroke and dementia came from a small cohort with many unadjusted analyses and likely reverse causation. Aspartame is well below safe intake limits at current consumption.

## Cohort studies

- **Diabetes:** in the Black Women's Health Study (43,960 women), sugar-sweetened soft drinks and fruit drinks were linked to more type 2 diabetes. One or more diet soft drinks a day wasn't (rate ratio 1.06, 95% CI 0.83–1.36). [Palmer 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2708080/)
- **Heart disease:** in the Nurses' Health Study (88,520 women followed for 24 years), 2 or more sugar-sweetened drinks a day were linked to 35% higher [[Glossary#CHD|coronary heart disease]] risk. Artificially sweetened drinks weren't linked. [Fung 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2667454/)

## Stroke and dementia

In the Framingham Offspring cohort, drinking 1 or more artificially sweetened soft drinks a day was linked to about 3 times the risk of ischemic stroke (HR 2.96, 95% CI 1.26–6.97) and Alzheimer's dementia (2.89, 1.18–7.07). Sugar-sweetened drinks weren't linked to either.
[Pase 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5405737/)

This result is weak for several reasons:
- **Few cases.** There were only 97 strokes and 81 dementia cases, hence the wide confidence intervals.
- **Reverse causation.** Diabetes was more common in daily diet soda drinkers. Once prevalent diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypertension and waist-to-hip ratio were adjusted for, the dementia links were no longer significant. The authors said they couldn't tell whether diet drinks raised dementia risk through diabetes, or whether people with diabetes simply drink more diet soda.
- **No correction for multiple comparisons.** The authors ran many analyses: 3 beverage types, recent and cumulative intake, 4 outcomes, and several models. They said they didn't adjust for [[Multiple comparisons|multiple comparisons]], so some findings may be due to chance. A letter in response counted 159 analyses. With that many, a Bonferroni correction would require P below about 0.0003, and none of the results reached that. [Fenton 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28784923/)

## Trials

- **Insulin sensitivity:** in a 12-week double-blind crossover trial (50 nondiabetic adults), 2 cans a day of a drink sweetened with aspartame and acesulfame K didn't change insulin sensitivity or insulin secretion compared with an unsweetened carbonated drink. Body weight, self-reported diet and self-reported physical activity didn't change either. [Bonnet 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29982723/)
- **Aspartame and metabolic markers:** a meta-analysis of trials found aspartame didn't change fasting glucose, insulin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, body weight or energy intake compared with control or sucrose. [Santos 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28394643/)
- **Replacing sugary drinks:** in a meta-analysis of 17 trials (1,733 adults with overweight or obesity), replacing sugar-sweetened drinks with low- or no-calorie sweetened drinks led to slightly lower body weight (−1.06 kg), BMI and body fat. Water instead of sugary drinks showed no significant differences, though most outcomes favored it, and the direction of benefit was similar to the sweetened drinks. Several authors have received fees from sweetener industry groups. [McGlynn 2022](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2790045)

## Glucose, insulin and appetite

In a crossover study of 30 healthy men, drinks sweetened with aspartame, monk fruit or stevia caused smaller glucose and insulin spikes than a sucrose drink in the first hour. But the men ate more at lunch afterwards, which fully made up for the calories saved, so total daily energy intake didn't differ. Glucose and insulin were then higher after lunch, so total glucose and insulin over 3 hours were the same for all four drinks.
[Tey 2017a](https://www.nature.com/articles/ijo2016225)

In 10 healthy men using continuous glucose monitors, 24-hour glucose didn't differ between aspartame, monk fruit, stevia and sucrose drinks.
[Tey 2017b](https://www.nature.com/articles/ejcn201737)

These are single-drink studies, so they don't show what happens with regular use. The longer trials above found no change in body weight or energy intake.

## Aspartame safety

Even high consumers of aspartame stay well below the acceptable daily intakes set by the FDA (50 mg/kg a day) and EFSA (40 mg/kg a day). Animal studies found no adverse effects at doses up to at least 4,000 mg/kg a day, and a review of the carcinogenicity, neurotoxicity and epidemiological studies found no credible evidence that aspartame causes cancer or affects the nervous system.
[Magnuson 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17828671/)

The review was funded by Ajinomoto, a maker of aspartame. The authors say the panel didn't know who the sponsor was.
[ScienceDaily 2007](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/09/070914183700.htm)
