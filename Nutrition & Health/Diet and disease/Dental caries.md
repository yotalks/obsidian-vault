> [!summary] Bottom line
> Cavities come from oral bacteria fermenting dietary sugars into acid that dissolves enamel, and about 2.5 billion people have untreated decay in permanent teeth. Fluoride toothpaste has strong trial evidence and the benefit rises with concentration, while flossing's evidence is weak. A genetically modified *S. mutans* strain that can't produce lactic acid, BCS3-L1, is now sold direct to consumers, but its published evidence stops at rodent studies from 2000 and no controlled human efficacy trial has been published.

## Mechanism

*Streptococcus mutans* and related oral bacteria ferment dietary sugars into lactic acid. The acid demineralizes enamel, and repeated cycles of demineralization outpacing remineralization produce a cavity. This is why the disease tracks fermentable carbohydrate intake rather than sugar's systemic effects.

## Burden

Untreated caries in permanent teeth affected roughly 2.5 billion people in 2019, making it one of the most prevalent conditions measured by the Global Burden of Disease study. Prevalence fell only slightly between 1990 and 2019 despite large increases in dental care spending.
[Heliyon 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2022.e10714)

## Transmission

Oral bacteria are shared between people, but the pattern isn't what's usually claimed. Across more than 9,700 metagenomes with strain-level profiling, mother-to-infant transmission was substantial for the **gut** microbiome (around 50% strain-sharing during infancy). Transmission of the **oral** microbiome was largely horizontal instead, driven by cohabitation: median strain-sharing was 32% between people living together, and time since cohabitation predicted sharing better than age or genetics did.
[Valles-Colomer 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36653448/)

So caries-associated bacteria do spread between people, but the evidence points at household contact generally rather than maternal transmission specifically.

## What the trial evidence supports

**Fluoride toothpaste.** Strong and dose-dependent. The Cochrane review of fluoride toothpastes at different concentrations found greater caries prevention at higher concentrations.
[Cochrane 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007868.pub3)

**Flossing.** Weak. The Cochrane review found only very low-quality evidence for flossing in addition to brushing, with no evidence of an effect on caries specifically.
[Cochrane 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008829.pub2)

The asymmetry matters because flossing occupies far more space in dental advice than its evidence supports, while fluoride concentration — the thing with good evidence — gets less attention.

## Replacement therapy

The idea is to displace acid-producing *S. mutans* with a strain that colonizes the same niche but can't make lactic acid, giving a permanent change after one application rather than daily maintenance.

BCS3-L1 was built by deleting the lactate dehydrogenase gene from a supercolonizing *S. mutans* strain and substituting an alcohol dehydrogenase gene from *Zymomonas mobilis* to handle the resulting metabolic imbalance. It produced no detectable lactic acid, was significantly less cariogenic than its parent strain in both germ-free and conventional rodent models, colonized rat teeth as well as the parent strain, and showed no organ abnormalities over 6 months. No acid-producing revertants appeared.
[Hillman 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10639415/)

That study is in rodents. The only other indexed publication on BCS3-L1 is a review by the same author.
[Hillman 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12369203/)

### State of the evidence

A PubMed search for clinical trials of *S. mutans* replacement therapy for caries returns nothing. What exists is:

| Evidence | Status |
|---|---|
| Lactic acid production abolished in vitro | Shown |
| Reduced cariogenicity in rodents | Shown |
| Colonization and genetic stability in rodents | Shown |
| Safety in humans | Small early colonization studies, single digits |
| Caries reduction in humans | No published controlled trial |

The strain is sold to consumers as Lumina at around $250. The gap between the rodent evidence and the marketing claim is the whole question, and it hasn't been closed in the published literature.
