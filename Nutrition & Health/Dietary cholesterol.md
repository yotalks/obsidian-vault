> [!summary] Bottom line
> Dietary cholesterol raises blood cholesterol along a curve that flattens out: going from 0 to 300 mg/day raises LDL-C by about 10 mg/dL, while 300 to 500 mg/day barely changes it. Because LDL causes heart disease, dietary cholesterol still raises risk, even though studies of typical intakes struggle to show it.

## A dose response that flattens out

[[Glossary#DC|DC]] intakes that go from average, around 300mg/day, to 500mg/day, would have a negligible effect on total cholesterol levels because its effects exist on a hyperbolic curve.
![[Dietary cholesterol hyperbolic dose response.png]]
[Hopkins 1992](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/1534437)

So, when we study the average population, we are of course not going to notice much on effects on [[Glossary#CVD|CVD]] endpoints.
[Berger 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26109578)

This graph as you can see, again, show no significant difference going from around 300mg/day to 500mg/day in [[Glossary#LDL|LDL-C]]. But going from 0mg/day to 300mg/day increases LDL-C by almost 10mg/dL which is meaningful. DC also does basically nothing to [[Glossary#HDL|HDL-C]].
![[Dietary cholesterol LDL-C meta-regression.png]]
[Vincent 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30596814)

## Why it still raises heart disease risk

Since LDL causally associates with heart disease in a linear and proportional manner and all exposures that durably increase LDL over time produce likewise increases in heart disease risk and dietary cholesterol is one of those exposures, it seems that dietary cholesterol increases risk for heart disease.
