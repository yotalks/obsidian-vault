> [!summary] Bottom line
> Ornish's trials found better ejection fraction, perfusion, stenosis flow reserve and endothelial function in the lifestyle groups than in controls. None of it shows reversal. Ejection fraction improves with yoga-type exercise alone. The perfusion gains were significant only against a worsening control group, not from baseline. Stenosis flow reserve is calculated from angiogram dimensions rather than measured. And plant-based diets haven't been shown to improve endothelial function from baseline either.

## Ejection fraction

In Ornish's first randomized trial, 23 people with ischemic heart disease did 24 days of stress management training and dietary changes, compared with 23 controls. The experimental group had a net change of +6.4% in left ventricular ejection fraction from rest to maximum exercise, a 44% increase in exercise duration, a 20.5% fall in cholesterol and a 91% fall in angina episodes.
[Ornish 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6336794/)

The stress management included stretching and breathing exercises, which aren't aerobic, and yoga alone improves ejection fraction by similar or larger amounts:
- In 130 people with heart failure, adding 12 weeks of yoga to standard therapy raised ejection fraction 36.9%, versus 16.9% in controls. [Krishna 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24737932/)
- In 250 men after bypass surgery, yoga-based rehabilitation improved ejection fraction more than physiotherapy over a year in those with a low baseline ejection fraction. [Raghuram 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25443601/)

So whether the plant-based diet contributed to any improvement in ejection fraction remains to be shown. Ejection fraction can also rise from physiologic hypertrophy of healthy heart muscle, without any change in the diseased arteries.

## Perfusion

The 5-year PET study measured three things on the scans:
- **Lowest quadrant average:** the average activity in the quadrant of the heart with the most severe perfusion defect, as a percentage of the maximum. It measures how severe the defect is.
- **Percentage outside 2.5 SDs:** how much of the heart had activity outside 2.5 standard deviations of 20 disease-free people. It measures the defect's size.
- **Percentage below 60% of maximum:** how much of the heart had activity under 60% of the maximum, about 3 SDs below the normal mean. It combines size and severity.

All three improved significantly in the lifestyle group compared with controls. But the significance came from the difference between groups, and controls got worse. Within the lifestyle group, the changes (mean ± SE) were −5.1% ± 4.8% for the size measure, −4.2% ± 3.8% for the combined measure, and +4.9% ± 3.3% for the lowest quadrant. Each is less than 1.5 standard errors from zero, so none shows a significant improvement from baseline.
[Gould 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7674504/)

Reversal means blood flow that was falling has actually increased. Preventing the decline that would have happened without the intervention is a different claim. If controls lose 20% of blood flow and the experimental group gains 0.001%, the difference between groups could be significant without anyone's blood flow meaningfully improving. In a parallel trial, showing improvement needs both:
1. a significant change from baseline to follow-up within the experimental group, and
2. a significant difference in that change between the experimental and control groups.

The same applies to ejection fraction measured against a control group.

A German trial of intensive exercise and a low-fat diet reported both. Stress-induced ischemia on thallium scans fell, but the improvement wasn't limited to patients whose lesions regressed, so perfusion can improve independently of regression.
[Schuler 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1729343/)

## Stenosis flow reserve

An earlier analysis of the trial's angiograms reported worsening stenosis flow reserve in controls and improvement in the lifestyle group.
[Gould 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1550011/)

Stenosis flow reserve isn't a measurement of blood flow. It's a theoretical pressure drop across a stenosis, calculated from the angiogram's dimensions (minimum lumen diameter, reference diameter and lesion length) by assuming a resting flow velocity and adding up laminar and turbulent resistance. Fractional flow reserve, which measures the actual pressure drop with a wire, is the gold standard. Stenosis flow reserve predicts a significant fractional flow reserve only modestly, with a sensitivity of 56% and specificity of 81%.
[Potter 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28164013/)

The best correlation between angiographic stenosis flow reserve and myocardial fractional flow reserve was r = 0.78, when measurements from before and after angioplasty were pooled. Even then, fractional flow reserve varied widely for a given degree of stenosis in individual patients.
[Bartúnek 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7608431/)

Correlations at a single time point tend to drop sharply for changes over time, as with angiography versus ultrasound (r = 0.65 at one time point but 0.14 for changes, see [[Plant based diet CVD reversal]]). When the correlation for changes is close to zero, an impressive improvement in stenosis flow reserve could even come alongside worsening blood flow.

Because percent stenosis and stenosis flow reserve are both calculated against a reference segment, a narrowing reference segment makes the lesion look less severe even if the lesion hasn't changed. This is pseudoregression.

## Endothelial function

Three trials have tested plant-based diets on endothelial function outside the period right after a meal:
- **Lin 2020:** 52 people with peripheral arterial disease were randomized to a plant-based diet or no dietary advice for 4 months. Flow-mediated dilation improved more with the plant-based diet. [Lin 2020](https://ijdrp.org/index.php/ijdrp/article/view/53)
- **Shah 2018:** 100 people with coronary artery disease were randomized to a vegan or American Heart Association diet for 8 weeks. Endothelial function didn't change significantly in either group (reactive hyperemia index: vegan 1.96 to 1.88, AHA 2.12 to 1.84). The vegan diet lowered hsCRP 32% more than the AHA diet. [Shah 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30571591/)
- **Dod 2010:** 27 people on Ornish's program were compared with 20 historically matched controls, not randomized. Flow-mediated dilation rose from 4.23 to 4.65 mm while it fell in controls from 4.62 to 4.48 mm. Ornish was a co-author. [Dod 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20102949/)

Improving relative to a control group again isn't the same as improving from baseline. The best we can say with some confidence is that a plant-based diet can keep endothelial function from getting worse, not that it improves it.

Mediterranean diets did improve endothelial function compared with controls in a meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials (2,300 people): flow-mediated dilation rose 1.86% more, and hsCRP fell 0.98 mg/L more.
[Schwingshackl 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24787907/)
