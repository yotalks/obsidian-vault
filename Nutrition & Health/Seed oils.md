> [!summary] Bottom line
> Claims that seed oils cause disease rest on mechanistic and ecological research, not human outcomes. Linoleic acid makes LDL slightly easier to oxidize in lab tests, but antioxidants and overall diet matter far more, and oxidized LDL doesn't predict heart disease once ApoB is accounted for. On actual outcomes, replacing saturated fat with seed oils lowers heart disease (see Saturated fat). The cancer concern comes from a post-hoc analysis of one trial (LAVAT), where the excess disappears among people who stuck to the diet and tracks smoking; across trials the effect is null, and tissue linoleic acid and Mendelian randomization point to lower cancer and skin cancer risk.

## Mechanisms aren't outcomes

Negative attitudes to seed oils have gone crazy recently, despite this, almost all claims regarding negative health effects of seed oils are rooted in mechanistic or ecological research. Mechanistic studies includes studies like cell culture, animal studies, and in-vitro studies, or some short term human experiments. Ecological research just investigates largely unadjusted associations between population-level exposures and outcomes, such as the association between seed oil availability in the food supply and type 2 diabetes incidence.

These types of research can definitely be useful, but it's wrong to extrapolate from these types of research to population-level health effects. In the absence of corroborating human outcome data, it's very sus to make claims on what outcomes will happen based on speculation about intuitive mechanisms, especially when there's no high internal validity population-level outcome data that actually agrees with the mechanistic speculation to begin with.

Mechanistic studies tell us almost nothing about actual human disease risk.

## The LDL oxidation hypothesis

The primary mechanism that seed oils are suggested to increase the risk of [[Glossary#CVD|CVD]] is through the oxidation of [[Glossary#PUFA|PUFAs]], particularly [[Glossary#LA|LA]], in [[Glossary#LDL|LDL]]. This hypothesis seems to have started from this study where their case against seed oils starts with the claim that seed oils contain fatty acids, particularly LA, which increase the susceptibility of LDL to oxidize, and that CVD risk is mediated through the oxidation of LDL.
It's also suggested that [[Glossary#SFA|SFA]] are resistant to this sort of oxidative destruction, which implies that the replacement of SFA for LA in the diet may lower CVD risk. This hypothesis would have entailments that go against the common understanding of how these different fats influence CVD risk. 
[DiNicolantonio 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30364556/)
→ [[Saturated fat]]

## Fat type barely changes LDL oxidation

We have comprehensively looked at the effects of altering fatty acid composition in diet on LDL oxidation rates in humans. This is one of the best studies of its kind and found no statistically significant differences between the effects of high SFA diets and high PUFA diets on the lag time to LDL oxidation were observed.
![[LDL oxidation lag time by dietary fat.png]]
Diets higher in [[Glossary#MUFA|MUFA]] might actually increase lag time to LDL oxidation more than either SFA rich and PUFA rich diets. It would appear as though this effect could be explained due to the fact that MUFA might be better than SFA at replacing LA in LDL particles.
[Mata 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8911273/)

When comparing LDL that were enriched with PUFA to LDL that were enriched with MUFA, this study found a stepwise reduction in LDL lag time to oxidation with increasing PUFA, specifically LA. These results similarly show MUFA increases the lag time to LDL oxidation more than SFA or PUFA.
[Kratz 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11840183/)

## Antioxidants matter far more

It seems to be true that seed oil consumption when in isolation increases the susceptibility of LDL to oxidize. But, these changes are nothing compared to what antioxidants do.

The lag time to LDL oxidation with vitamin E supplementation was more than double that which was observed in the previous study (or almost any other random population from any other study). They found the lag time to LDL oxidation was very high in all groups after a run-in diet that had 1200mg/day of vitamin E for three months before randomization. Compared to the previous study, the LDL lag time was 150% higher.
[Reaven 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8148354/)

This lines up with other data showing that vitamin E content of LDL particles linearly increases the lag time to LDL oxidation.
[Esterbauer 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1985404/)

The effect of dosing vitamin E on increasing the lag time to LDL oxidation observed by the previous study was about 7.5x stronger than the effects observed between fatty compositions on diet. This strongly suggests that antioxidants are way more important if one wants to reduce susceptibility of LDL to oxidize.

All three studies used the same copper sulfate solution methodology to measure the lag time to oxidation, so since they reference the same source for the methods developed by this study, it wouldn't seem likely that the increase in LDL lag time to oxidation seen in vitamin E supplementation would be due to a difference in measurement methods.
[Esterbauer 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2722022/)

## Vitamin E and polyphenols in fat sources

Going back to the Kratz study, despite all diet groups had similar levels of vitamin E, the olive oil group had significantly higher vitamin E status than sunflower oil group. This is consistent with MUFA increasing lag time to LDL oxidation to a greater degree than SFA or PUFA. Which leads to higher vitamin E representation in the end.

It's well understood that higher PUFA intakes increases vitamin E requirements in humans.
[Raederstorff 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26291567/)

But the olive oil group started with much higher status to start, which likely contributed to that effect. The run-in diet was high in SFA. Such a diet is very likely going to be lower in vitamin E, which can exaggerate the effects of high PUFA diets.

SFA is not vulnerable to lipid peroxidation in any way similar to that of PUFA. But, SFA being less vulnerable to lipid peroxidation typically means that sources of SFA contain very low amounts of antioxidants.
[Herting 1963](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14100992/)

For example, both coconut oil and butter contain negligible vitamin E and minimal polyphenols. The potential importance of polyphenol antioxidants in protecting LDL from oxidation has been shown in research looking at olive oil.
They found virgin olive oil increases lag time to oxidation to a greater degree than that of olive oil, with the primary differences between these different olive oils being their polyphenol content. Many polyphenols act as antioxidants once they're inside our bodies, so this isn't surprising.
[Marrugat 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15168036/)

## Overall diet matters most

It's been repeatedly shown that the overall dietary pattern matters more than PUFA, or even LA, in altering the susceptibility of LDL to oxidation.
The Aronis trial, for example, had diabetic subjects placed on either a fast food diet that was formulated to be "Mediterranean diet-like" or assigned to follow their usual diet. There was also a third arm of non-diabetics placed on the Mediterranean-style fast food diet. Lag time to LDL oxidation was measured according to the same methodology as described above, with a copper sulfate solution.
The foods were specifically formulated to increase LDL lag time to oxidation. But, what makes the results impressive is that the fast food groups were having 2x as much PUFA than baseline. Despite this, they saw some of the longest lag time to LDL oxidation in the literature in that population that has not been primed with tons of vitamin E for 3 months.
[Hernáez 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28371298/)
[Fitó 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17563030/)
[Aronis 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17887946/)
[Kiokias 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30282925/)

Altogether, it seems diet quality matters more than PUFA, or LA, for LDL oxidation in aggregate. We see multiple times that low PUFA or low LA diets can be outperformed by high PUFA or LA diets of better overall quality.

## Lag-time tests may not reflect the body

Measuring lag time to LDL oxidation in this way is likely not reflective of normal physiological conditions. When you put the LDL particles in a copper sulfate solution, eventually all of the LDL will oxidize. The high-PUFA LDL will oxidize a bit faster, but this doesn't tell us what happens to these LDL particles under physiological conditions, so it's likely better to look at markers like [[Glossary#oxLDL|oxLDL]].

This can better tell us how many oxidized LDL are likely to form in the blood after a particular intervention or certain context. This is good because just looking at lag time to oxidation in vitro can exaggerate what will likely happen in vivo.

It may be the case that when you expose LDL particles to copper sulfate, they oxidize in under an hour, but in physiological conditions, that oxidation takes days. If LDL are cleared from the blood before oxidation can occur, then the copper sulfate tests don't tell us much at all.

## Butter vs. canola oil

One long crossover style RCT compared the effects of butter to canola oil on oxLDL and found overall that the butter diet resulted in higher LDL and higher oxLDL.
This is likely because SFA are a poor source of antioxidants or maybe because their baseline diets were higher in PUFA, and reducing seed oil intakes may cut off those sources of antioxidants and increases LDL oxidation.
[Palomäki 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21122147/)

## Does LDL oxidation even matter?

Even if we assume that high PUFA diets do increase LDL oxidation relative to high SFA diets, does this matter?
This study has found oxidized LDL are less likely to be retained within the arterial intima when compared to native LDL. If the LDL are oxidizing in the serum, maybe this just opens up more disposal pathways for LDL and lowers its chances of getting retained in the subendothelial space to begin with.
[Oörni 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9261142/)

While we have seen seed oil consumption does appear to have an independent impact on LDL oxidation (though tiny in comparison to antioxidants), it's also true that oxLDL isn't a robust risk factor for [[Glossary#CHD|CHD]]. This study saw the association doesn't survive adjustment for traditional risk factors like [[Glossary#ApoB|ApoB]] or [[Glossary#HDL|HDL-C]]. This means after accounting for those, risk is more tracking those factors and not likely tracking oxLDL at all. This suggests the hypothetical detriment of increased LDL oxidation aren't significant enough to offset the LDL lowering benefits of a high PUFA diet.
[Wu 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16949489/)
→ [[LDL and heart disease]]

## Is the 4E6 oxLDL assay valid?

The above study used the Mercodia 4E6 antibody assay to measure oxLDL. Some say it's invalid and making poor distinctions between native and oxLDL.
[van Tits 2005](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/01.CIR.0000164264.00913.6D)

If the 4E6 assay was truly making poor distinctions, the two biomarkers would basically tell us the same thing, and thus the results of the model would suggest extreme multicollinearity as indicated by similarly very wide confidence intervals for both results and likely not significant. But, there is no evidence of extreme multicollinearity in the results, so it is unlikely that the 4E6 antibody assay is actually making poor distinctions between oxLDL and native LDL. 

It's suggested to critics of 4E6 antibody assay that measures of oxidized phospholipids such as E06 antibody assay, are better measures of oxLDL than 4E6 antibody assay. But the E06 antibody assay is actually more vulnerable to this exact kind of confounding.

The 4E6 antibody assay makes a clear distinction between native LDL and oxLDL by only binding to ApoB that has been modified sufficiently, as to not be recognizable to LDL receptors. The 4E6 antibody only binds to ApoB particles that have over 60 of their lysine residues modified by aldehydes, which is the threshold for initiating binding affinity with scavenger receptors, and foreclosing binding affinity with LDL receptors. This makes it a great assay for distinguishing.
[Mercodia: oxidized LDL ELISA](https://www.mercodia.com/product/oxidized-ldl-elisa/)

Because lipid peroxidation of the LDL particle's phospholipid membrane is not required for an LDL particle to oxidize, a measurement like [[Glossary#oxPL|oxPL]] could easily mistake a minimally oxidized LDL particle as an oxLDL. Because of this, it's likely the 4E6 antibody assay is likely to better reflect the actual number of oxLDL.
[Mercodia poster: know what you measure](https://cms.mercodia.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/poster-oxldl-know-what-you-measure.pdf)

This is important because the immune cells that mediate the formation of atherosclerotic plaques only tend to take up maximally oxidized LDL particles, not minimally oxidized LDL particles. Maximally oxidized LDL have to be disposed of through scavenger receptor-mediated pathways, rather than LDL receptor-mediated pathways.
[Henriksen 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6838433/)
[Meyer 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24891335/)

If minimally oxidized LDL likely contribute as little to foam cell formation as native LDL, there's no reason to favor measures of minimally oxidized LDL such as E06 assay over measures of maximally oxidized LDL with 4E6.

We don't have data that looked at the relationship between oxLDL, as measured by the E06 antibody assay, and CHD outcomes after adjustment for total ApoB. The closest we have is a single study that found no association between oxPL/ApoB and ApoB.
[Tsimikas 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16750687/)

If ApoB and oxPL tend to vary in tandem, the oxPL/ApoB ratio might not be expected to change much subject to subject. We would then not expect oxPL/ApoB to correlate very well at all.

## Tissue linoleic acid and heart disease

If having more LA in your body means more lipoprotein oxidation and more CHD, we would not expect results of meta analyses that show the higher the tissue LA content, the lower the risk of both CHD and ischemic stroke. There wasn't a single statistically significant increase in risk observed in any of the included cohorts when comparing lowest tissue LA to highest though.
[Marklund 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30971107/)

This regression analysis of the Scottish Heart Health Extended Cohort found a strong inverse correlation between adipose LA and CHD incidence. This is not what is expected if LA causes oxLDL which causes CHD. We would expect SFA causes increasing LDL which increases risk of CHD.
[Wood 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6146032/)

## Heart disease outcomes

The outcome evidence (the saturated fat threshold, cohorts, the Cochrane trials, the Sydney and Minnesota trans-fat margarine trials, the Lyon Diet Heart Study and Mendelian randomization) is in [[Saturated fat#The threshold effect|Saturated fat]]. In short: replacing saturated fat with seed oils lowers heart disease risk.

## Cancer: the LAVAT trial

The data most often cited to support the notion that seed oils increase risk of cancer comes from a post-hoc analysis of the LAVAT study. This is likely because this is only one of the few RCTs that actually shows increase in risk.
[Pearce 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4100347/)
[Ederer 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21090384/)

The LAVAT was a double blind RCT that aimed to investigate effects of substituting seed oils with animal fat on risk of CVD. But, cancer was one of their secondary endpoints. The researchers made sure the only substitutions was the fat to the extent they even gave ice cream made of seed oils rather than dairy fat.

Now the concern that increasing seed oils increases cancer risk comes from figure 1 and clearly shows the cumulative deaths due to carcinoma rise faster in the seed oil group than in the control group after around 2 years in the study.
![[LAVAT cumulative carcinoma deaths.png]]

Firstly, cancer deaths were not a pre-specified primary endpoint of the trial itself, which means it's not clear this study was powered or equipped enough to investigate this endpoint rigorously. And the results were also not statistically significant, despite it looking like a big difference.
They also did an analysis where they adjusted for the differences in relative adherence between groups and after adjustment, the findings were consistent with what would be expected from random distribution. So, the excess cancer deaths seen in the seed oil group were likely due to chance and wrongly attributed to poor adherence. Because of this, the authors say that the high seed oil diet was not likely to be the cause of the increase in cancer mortality. They even specifically mention their findings were an outlier relative to breadth of literature. Also about 33% of excess cancer was occurring among the low adherers in the seed oil group, and when you remove those and compare the between group difference in carcinoma risk among the highest adherers, it nullifies the effect of the seed oil diet on cancer outcomes. 
![[LAVAT carcinoma by adherence.png]]

There are also other plausible explanations for the effect as well like the seed oil group had many more moderate smokers than the animal fat group. Moderate smokers were defined as those who had 0.5-1 packs of cigarettes a day and the observed cancer in the moderate smokers was 3x higher in the seed oil group which is where all the excess cancer risk was accumulating. 
![[LAVAT carcinoma by smoking.png]]

Which means the low adherers in the seed oil group were likely to be smokers and the smokers are likely to get more cancer.
[Lee 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22943444/)

So to say the results of this study show higher risk of cancer for those eating seed oils is just cherry-picking, misunderstanding, and misrepresenting the data as they didn't divulge an independent effect of seed oil intake on cancer mortality and looking at high adherers, there's no statistically significant differences in cancer. As the authors say, if the increase in cancer risk was actually due to seed oils in diet, we would see more cancer risk in high adherers, but we do not.

## Cancer across all trials and cohorts

There is insufficient evidence to say seed oils are an independent risk factor for cancer and the data, at worst, says it has neutral effects compared to high animal fat intakes. Three other trials that substituted seed oils for SFA also reported cancer outcomes.
When looking at all the studies, we see the effect of substituting seed oils for SFA on cancer is null, but the LAVAT study is still skewing it, so if we take out all regular smokers it pushes the results to a non significant decrease in cancer risk with substituting seed oils for SFA.
![[Cancer in PUFA-SFA substitution trials.png]]

Which is the effect that aligns with epidemiology on this. Like here, we see a strong inverse association between tissue LA representation and cancer risk overall.
[Li 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32020162/)

## Skin cancer: cohorts

There is also a meta-analysis that looked at the limited epidemiological research on seed oils and skin cancer. The results suggest higher intakes of PUFAs could also increase risk of skin cancer in prospective cohorts.
[Ruan 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31298947/)

Increasing PUFA intake seems to increase risk of squamous cell carcinoma, but a single study by Park et al. contributes 92.6% of the weight.
[Park 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29636341/)
![[PUFA intake and skin cancer cohorts.png]]

This could be because the study's adjustment model lacked several important covariates that could have plausibly changed the association. Like they adjusted for hair color instead of skin tone which is a problem because hair color doesn't sufficiently capture the differential effects of skin tone on skin cancer.
[Gogia 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23107311/)

In at least one of the included prospective cohorts that looked at the relationships between LA and skin cancer that included adjustments for skin color, their results were still null, which gives us doubt for the Park study findings.
[Ibiebele 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19462452/)

When tissue representation of LA was looked at in a separate cohort study, non-significant reduction in the risk of basal cell carcinoma can be observed. Also among those with previous skin cancers, higher tissue representation of LA was associated with a statistically significant reduction in risk of re-occurrence.
[Wallingford 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23885039/)

There is also a study that investigates the relationship between red blood cell cis-LA content and squamous cell carcinoma, and while it is a case control study that lacks the temporal component needed to establish causality, it is some of the only human outcome data on this and found the results null.
[Harris 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15824162/)

## Skin cancer: the LAVAT trial and Mendelian randomization

The LAVAT study also had skin cancer on one of their secondary endpoints. In table 2 of the post-hoc analysis, we see that in the seed oil group, there were 10 cases of skin cancer, where in the animal fat group, there were 21 which produces a statistically significant increase in skin cancer risk for the animal fat group, whether or not we exclude or include the post-diet period.

There was no statistically significant differences in cancer risk among heavy smokers, of which there were only 2 cases in the animal fat group, but there was a non-significant increase in cancer risk among moderate smokers, of which there were 19 cases in the seed oil group. This suggests the increase in skin cancer seen in the animal fat group is not due to heavy smoking as there were only 2 cases of cancer among heavy smokers in the animal fat group, and we have 21 cases of skin cancer. The seed oil group had lower rates of skin cancer despite the fact there were more moderate smokers and more cancer risk in moderate smokers in the seed oil group. So, this increase in risk seen in the animal fat group can't be explained by smoking habits.

MR has been used to look at the relationship between elevated plasma LA and skin cancer. Data was looking at two different keratinocyte cancers, which were basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, and the results were consistent with the LAVAT study.
[Seviiri 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34088753/)

There was a statistically significant 6% reduction in [[Glossary#BCC|BCC]] risk with genetically elevated plasma LA whereas AA showed a statistically significant 4% increase in risk. The effect size of [[Glossary#EPA|EPA]] was actually larger than AA which would be at odds with RCT data on the subject.
[Noel 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24265065/)

For [[Glossary#SCC|SCC]], the only statistically significant relationship between genetically elevated plasma PUFA was a 4% increased risk with plasma AA. The results for all other PUFA were null.

So all in all, some cohorts say higher PUFA may increase risk of skin cancer, but the results from the LAVAT and MR trials are stronger evidence and go in the opposite direction so it appears likely that increased seed oil consumption is not an independent risk factor for developing skin cancer, and could actually reduce risk.
