> [!summary] Bottom line
> Lifestyle programs built around a very low-fat plant-based diet improve the things that matter: fewer cardiac events, less angina and better blood flow to the heart. But none of the evidence shows they reverse atherosclerosis. Angiograms only show small changes in narrowing and can't measure plaque, and perfusion, symptoms and outcomes can all improve without any change in plaque. To date, there isn't a single case of supposed heart disease reversal from a plant based diet that is compatible with established definitions used in literature. The case is for going beyond reversal, not for reversal.

A whole food plant based diet can prevent CVD from getting worse, reduce CVD events, make you healthier, reduce risk of heart attacks, strokes, dying of heart disease, the things that actually matter and go beyond CVD reversal, but not reversal.

## What reversal would mean

Coronary heart disease (also called coronary artery disease or ischemic heart disease) develops when the arteries of the heart can't deliver enough oxygen-rich blood to the heart, often because of plaque buildup.
[NHLBI: Coronary heart disease](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health-topics/coronary-heart-disease)

Atherosclerotic coronary artery disease is the buildup or persistence of plaque in the coronary arteries. Reversal means a change to the opposite direction, so:
- **Reversing coronary heart disease** would need, at minimum, an improvement in the diseased arteries themselves that improves their ability to deliver blood to the heart, caused by the diet or lifestyle change.
- **Reversing atherosclerotic coronary artery disease** would need, at minimum, regression of plaque in the coronary arteries caused by the diet or lifestyle change.

## The evidence offered for reversal

Claims of reversal rest on six lines of evidence:
1. **Angiography:** in randomized trials, percent stenosis and minimum lumen diameter improved with lifestyle changes and worsened in controls, and more adherent patients improved more.
2. **Perfusion imaging:** blood flow to the heart muscle improved with lifestyle changes and worsened in controls.
3. **Outcomes:** fewer deaths, cardiovascular deaths and cardiac events.
4. **Symptoms:** fewer and milder angina episodes.
5. **Ejection fraction:** improvements with lifestyle changes, while controls stayed the same or worsened.
6. **Anecdotes:** impressive angiograms, testimonials and cases of symptom reversal.

None of these show reversal of atherosclerosis.

## Angiography

In the Lifestyle Heart Trial, 28 patients with coronary heart disease went on a low-fat vegetarian diet, stopped smoking, and did stress management training and moderate exercise; 20 got usual care. After 1 year, average artery narrowing fell slightly in the lifestyle group (from 40.0% to 37.8% stenosis) and worsened in the control group (42.7% to 46.1%). That's a small regression in a small trial, and it came from several lifestyle changes at once, not diet alone.
[Ornish 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1973470/)
→ [[Coronary plaque regression]]

After 5 years, stenosis in the lifestyle group fell 3.1 percentage points (from about 40%) while the control group's rose 11.8 points, and more adherent patients improved more. There were 25 cardiac events in the 28 lifestyle patients versus 45 in the 20 controls. The lifestyle program was a 10% fat whole foods vegetarian diet with exercise, stress management, smoking cessation and group support, without lipid-lowering drugs.
[Ornish 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9863851/)

In a single physician's practice, 11 patients who kept to a diet under 10% fat, plus cholesterol-lowering drugs, for an average of 5.5 years cut cholesterol below 150 mg/dL. Average stenosis fell from 53.4% to 46.2%, but minimum lumen diameter didn't change significantly (1.3 to 1.4 mm), and there was no control group.
[Esselstyn 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7500065/)

These changes are small, and angiography is a poor way to detect them. Angiography doesn't measure atherosclerosis at all: it shows the lumen, can't assess what a lesion is made of, and underestimates disease, especially in segments that look normal. Changes reported in regression trials are around 0.3 mm or 10%, and measurement variability is large enough that big samples are needed.
[Hong 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8042825/)

Compared with intravascular ultrasound, which images plaque directly, angiographic measures of the lumen correlate reasonably at a single time point (r = 0.65). But changes over time correlate only weakly: change in stenosis score versus change in percent atheroma volume had r = 0.14. Patients with angiographic progression did gain more plaque (9.13 vs 0.20 mm³).
[Berry 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17389269/)

That comparison used data from the A-PLUS trial, where average plaque volume barely changed in either group (0.7 mm³ with placebo, 4–8 mm³ with avasimibe, not significant). Average changes near zero don't mean individuals didn't change, though. Individual patients' plaque volumes still rose or fell, so if angiographic measures tracked plaque, they should have correlated with those individual changes.
[Tardif 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15533865/)

## Perfusion imaging

In the same trial's 5-year follow-up, PET scans showed smaller and less severe perfusion defects in the lifestyle group, while they grew in controls.
[Gould 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7674504/)
→ [[Lifestyle Heart Trial]]

Images like these are often shown as evidence of reversal: a PET scan where a perfusion defect cleared after 3 weeks of plant-based eating, and an angiogram where a diseased artery looked normal after 32 months without cholesterol-lowering drugs.
![[Esselstyn perfusion and angiogram figures.webp]]
[Esselstyn 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25198208/)

Myocardial perfusion imaging underdetermines plaque formation. Blood flow can improve through better endothelial function, less vascular tone, or other changes, without any plaque regression. In a randomized, double-blind trial of 45 people with coronary heart disease, which Ornish co-authored, 240 mL a day of pomegranate juice for 3 months reduced stress-induced ischemia on perfusion scans compared with a placebo drink. Nobody claims pomegranate juice reverses plaque in 3 months.
[Sumner 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16169367/)

## Outcomes and symptoms

In a follow-up of 198 patients with heart disease counseled to switch to a plant-based diet with no meat, fish, dairy or added oil, 177 stuck with it. Over an average of 3.7 years, the adherent group had one recurrent major cardiac event (a stroke), while 13 of the 21 non-adherent patients had adverse events. There was no randomized control group.
[Esselstyn 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25198208/)

Fewer events and less angina are what matter. But they can happen through many routes besides plaque regression, so they don't show that plaque shrank. The point in contention was never whether lifestyle changes improve what matters, only whether they reverse atherosclerosis. Anecdotes don't show plaque change either, or that any change came from the diet.

## Beyond reversal

The main conclusion isn't just that no diet or lifestyle intervention has been shown to reverse atherosclerotic coronary artery disease. It's that reversal isn't the important thing compared with cardiovascular events and deaths, which lifestyle changes do improve. So rather than claiming reversal, the claim should be "beyond reversal".
