> [!summary] Bottom line
> There's little reason to think grass-fed beef is healthier than grain-fed. Switching US beef to grass-finished would need roughly 145–475 million more acres of pasture, more than all the land now used for livestock feed. White Oak Pastures, a showcase regenerative farm, had a lower net carbon footprint than conventional farms once soil carbon was counted, but used 2.5 times as much land and still emitted more per kg than a Beyond Burger.

## Health

It's often claimed grass-fed beef is healthier than grain-fed, though it's unclear why. The only small reason could be that grain-finished beef has more fat, and so more saturated fat, even though the percentage of fat that's saturated is the same. But the people who promote grass-fed beef tend to believe saturated fat is healthy anyway.
[Van Elswyk 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0309174013004944#tf0025)

## Land use

One common proposal is to replace the corn and soy that go to feedlots with grazing. US cropland is used like this:

| Use | Acres | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Livestock feed | 127.4M | 32.5% |
| Food Americans eat | 77.3M | 19.75% |
| Grain and feed exports | 62.8M | 16% |
| Idle or fallow | 52M | 13.3% |
| Biofuel | 38.1M | 9.7% |
| Wheat exports | 21.5M | 5.5% |
| Non-food crops like cotton | 13.6M | 3.5% |
| **Total cropland** | **391.5M** | |

[Bloomberg 2018](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2018-us-land-use/)

Assuming cattle use the same amount of land at every stage of life (they don't; calves use less), shifting grain-finished cattle to grass-finishing would need this much extra pasture to keep current beef production:
- **Lower estimate:** 144.5M acres
- **Middle estimate:** 250.5M acres
- **Upper estimate:** 473.6M acres
→ [[Cattle production in the USA]]

All of these are more than the land used to grow livestock feed. Even converting all 90 million acres of corn and 85 million acres of soy to pasture wouldn't reach the 250 million extra acres needed.

That feed land also feeds other animals. US feed use in 2016 by animal, out of 236,275,155 tons in total:

| Animal | Tons | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Beef cattle | 74,673,636 | 31.6% |
| Broiler chickens | 56,283,241 | 23.8% |
| Pigs | 46,347,318 | 19.6% |
| Dairy cattle | 23,847,089 | 10.1% |
| Egg-laying hens | 16,353,189 | 6.92% |
| Turkeys | 9,889,124 | 4.18% |
| Horses | 8,016,528 | 3.39% |
| Aquaculture | 707,688 | 0.30% |
| Sheep | 157,342 | 0.07% |

![[US feed use by animal 2016.png]]
[AllAboutFeed 2018](https://www.allaboutfeed.net/Compound-Feed/Articles/2018/1/Insight-into-the-US-animal-feed-market-242428E/)

Assuming every ton of feed takes the same land, cattle feed uses about 53.1M acres. So even freeing up all of it, the middle estimate would still need 197.4M acres more, an extremely inefficient trade-off.

Some also say the roughly 52 million acres of idle or fallow land should be grazed to regenerate it. There's no evidence fallow land isn't needed to protect against erosion. And even if grazing it worked as claimed, it wouldn't free up enough land: we'd still be 145.4M acres short.

A national analysis reached similar conclusions.
[Hayek 2018](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aad401)

## White Oak Pastures

A peer-reviewed life-cycle analysis of White Oak Pastures, a multi-species regenerative farm, compared it with conventional (commodity) production. Before counting soil carbon, it emitted more greenhouse gases per kg of carcass weight. Its soils sequestered an average of 2.29 Mg of carbon per hectare per year over 20 years, and counting that cut its net emissions by 80%, to about 2.9 times lower (66% lower) than conventional farms.
![[White Oak Pastures emissions.png]]

But it needed 2.5 times as much land: about 1,500 hectares for its cattle and about 500 for its poultry and pork.
![[White Oak Pastures land use.png]]
[Rowntree 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2020.544984/full#F2)

Counted across the whole farm, it isn't a net carbon sink: net emissions were about 4 kg CO2e per kg of carcass weight. (If all the soil carbon were credited to the cattle alone, the beef would be a net sink of −4.4 kg.) That's still more than a Beyond Burger's 3.4 kg CO2e per kg of product, and the gap is bigger than it looks, since carcass weight includes bone and fat that aren't eaten.
[Heller 2018](https://css.umich.edu/sites/default/files/publication/CSS18-10.pdf)
