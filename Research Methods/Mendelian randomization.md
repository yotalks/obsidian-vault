> [!summary] Bottom line
> Mendelian randomization uses the random inheritance of gene variants as a natural experiment, so it can support causal claims in a way ordinary associations can't. You don't need to know a mechanism to infer causation: we routinely accept causes without one, and requiring one would rule out things nobody doubts. Anecdotes are much weaker, especially for diseases that take decades to appear.

## How it works

Alleles are sorted at random during meiosis, so people who inherit a variant that affects something like LDL aren't otherwise systematically different. Comparing outcomes between people with and without the variant works like a lifelong randomized trial of that exposure. That's why it's second to a randomized trial for causal inference, rather than being just another association.

PCSK9 is the classic example. Among 3,363 Black participants, 2.6% had PCSK9 nonsense mutations, which came with 28% lower LDL and 88% lower risk of coronary heart disease (HR 0.11, 95% CI 0.02–0.81). Among 9,524 white participants, 3.2% had a variant giving 15% lower LDL and 47% lower risk (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.32–0.79).
[Cohen 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16554528/)

## Causation without a mechanism

Mendelian randomization doesn't try to explain how something causes an outcome. It only addresses whether it does. Not knowing the mechanism doesn't undo that, for a few reasons:

- **We accept causes without mechanisms all the time.** Most people believe drinking bleach or taking cyanide can kill you without being able to explain how.
- **History shows it works.** Smoking was linked to lung cancer in case-control data before the mechanism was understood. [Doll 1950](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10063665/)
- **New harms get found before they're explained.** A rule requiring a mechanism would stop us acting on them until later.
- **Not knowing a mechanism doesn't mean there isn't one.** It only tells us what we haven't worked out.

The standard also has to be applied consistently. If you accept an association for one exposure without a mechanism, you can't demand one for another exposure unless you can name a relevant difference.

Funding doesn't settle it either. A review or study being funded by industry is a reason to look closer, but it doesn't show the result is wrong, and the same goes for studies funded by the meat, egg or dairy industries.

## Anecdotes

Before arguing over evidence, it helps to ask what evidence would change someone's mind. If the answer is only their own experience, then no study could matter, and that standard falls apart quickly. On it, they'd have to ignore a perfect trial in billions of their own clones, couldn't say smoking is bad if they'd never smoked, and would have to call smoking 10 packs a day healthy if their blood work improved. Saying the rule only applies to food is a double standard.

An anecdote can't establish that a diet is better or worse:

- **Diseases with long latency:** Feeling good on a diet says little about something that takes decades to develop, which is why outcome studies are needed.
- **Feeling good isn't being healthy:** Heroin feels great. A disease with no symptoms until it's advanced can't be judged by how you feel.
- **Before isn't because:** Something happening before an improvement doesn't mean it caused it.
- **Any diet has them:** If anecdotes count, they can support every diet, so they can't separate diets.
- **Other explanations:** Someone could be following a diet poorly, or a change could have a more common cause. A falling GFR, for example, is more often a matter of hydration than something rare, and if a test improves after stopping a food, that doesn't show the food was the cause.
