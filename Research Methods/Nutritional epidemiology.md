> [!summary] Bottom line
> Dismissing "correlational studies" doesn't work, because all scientific findings are correlational in the end, trials included. And in nutrition, meta-analyses of cohort studies agree closely with meta-analyses of randomized trials when both look at diet rather than supplements, so anyone who trusts RCTs has reason to trust nutritional epidemiology too. That agreement is on average, though: individual cohort-trial pairs disagree a fair amount of the time, and a pooled ratio near 1 can partly come from many small, null results. So RCTs still outrank cohort studies when both exist, but cohort studies aren't uninformative when there are no trials.

## All findings are correlational

Those that say we cannot use "correlational studies" are confused, because as per Duhem-Quine's thesis, all scientific findings are correlational. The state of being "correlational" isn't a differential property between any modes of scientific investigation. Like intervention studies are just a form of observational study because you're just observing what happens when you intervene.
[Wikipedia: Duhem–Quine thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duhem%E2%80%93Quine_thesis)

## Cohort studies agree with RCTs

This enormous meta-analysis that compared the results of 950 nutritional RCTs to results from 750 prospective cohort studies. The results in aggregate from nutritional epidemiology are consistent with results from nutritional RCTs around 92% of the time when comparing intakes to intakes (without supplements).
[Schwingshackl 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34526355/)

The study matched 97 diet-disease pairs, built on more than 950 trials and 750 cohort studies. Across all binary outcomes, trial results were on average a little closer to null than cohort results (ratio of risk ratios 1.09, 1.04–1.14), with wide prediction intervals. Most of the differences came from comparing different exposures, especially supplements in trials against blood nutrient levels in cohorts (1.29, 1.17–1.42). When both looked at dietary intake, the results were nearly identical (0.98, 0.93–1.04) with almost no heterogeneity (I² = 4%). The authors are members of the GRADE working group.

Across 23 comparisons of meta-analytically summated findings from both bodies of evidence, only 2 comparisons showed discordance. This means nutritional epidemiology tends to replicate in RCTs in the supermajority of cases. If not RCTs, I don't know what one would use as a goalpost for high evidential quality, and given that, the current evidence suggests nutritional epidemiology is highly validated methodology if RCTs are used as the standard. If one places high credence in RCTs, it's unclear why they wouldn't also place high credence in nutritional epidemiology.

## Agreement between individual studies

A replication study of 82 pairs of RCT and cohort evidence from 51 nutrition systematic reviews found the same thing: on average, the two gave similar results (ratio of risk ratios 1.04, 0.99–1.10). The prediction interval was wide (0.77–1.41), and most disagreement came from the trials and cohorts not asking the same question, especially comparing different types of intake or exposure.
[Stadelmaier 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38177572/)

The same group then matched 64 individual nutrition RCTs to individual cohort studies by population, intervention/exposure, comparator and outcome (PI/ECO). On average the effect estimates agreed (ratio of risk ratios 1.00, 0.91–1.10, across 54 pairs with binary outcomes). Judged by direction of effect and confidence intervals, 53 of 64 pairs agreed and 11 disagreed. Most cohort studies had "some concerns" (47%) or a "high risk of bias" (48%), mainly from inadequate control of confounding, and risk of bias explained more of the disagreement than how closely the pairs were matched.
[Stadelmaier 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39838444/)
[Stadelmaier 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41957603/)

## Is the agreement just chance?

A pooled ratio of risk ratios near 1 only shows that cohorts don't systematically overestimate or underestimate effects compared to trials. It doesn't show that each cohort got the same answer as its matching trial.

A reanalysis of the 54 binary pairs above randomly reshuffled which cohort was paired with which trial, 10,000 times. The shuffled pairs gave a pooled ratio near 1 almost every time (99.96% fell inside the original confidence interval), and the real pairing wasn't distinguishable from random pairing (p = 0.12). Proper matching didn't make individual pairs agree more closely than random pairs did (p = 0.21). A precision-weighted measure did favor the real pairs (p = 0.008), but 4 pairs carried 55% of the weight. Using a stricter definition of agreement from the authors' earlier work (both significantly positive, both significantly negative, or both with confidence intervals between 0.85 and 1.15), only 7 of 54 pairs (13%) agreed. The reanalysis argues this happens because nutrition effects are small and estimates are imprecise, so averaging many near-null results lands near 1 regardless.
[Calkins 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41957646/)

The original authors replied that they never treated the pooled ratio as proof of agreement on its own. Their conclusion also rested on 53 of 64 pairs agreeing by direction and confidence interval, and on disagreement being more likely when pairs were less similar, came from different countries, had different follow-up lengths or had different risk of bias. They agreed that PI/ECO matching alone doesn't capture everything that matters for comparing study types, and that showing shuffled pairs average to 1 is neither surprising nor a sign that the paired results are invalid.
[Stadelmaier 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41957603/)

## Outside nutrition

In medicine more broadly, observational studies and trials also tend to agree on average:
- Across 136 reports on 19 treatments from 1985 to 1998, observational and randomized estimates were usually similar, and only 2 of 19 pooled observational effects fell outside the trials' 95% confidence interval. [Benson 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10861324/)
- A Cochrane review of 34 methodological reviews (2,869 RCTs and 3,924 observational studies) found no or only a very small difference between the two (ratio of ratios 1.08, 1.01–1.15), with 23 of 34 reviews in agreement on average. The certainty of this evidence was rated low. [Toews 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38174786/)

Agreement is far from guaranteed in individual reviews, though. In 220 clinical systematic reviews that pooled RCTs with non-randomized studies, 35.5% had estimates that disagreed qualitatively, and adding the non-randomized studies changed the direction of the RCT result in 36%.
[Mei 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39623370/)

## What agreement tells us

There are two separate claims here:
1. Observational studies can stand in for RCTs.
2. Observational studies carry some causal evidence even without RCTs.

The first doesn't follow from any of this: if an RCT and an equally well-done cohort study disagree, the RCT should win. The second can be true even if cohorts and trials only agree around two-thirds of the time. If agreement were no better than chance, a cohort result would tell us nothing about what a trial would find, but any agreement above chance shifts the odds. So when there are no RCTs, well-done cohort studies are the best evidence available, with a real chance the trials will disagree once they're done.

How far above chance nutrition cohorts get is the open question. Nutrition trials tend to have small effects, wide confidence intervals and many null results, which makes agreement by chance more likely and the true signal harder to see than in drug research. Since risk of bias explained more of the disagreement than how closely studies were matched, cohorts with better control of confounding may agree more closely.
