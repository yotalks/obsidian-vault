> [!summary] Bottom line
> The confidence needed before recommending something isn't fixed. It scales with how many people will act on the recommendation without examining it, and with who carries the cost if it's wrong. Being transparent about low confidence is a separate question from whether the action is worth taking, so transparency neither licenses nor condemns it. The cost side includes the money, time, effort and risk of obtaining the thing, measured against what the person would realistically have done instead rather than against the best thing they could have done.

## Three things to keep apart

A point estimate, the confidence attached to it, and the decision are different objects. Most decisions get made without a real point estimate at all, on something closer to an intuition about where the estimate would land and how vague that is. So "we don't know" doesn't discharge the decision, because some action, including inaction, still gets taken.

## The threshold scales with the role

An institution that represents scientific consensus, or a clinical protocol, needs a high level of confidence. Not because of credentials, but because its outputs get acted on as soundbites with little questioning, and because a protocol determines what everyone treating a patient does. The appropriate standard is a position the field would barely disagree with.

Someone deciding for themselves faces a subjective threshold, and the latitude is wide. How much confidence is enough before acting is a value judgment, not a fact, so two people can take the same evidence and reasonably act differently on it.

The asymmetry is about how many people will act on the claim unexamined, so it tracks reach and reliance rather than expertise.

## Transparency is a separate axis

Disclosing low confidence doesn't make a harmful action acceptable, and it doesn't make a neutral action harmful. A clear disclosure attached to something damaging is still damaging; an honest "this is my best guess" attached to something cheap and safe isn't made wrong by the uncertainty. So "they were upfront about it" neither licenses nor condemns, and the action has to be judged on its own.

The related failure is presenting a conclusion as more settled than it is. A recommendation likely to be revised isn't disqualified by that, but giving it a sense of permanence it hasn't earned is a real problem, and the fix is an explicit commitment to update → [[Evidence hierarchy#When mechanistic reasoning is reliable]].

## Make the symmetry breaker do the work

When two cases are being treated differently, the stated reason has to be something that actually differs between them. "It's a subjective decision" and "I'm speaking as a private individual" apply to both sides of almost any comparison, so neither can be what separates a cheap supplement from a dangerous drug. What separates them is expected harm and benefit: addictiveness, the size of the downside, whether there's a quantified burden of harm at all.

This is a general check on an argument's structure. If a proposed distinction would survive being applied to the case it's meant to rule out, it isn't the thing doing the work.

## Count the cost of acquiring it

Every intervention carries a cost before any effect: the money, the time, the effort of getting it, and whatever risk attaches to obtaining it. So an intervention with no effect isn't a wash, it's a small loss, and a beneficial one has to clear that cost before it's net positive.

## Use the realistic counterfactual

Opportunity cost has to be measured against what the person would otherwise actually do, not against the best available alternative. Assuming the money or time would have gone to the single most beneficial option inflates the cost and is almost always false, and nobody is in a position to know what that option would have been anyway.

This often changes the comparison. Someone already buying supplements is choosing among them, so the relevant question is this one against another, not this one against nothing. Someone already making a trip isn't adding the risk of the trip.

## When the cost becomes objective

While the cost is small relative to someone's disposable income, there's little objective to say about it, because what that loss is worth to them is their own valuation. As it approaches things needed for health and basic functioning, the harm becomes assessable and there's little room to disagree, whatever the person's own valuation.

## Low confidence cuts both ways

If confidence and effect size are both low on the benefit side, and the cost is also small and poorly characterized, the comparison can be genuinely indeterminate. Low confidence in a benefit is not by itself an argument against acting, any more than it's an argument for it.

## Harm below the symptom threshold

"Stop if it bothers you" only works when the harm announces itself. Where damage accumulates in small increments, each one unnoticeable, the first symptom can arrive after the injury is done. In that case self-monitoring is not a substitute for a dose or duration limit set in advance.
