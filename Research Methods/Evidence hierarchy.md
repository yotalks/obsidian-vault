> [!summary] Bottom line
> When asking whether something helps or harms people, outcome data beats mechanistic reasoning. The order is randomized trials, then cohort studies, then mechanistic studies, because that's how well each tracks what happens in trials. Mechanisms can't be tallied into a net effect, since biology involves thousands of interacting pathways and no study models them all. Saying epidemiology can't "prove causation" only means it's fallible, which is true of every kind of science. All of this is about science in general, including nutrition.

## The order

For an exposure-outcome question like "does this food raise heart disease risk?":

1. **Randomized controlled trials** of the outcome
2. **Cohort studies** with reliable measurement of the exposure and outcome, adjusted for confounders
3. **Mechanistic studies**, including cell and animal work
4. **Anecdotes**, for anything slow to develop

This depends on the question and on quality. A poorly done trial can be worth less than a good cohort study, and a clear mechanism can beat weak, inconsistent epidemiology. And a very convincing body of epidemiology can trump a mechanism that points the other way. So the tiers are about what's true all else equal.

Cohort studies sit between the other two because of how well they track trials. In nutrition, matched cohort and trial evidence agree closely on average. → [[Nutritional epidemiology]]

## Why outcomes beat mechanisms

Almost every intervention acts on many pathways at once, some good and some bad. A mechanistic study usually looks at one or a few of them, so it can't tell you the net effect. Counting up the good and bad mechanisms you've heard of doesn't work either, because you don't know how much each one counts, how they interact, or which ones nobody's found yet.

If we could model every interaction of every relevant mechanism, that would be the best approach. But no study in nutrition, or in nearly any other area of biology, does this. So to learn whether a mechanism that looks good or bad actually is, you have to test the outcome, which is what a trial or a cohort study does.

Cases where the mechanism and the outcome diverged:

- **Growth factors:** Cells exposed to platelet-derived growth factor grow and thrive, but that doesn't make it good for the person. In dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, a gene rearrangement makes tumor cells overproduce PDGF, which keeps stimulating them.
[Rutkowski 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28365129/)
- **Beta-carotene:** Its antioxidant role suggested it would prevent cancer, and trials found harm in smokers. → [[Beta-carotene conversion]]
- **Raising HDL:** HDL shuttles cholesterol out of the bloodstream, and drugs that raised it still didn't help. → [[HDL]]

Inferences from chemistry are the same. Saying sugars are bad because they're aldehydes tells you nothing about what happens when people eat them. Not all sugars are aldehydes anyway, since fructose is a ketone.

## When mechanistic reasoning is reliable

Mechanistic reasoning isn't weak everywhere. How far it can be trusted tracks whether the mechanisms carrying the weight can be identified.

In physics you can predict how fast something falls on a distant planet without going there, given the masses involved. Other factors exist, like local air density, but it's easy to say which ones dominate and which are rounding errors. Asking what happens when a bullet enters a skull is the same: no trial is needed, because the load-bearing mechanism is obvious and the rest are negligible.

Nutrition and physiology are the opposite case. Many pathways act at once, their weights are unknown, and there's no way to tell which dominate. The mechanisms can't be ranked against each other, so outcome data earns more weight, because it already subsumes all of them, including the ones nobody has found.

So "mechanistic speculation" isn't one category with one reliability. The question to ask of a mechanistic argument is whether its field is one where the dominant mechanisms can be picked out. Where they can, a mechanism alone can justify a confident prediction. Where they can't, it's a hypothesis waiting on an outcome.

Two further properties follow from this, and both apply to the fields where mechanisms can't be ranked.

**Mechanistic conclusions are easier to overturn.** A conclusion resting on a proposed mechanism can be undone by a single new finding about that mechanism, where an outcome result needs a new outcome study to move it. That makes mechanistic recommendations more open to amendment than outcome-based ones, which is a reason to pair them with a commitment to revise rather than a reason to withhold them. The failure is presenting them with a permanence they haven't earned.

**A mechanism doesn't transfer across targets just because the targets are similar.** The same mediator can be protective in one context and harmful in another, so structural or genetic similarity between two targets isn't sufficient grounds to carry an effect from one to the other. Similarity raises the prior; it doesn't preserve the sign.

## How well mechanistic research predicts trials

Mechanistic research translates poorly and unpredictably:

- Across 121 references comparing animal and human results, translation rates ranged from 0 to 100%, so whether a result will carry over is hard to predict. The authors rated the evidence as high risk of bias, and much of it was old.
[Leenaars 2019](https://translational-medicine.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12967-019-1976-2)
- Of 367 therapies tested in animals across 54 diseases, 50% reached human studies, 40% reached randomized trials and 5% reached approval. Positive results agreed between animal and clinical studies 86% of the time, so the failure comes partly from what happens in later human testing.
[Ineichen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002667)

These are mostly drug and disease models, not nutrition.

## Measuring the hierarchy instead of asserting it

The order above can be measured rather than stipulated. Once a gold standard is chosen, every lower tier can be scored by how often it agrees with that standard on the direction of effect, which gives an empirical ranking in place of a conventional one. That work exists for cohort studies in nutrition → [[Nutritional epidemiology]]. It hasn't been done for mechanistic studies in nutrition, so claims about what mechanistic evidence is worth in this field are still arguments rather than measurements.

Three things follow.

**Objections to a whole tier are already priced in.** If a tier agrees with trials some measured fraction of the time, then naming a bias that afflicts that tier doesn't subtract further confidence from the tier's average performance. Whatever healthy user bias and the rest are doing, they're already inside the figure. The objection has force against a particular study, not against the tier whose score already reflects it.

**A tier that systematically disagreed would be as useful as one that agreed.** Uninformativeness comes from agreement at chance, not from disagreement. If no cohort finding ever matched a trial, cohort studies would be maximally informative, because inverting them would give the trial result every time. So the claim that none of them replicate would, if true, make cohort evidence more valuable rather than worthless.

**The average doesn't settle the individual case.** A study can be poor enough to set aside whatever its tier's average is, so tier-level figures set the prior and don't replace reading the study.

## Holding everything to the same standard

**"It doesn't prove causation."** If this means an inference could be wrong, then it's true of every RCT and mechanistic study too. That's human fallibility, not a problem with epidemiology. Causal claims in science are inferences that make an outcome more expected on one hypothesis than another. They were never 100% certain.

**"Trials only count in identical people."** If trials need to be done in phenotypically identical people to carry weight, then mechanistic studies have to meet that too, and none do. So this can't be used to rank mechanisms above trials.

**"Common sense."** If this means what seems obvious on first impression, it isn't evidence. If it means looking at trials, cohorts and mechanisms together, that's just the evidence hierarchy. A theory that explains all three levels with one explanation is more parsimonious.

**"My mechanisms fit what we observe."** Choosing mechanisms because they fit the observed data means the outcome data is doing the work. It's also what anyone citing mechanisms for a diet they already like would say.

## Anecdotes

An anecdote is a real observation, so it's weak evidence rather than no evidence. How weak depends on the outcome. A nut allergy can be shown by anecdote, because shock follows quickly and reliably every time. Heart disease can't, since it builds for decades. That means anecdotes are weakest for what kills most people, and they take a back seat to epidemiology there. → [[Mendelian randomization#Anecdotes]]

## Thin evidence

A lack of long-term data on a diet doesn't count in its favor. Vegans are more common and easier to study than carnivore dieters, and vegan communities have been around longer, so more data exists on them. If the data did favor one diet, that would be a reason to prefer it, whatever anecdotes say.
