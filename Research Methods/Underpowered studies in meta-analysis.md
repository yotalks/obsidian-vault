> [!summary] Bottom line
> More studies don't always make a better meta-analysis. Underpowered studies give weaker causal estimates and tend to show bigger effects, so when enough adequately powered studies exist, leaving the underpowered ones out can give a better estimate, at the cost of a little precision.

## The argument

Some people argue that omission of studies from meta-analysis is bad form because the more point estimates, the better. However, there are circumstances where including more studies can actually lower the quality of causal estimates.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| P | underpowered studies provide weaker causal estimates than adequately powered studies |
| Q | meta-analytic summations that include adequately powered studies to the exclusion of insufficiently powered studies will provide better causal estimates than meta-analytic summations that include both adequately powered studies and insufficiently powered studies |

- **P1:** If underpowered studies provide weaker causal estimates than adequately powered studies, then meta-analytic summations that include adequately powered studies to the exclusion of insufficiently powered studies will provide better causal estimates than meta-analytic summations that include both adequately powered studies and insufficiently powered studies. (P→Q)
- **P2:** Underpowered studies provide weaker causal estimates than adequately powered studies. (P)
- **C:** Therefore, meta-analytic summations that include adequately powered studies to the exclusion of insufficiently powered studies will provide better causal estimates than meta-analytic summations that include both adequately powered studies and insufficiently powered studies. (∴Q)

## Evidence

A study with low statistical power is less likely to detect a true effect, and also less likely to have a statistically significant result that reflects a true effect. Low power leads to overestimated effect sizes and results that don't replicate.
[Button 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23571845/)

Across 14,886 meta-analyses from 1,991 Cochrane reviews, 70% contained only underpowered studies (under 50% power to detect a 30% relative risk reduction), and only 17% had at least two adequately powered studies. In meta-analyses with both, underpowered studies showed bigger effects: odds ratios 15% lower than in adequately powered studies for drug trials and 12% lower for non-drug trials. Leaving the underpowered studies out raised the standard error of the pooled effect by a median of 11% and tended to reduce heterogeneity. The authors concluded that when at least two adequately powered studies exist, underpowered studies often add little information.
[Turner 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3609745/)
