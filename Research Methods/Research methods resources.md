## Biostatistics

**STAT 6200: Introduction to Biostatistics lecture notes (University of Georgia)**, shared in the Study Archives Discord:
- Part 1: diagnostic tests, sensitivity, specificity and predictive values (pages 63–68), ROC curves (69–71), relative risk and odds ratios (74–77), probability distributions (78–100) → [[Diagnostic test accuracy]], [[Odds ratios and risk ratios]], [[Probability distributions]]
- Part 2: confidence intervals (pages 21–36), hypothesis testing (37–39), type I and II errors and false positives and negatives (40–59), power and sample size (59–69), comparing two means (70–75)
- Part 3: chi-squared test (pages 12–20), odds ratios (27–33)

Other readings:
- [Sainani 2011: Understanding odds ratios](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21402371/): when odds ratios can be read as risk ratios and when they exaggerate effects → [[Odds ratios and risk ratios]]
- Basic distribution overview: a cheat sheet of the common statistical quantities (expected value, variance) and distributions
- [UCLA OARC: Introduction to power analysis](https://stats.oarc.ucla.edu/other/mult-pkg/seminars/intro-power/): a thorough seminar on power and sample size → [[Sample size and power]]
- [Learning statistics with jamovi](https://www.learnstatswithjamovi.com/): a free statistics textbook

## Causal inference

- [Causal Inference: What If](https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook) (Hernán & Robins): the standard text on causal inference, free online. Essential reading for anyone commenting on causal inference.
- [Observation and Experiment: An Introduction to Causal Inference](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674241633) (Rosenbaum): covers RCTs, observational studies, notation for treatment assignment, propensity score matching and other matching methods, and sensitivity analysis. Very non-technical, so it's much more approachable than most books on the subject.
- [The Effect: An Introduction to Research Design and Causality](https://theeffectbook.net/) (Huntington-Klein): another accessible book, free online, and easier than What If. The first part covers statistics like regression and can be skipped if you already know it. It includes code (R, Stata and Python), so it's also some exposure to coding, and the author has a [YouTube playlist](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLcTBLulJV_AK1hKtnO0-kYrU0D09K-kj8) for the book.
- [A First Course in Causal Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18793) (Ding): a rigorous treatment, based on the author's Berkeley course. It assumes calculus-based probability and mathematical statistics, plus linear and logistic regression.

See also [[Nutritional epidemiology]] for how observational studies compare with RCTs.

## Learning R

**Complete beginners (no programming):**
1. [swirl](https://swirlstats.com/students.html) teaches R inside R itself, step by step. Start with the basic "R Programming" course and finish it completely, then pick other swirl courses depending on what you want to use R for.
2. [Doing Meta-Analysis in R](https://bookdown.org/MathiasHarrer/Doing_Meta_Analysis_in_R/) (Harrer et al.): a guide to meta-analysis, meta-regression, forest plots and power analysis in R.

**R as programming (computer science):**
- [Advanced R](https://adv-r.hadley.nz/) (Wickham): how R works as a programming language.

**R through statistics:**
- [R by Example](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-031-76074-7) (Albert et al.)
- [R for Data Science](https://r4ds.hadley.nz/) (Wickham et al.): importing, tidying, transforming and visualizing data.

**R for work and research projects:**
- [Building reproducible analytical pipelines with R](https://raps-with-r.dev/) (Rodrigues): writing and organizing code, reproducibility and version control. Best read after an introductory computer science course that covers things like functional programming and data types.
