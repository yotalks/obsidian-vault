> [!summary] Bottom line
> Animal experiments suggest that partial strains lower the length at which muscle tears. The proposed explanation for strains during normal movement is that smaller injuries accumulate without enough recovery. The practical takeaway is to manage stretched-position training volume, with warming up and rest between sets as additional precautions.

## Muscles tear at a set length

When animal muscles were rapidly lengthened to failure, stimulated muscles needed 13–18 N more force to tear and absorbed 88–100% more energy than passive ones. But they tore at the same length, always at the far muscle-tendon junction. So length, not force or energy, decided when the muscle tore. Activation let the muscle absorb more energy before reaching that length.
[Garrett 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3674268/)

Five muscles with different architectures, pulled passively at three speeds, all tore at the muscle-tendon junction, whichever end they were pulled from. They stretched 73% to 226% of their resting fiber length before failing, further in more pennate muscles.
[Garrett 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3344884/)

Stretch that large probably can't happen within a joint's normal range of motion. That raises the question of how strains happen at all.

## Strain damage is different from exercise damage

A single very fast lengthening contraction (900°/s over 90°) of a rat muscle halved its force and damaged the membranes of 54% of its fibers. The damaged fibers lost dystrophin, a protein that anchors the contractile machinery to the cell membrane and surrounding matrix as part of the costamere, while other costamere proteins weren't affected. The authors suggest dystrophin is key to transmitting force. Everything recovered by 21 days.
[Lovering 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4489567/)

Costameres link the myofibrils sideways to the surrounding collagen, so force can spread across fibers (lateral force transmission). That's thought to protect individual fibers from being torn.

One large-strain lengthening contraction and many small-strain ones recover differently. Blocking new muscle cell growth stopped recovery after the repeated small-strain contractions but not after the single large one. Damaged membranes resealed after the single large strain but not after the repeated small ones.
[Lovering 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17466731/)

High-force lengthening contractions in rats also damaged fibers mostly in the middle of the muscle, not at the muscle-tendon junction where pulled muscles tear.
[Lovering 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3521509/)

## One strain makes the next one easier

Rabbit muscles were stretched just short of rupture, then pulled to failure. They tore at 63% of the normal peak load and 79% of the normal stretch, with partial tears near the far muscle-tendon junction. So one partial strain lowered the length at which the muscle tore, which makes a re-injury much more likely.
[Taylor 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8465911/)

## How strains could happen in normal movement

The proposed explanation is that lengthening that doesn't tear a muscle still causes minor damage, including lost costamere proteins. Each bout lowers the length at which the muscle will tear. If it keeps happening without time to repair, that length eventually falls within the normal range of motion, and an ordinary movement tears the muscle. A strain would then come from damage building up, not from one bad movement, which is the mechanical fatigue model of [[Overuse injuries|overuse injuries]].

In 281 professional football players followed for three seasons, there were 44 non-contact hamstring injuries in matches. In the 5 minutes before an injury, players covered more distance at 21 km/h and above than in the same period of their previous 5 matches. The 15 minutes before didn't differ. Running more than 30 m at 21 km/h or faster in a 5-minute period was linked to 7 times the odds of a hamstring injury ([[Glossary#OR|OR]] 7.1).
[Moreno-Perez 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10765438/)

## Avoiding strains

### Volume and recovery

If strains come from damage building up, the most important thing is to keep the volume and frequency of stretched-position exercises at a level you can recover from. This may be why powerlifters tear a pec in the bench press, or a biceps when deadlifting with a mixed grip. Nothing has to go wrong in the rep where it tears. The cause is more likely the stretched-position work in the training block leading up to it.

### More force, less muscle stretch

Because muscle and tendon are in series, a muscle producing more force during an eccentric contraction stretches its tendon more and lengthens less itself over the same joint movement. If length decides when a muscle tears, then anything that raises eccentric force should lower the risk of a strain. Warm and unfatigued muscles also absorb more energy before reaching the length where they tear.

- **Warming up:** rat muscles hit with increasing impact energy needed less energy to suffer lasting damage at 17–32 °C than at 37 °C. Muscle can sit around 32 °C in normal conditions, so the authors recommend warming muscles to near core temperature before fast exercise. [Scott 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4852792/)
- **Resting between sets:** rabbit muscles fatigued by 25% or 50% tore at the same length as unfatigued ones, but absorbed only 70–92% as much energy before reaching it, and less the more fatigued they were. [Mair 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8775109/)
- **Maximizing recruitment:** verbal encouragement, visual or numerical feedback, and autonomy support may raise motor unit recruitment and so eccentric force. → [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]
