> [!summary] Bottom line
> Post-workout fatigue accumulates over conventional strength training programs with too little recovery between workouts, but not over eccentric-only programs. After very damaging eccentric workouts, the inflammatory response causes enough CNS fatigue to block the easily damaged fast twitch fibers from being used again, which protects them. After conventional workouts that CNS fatigue is much smaller, so later workouts can still activate and damage those fibers. Accumulated fatigue then clears like a fatigue debt: whatever isn't recovered before the next workout gets added to the end of the recovery after it.

## Eccentric-only training: no accumulation

Direct observations of post-workout fatigue accumulating over conventional strength training programs, like those bodybuilders use, are fairly recent. Earlier studies only tested very muscle-damaging eccentric-only programs, and found that post-workout fatigue doesn't accumulate even with too little recovery between workouts. The first workout produced a very large amount of post-workout fatigue, and later workouts done shortly afterward added no more damage.

In 26 male students, a second bout of maximal eccentric elbow flexions 3 days after the first (30 or 70 contractions) produced no extra damage and didn't slow recovery, even when it was more strenuous than the first. Muscle activity and EMG frequency were lower during the second bout, consistent with less use of fast twitch motor units, which the author interpreted as a neural protective mechanism.
[Chen 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12665973/)

In 22 untrained people doing 6 sets of 8 maximal eccentric biceps contractions every other day for 20 days, strength had fallen by day 8 and stayed lower for the rest of the study, while muscle activation rose from day 14.
[Krentz 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20495928/)

## Why: protective CNS fatigue

This can be explained by post-workout [[Supraspinal CNS fatigue|central nervous system fatigue]]. When muscle damage happens, the brain detects the inflammatory response and lowers central motor command at maximal effort. The more damage, the more motor unit recruitment is suppressed.

In mice, raising IL-1β in the brain worsened running performance after uphill running, and blocking it improved performance after muscle-damaging downhill running.
[Carmichael 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16778069/)

Suppressing recruitment stops the fast twitch fibers in the top half of the motor unit pool from being used in later workouts. Since fast twitch fibers are the ones mainly damaged during exercise → [[Muscle differences in recovery]], this CNS fatigue protects them from more damage while repair from the first workout takes place.

## Conventional training: fatigue accumulates

More recent studies have found that post-workout fatigue does accumulate over conventional strength training programs with too little recovery between workouts.
→ [[Recovery between workouts]]

In 11 people lifting 40% of maximum with the biceps to failure (concentric only) every day for 13 days, maximal strength recovered by the next day each time, but reps to failure and muscle contractile properties kept declining through day 13.
[Tosovic 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27260367/)

In 17 untrained men doing blood flow restricted knee extensions at 20% of 1RM, 7 sessions in 5 days cut strength by 5–10%, though strength was 6–11% above baseline 24 days after training ended → [[Very light loads and BFR]].
[Bjørnsen 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33735465/)

This can be explained by conventional workouts causing much less CNS fatigue than eccentric-only workouts, as shown by how long it lasts: 1–2 days versus 7–8 days.

In 10 male athletes, heavy squats (10 × 5 at 80% of 1RM) reduced voluntary activation for 48 hours.
[Thomas 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30067591/)

In 8 people doing eccentric elbow flexions until voluntary torque fell about 40%, voluntary activation was impaired at 2 hours and 1 day, and still hadn't fully recovered at 8 days, along with maximal torque.
[Prasartwuth 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1805656/)

After very damaging eccentric-only workouts, all the easily damaged fast twitch fibers are completely blocked from being activated in later workouts, so no more damage can happen and post-workout fatigue doesn't accumulate. After conventional workouts, the CNS fatigue is much smaller, so some easily damaged fast twitch fibers in the top half of the motor unit pool can be activated and damaged further.

Muscle fibers aren't damaged by tearing forces but by processes started by calcium ions flowing in → [[Calcium ion-related fatigue]]. So these fibers only need to be activated in later workouts for more damage to happen and accumulate.

## How long accumulated fatigue takes to clear

Post-workout fatigue accumulates, up to a point, during conventional strength training programs as bodybuilders do them. There's relatively little data, but we can estimate how long accumulated fatigue takes to dissipate.

One study programmed 7 workouts in 5 days, then 10 days of rest, then another 7 workouts in 5 days, then measured strength 3, 10, 17 and 24 days after training ended. Each workout was 4 sets of knee extensions at 20% of 1RM with blood flow restriction, one leg to failure and the other not to failure. Strength fell 8–10% by the rest week. The leg trained to failure was still about 5% weaker 10 days after training ended, about 30 days after starting, and 1RM was 7–8% above baseline in both legs by 17 days after training ended. MVC was about 7% above baseline at 24 days.
[Bjørnsen 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33735465/)

Light loads with blood flow restriction cause about the same post-workout fatigue as moderate loads without it, which is normal for bodybuilding → [[Very light loads and BFR]]. In 10 young women, sets to failure at 20% of 1RM with partial blood flow restriction and at 70% of 1RM without it both lowered strength 8–12% at 24–48 hours, with no difference between them, though only the blood flow restriction protocol raised soreness.
[Alvarez 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31043129/)

So we'd expect each workout to cause 2–2.5 days' worth of post-workout fatigue. Over 14 workouts, that's 28–35 days to get back to baseline strength. In fact, strength recovered about 30–32 days after starting the program.

This suggests post-workout fatigue builds up like a "fatigue debt": any fatigue not recovered before the next workout simply gets added to the end of the recovery period after that workout. How this happens physiologically isn't clear, because of the interactions between muscle damage, inflammation and post-workout CNS fatigue. But it's useful in practice, because it lets us use the normal relationship between workout volume and recovery time to estimate how long to deload after an overreaching block of training → [[Periodization]], [[Training volume]].

## Conclusion

Post-workout fatigue accumulates during conventional strength training programs with too little recovery time between workouts, but not during eccentric-only programs with too little recovery. Muscle damage accumulates whenever CNS fatigue from earlier workouts doesn't completely block access to the easily damaged fast twitch fibers in the top half of the motor unit pool. After eccentric-only workouts, the damage and inflammatory response are big enough to cause a large CNS fatigue effect that blocks those fibers and protects them in later workouts. After conventional workouts the effect is much smaller, so later workouts can still activate those fibers and add damage, but only until all the fast twitch fibers are blocked from being activated → [[Recovery between workouts]], [[Junk volume]].

Accumulated post-workout fatigue clears like a fatigue debt: any fatigue that isn't recovered before the next workout simply gets added to the end of the recovery period after that workout.
