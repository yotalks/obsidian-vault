> [!summary] Bottom line
> Recovery depends on the workout, the lifter and the performance we're trying to recover. Sets taken to failure and lighter-load, higher-repetition work can take longer to recover from than heavier work stopped short of failure. Maximal force, repeated-work capacity and jump performance can return at different times, so we need to match workout spacing to the demands of the next session.

## What we're recovering

Force can remain reduced because the muscle produces less force in response to activation, while reduced voluntary activation can also limit performance. These are different parts of post-workout fatigue. The calcium-related mechanisms are covered in [[Calcium ion-related fatigue]], and the comparison of central and peripheral fatigue after failure versus nonfailure squats is in [[Training volume]].

Set count does not capture the whole recovery demand. Sets with different loads, repetitions and proximity to failure can produce different recovery patterns, even when total repetitions are matched.
→ [[Training volume]]

## Proximity to failure

In 10 resistance-trained men, bench press and squat sessions compared 3 × 5, 6 × 5 and 3 × 10 using a 10RM load. The 3 × 10 session took every set to failure, while the other sessions stopped at 5 reps. Training to failure caused larger immediate losses in jump height and lifting velocity, and recovery was slower over the next 24–48 hours. Even 6 × 5 recovered faster than 3 × 10 despite matching the total repetitions.
[Morán-Navarro 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/)

## Load and repetition count

In 11 trained men, one leg performed knee extensions to failure at either 40% or 80% of maximal voluntary force, with volume-load matched. After the heavier session, strength in the exercised leg had recovered by 24 hours; after the lighter session, it had not fully recovered by 48 hours. The lighter session also reduced strength in the opposite, unexercised leg, which recovered by 48 hours.
[Farrow 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32401690/)

In 15 trained men, 4 sets of knee extensions to failure at 30% or 80% of 1RM produced more repetitions and greater immediate torque loss with the lighter load. Average torque recovery across testing velocities was also poorer at 48 hours after the lighter session, while soreness and blood myoglobin responses did not differ significantly.
[Haun 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5617935/)

## Exercise choice

In 12 well-trained men, 4 sets to failure at 80% of 1RM were performed separately for the squat, bench press and deadlift. Squat lifting velocity at 70% of 1RM remained reduced through 72 hours, by 8.61%. Bench-press velocity fell 26.69% immediately after exercise, while deadlift velocity did not significantly decline. Overall recovery did not differ significantly between exercises, and the deadlift did not cause more muscle damage or a longer recovery than the other lifts.
[Belcher 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30779596/)

## Maximal force and repeated work recover differently

In 26 trained men, 8 bench-press sets to failure reduced shoulder horizontal-adduction peak torque by 17% and total work by 25% immediately afterward. Soreness lasted 72 hours. Peak torque returned to baseline at 96 hours, but total work had not, so recovering the ability to produce one maximal effort did not mean recovering the ability to repeat efforts.
[Ferreira 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28595855/)

## Lifters and performance tests differ

Eleven trained men and eight trained women performed 5 × 5 back squats at 80% of 1RM, followed by a set to maximum repetitions. At 24 hours, jump height was down 10% in men (90% CI −13% to −7%) and 20% in women (−27% to −13%). At 72 hours, women were still down 13% (−21% to −5%). Isometric, concentric and eccentric strength followed different recovery patterns. The study was funded by Food for Health Ireland, and the authors declared no commercial or financial conflicts.
[Davies 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6206044/)

## Putting recovery into the program

We need to consider the session's demands and the next session's requirements together. A return in maximal strength can happen before a return in repeated-work capacity. The recovery interval we choose should therefore reflect both the exercises and the kind of performance we need to repeat.

The longer-term question of how often to train for muscle growth is covered in [[Training frequency and hypertrophy]]. The high-volume versus high-intensity recovery comparison is already there, while [[Repeated bout effect]] and [[Age and recovery]] cover familiarity and age.
