> [!summary] Bottom line
> Muscles with more fast twitch fibers, and muscles we can activate more fully, get more damaged by the same training volume. Lower body muscles tend to be large and slow twitch, so they usually recover faster than most upper body muscles, except the hamstrings. The biceps, triceps and pecs are damaged most easily, the rear delts and traps least, and the front and side delts, lats and other back muscles fall in between.

## Fiber type

All muscles contain a wide range of fibers, from very slow twitch to very fast twitch. Slow twitch fibers are usually activated at lower levels of effort and fast twitch fibers at higher levels. But the overall proportion of slow and fast twitch fibers varies greatly between muscles, and between people.
[Johnson 1973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4120482/)
→ [[Fiber-specific muscle growth]]

Fast twitch fibers are far more easily fatigued and damaged during exercise than slow twitch fibers. So muscles with a high proportion of fast twitch fibers get more damage than muscles with a low proportion.

In 8 untrained people doing 10 sets of 10 squat jumps, mostly fast twitch fibers were damaged. Membrane damage affected 7.6% of type I fibers, 10.3% of type IIa and 14.3% of type IIx, and sarcomere damage was seen in 84–86% of type II fibers versus 27% of type I fibers.
[Macaluso 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396301/)

## Activation capacity

How fully we can activate a muscle during a maximal effort also varies greatly between muscles, and tends to be higher in smaller muscles than larger ones → [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]. Activating more of a muscle's fibers means reaching more of its fast twitch fibers and causing more damage.

In 12 men, the quadriceps couldn't be activated by 15.5% during maximal contractions, versus 5.0% for the elbow flexors and plantar flexors and 1.3% for the dorsiflexors.
[Behm 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11870692/)

Central activation was about 87% for the gluteus maximus versus 96% for the gluteus medius in 20 healthy people, and trapezius activation was about 96% in healthy people.
[Gilfeather 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31775118/)
[Bech 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28735103/)

So smaller muscles like the biceps tend to get much more damage than larger muscles like the quadriceps. Large muscles with a high proportion of slow twitch fibers get the least damage from a given training volume, and small muscles with a high proportion of fast twitch fibers get the most.

## Damage measured across muscles

In 15 sedentary men doing 5 sets of 10 eccentric contractions at 80% of maximum for 9 muscle groups, strength fell 16–57% 1 day later. Strength loss was greater for the elbow flexors, elbow extensors and pecs than the other muscles, and greater for the knee flexors than the knee extensors, plantar flexors and spinal erectors. Soreness was greatest in the elbow flexors, elbow extensors and spinal erectors. A second session 2 weeks later caused less damage in every muscle, and the size of this protection was similar across muscles → [[Repeated bout effect]].
[Chen 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30663816/)

## Conclusion

Lower body muscles tend to be large and contain a high proportion of slow twitch fibers, so they usually get less damage and recover faster after the same strength training volume than most upper body muscles. The hamstrings are an exception, since they're easy to activate fully despite their size.

Among upper body muscles:
- **Most easily damaged:** biceps, triceps and pecs, since they have the most fast twitch fibers by proportion and are easy to activate fully because they're small.
- **Least easily damaged:** rear delts and traps, which have a high proportion of slow twitch fibers.
- **In between:** front delts, side delts, lats and the other back muscles, including the spinal erectors.

This affects how often each muscle can be trained → [[Recovery between workouts]], [[Training frequency and hypertrophy]].
