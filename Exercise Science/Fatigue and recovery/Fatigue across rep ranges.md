> [!summary] Bottom line
> Growth is similar across rep ranges when sets go to failure, but fatigue isn't. Lighter loads cause more fatigue per set, and more of it is central and calcium ion-related, while heavy sets cause relatively more metabolite-related fatigue. So lighter loads build less recruitment capacity and cause more post-workout damage for the same growth stimulus, which needs more recovery time. The idea that heavy training needs more recovery comes from strength sports, where light sets stop far from failure.

## Same growth, different fatigue

It's often claimed that since muscle growth is similar regardless of rep range, it doesn't matter what load we use. Across 21 studies with all sets taken to failure, low loads (60% of 1RM or less) and high loads grew muscle similarly, though high loads built more 1RM strength.
[Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)

But fatigue differs a lot between rep ranges. Fatigue is a temporary, reversible drop in exercise performance, usually caused by prior exercise. Bar speed slowing involuntarily over a set is one index of it, and fewer reps to failure from one set to the next is another.

## How much fatigue

The amount of fatigue a set to failure causes depends on the load. To fail with 80% of 1RM, fatigue has to reach 20% of fresh strength. To fail with 60% of 1RM, it has to reach 40%. So lighter-load sets cause more fatigue.

In 11 trained men doing knee extensions to failure with matched volume load, 40% of maximum caused more fatigue than 80% immediately afterward, in both the exercised leg and the unexercised leg, and the exercised leg still hadn't recovered after 48 hours, while it had recovered from the heavier session by 24 hours. The lighter load was also rated more uncomfortable.
[Farrow 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32401690/)
→ [[Recovery between workouts]]

## What kind of fatigue

Lighter loads cause a different kind of fatigue, not just more. Fatigue mechanisms can be in the central nervous system (the brain or spinal cord) or in the muscle, where they're metabolite-related or calcium ion-related, and they build up at different rates. Very short sets with heavy loads (1–5RM) involve relatively more metabolite-related fatigue, while moderate (6–15RM) and light (16–30RM) loads involve relatively less of it and more CNS and calcium ion-related fatigue.

In 9 adults holding finger abductions to exhaustion at 30%, 45%, 60% and 75% of maximum, central fatigue only appeared at 30%.
[Eichelberger 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17697026/)

Low-frequency fatigue, a calcium ion-related mechanism, was greater after low-force voluntary contractions than high-force ones, and tracked how long the fatiguing task lasted.
[Griffin 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18283442/)

Metabolite-related fatigue has few downsides for bodybuilding. But [[Supraspinal CNS fatigue|CNS fatigue]] lowers central motor command, and [[Calcium ion-related fatigue|calcium ion-related fatigue]] causes post-workout muscle damage. So lighter loads are worse for building the ability to recruit motor units, and they cause more damage for the same growth stimulus, which needs more time between workouts.

Heavy loads also raise voluntary activation more. In 26 men training to failure for 6 weeks, 80% of 1RM increased voluntary activation and muscle activity more than 30% did.
[Jenkins 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28611677/)
→ [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]

## Why heavy training seems harder to recover from

People often say heavy loads need more recovery than light loads. That idea comes partly from strength sports, where most early research was done. Weightlifters and powerlifters get a lot of joint wear from their exercises, and when they vary rep ranges, they train much further from failure with light loads than with heavy ones, so light workouts are easier to recover from. In bodybuilding, sets are compared at the same proximity to failure, and exercises aren't usually chosen for being hard on other tissues.

In 18 male weightlifters training for 8 weeks, a high-repetition, low-load program improved strength and muscle mass similarly to a high-load, low-repetition program, while causing less rise in muscle damage markers.
[Yeom 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10707615/)

## Conclusion

Lighter loads produce more fatigue per set, and more of it is CNS and calcium ion-related. That means a weaker stimulus for improving recruitment, so growth plateaus earlier, and more post-workout damage, which lengthens recovery and limits training frequency → [[Training frequency and hypertrophy]].
