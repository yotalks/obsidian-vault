> [!summary] Bottom line
> Sleep loss lowers strength, endurance, recruitment and coordination, and through glucocorticoids it reduces protein synthesis, raises breakdown and atrophies fast-twitch fibers. When sleep is short: train more often, with lower volume, heavier loads and a rep in reserve.

Sleep deprivation is defined as one or more nights without any sleep. Partial sleep deprivation (also called sleep restriction) refers to one or more nights with significantly less than the normal amount of sleep. When either happens for more than one night, this is often described as persistent sleep deprivation or restriction.

## Effects of insufficient sleep on exercise performance

Sleep deprivation reduces endurance, maximum strength, and power in trained athletes in a recent meta-analysis. But has minimal effects on anaerobic performance. Why is unclear.
[Kong 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11996801/)

We see persistent sleep restriction also reduces training performance during strength training workouts in trained lifters. Thus, sleep deprivation for single nights seems to produce similar effects to persistent sleep restriction for multiple nights.
[Knowles 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36136596/)

## Insufficient sleep effect on motor unit recruitment

Sleep deprivation increases the rating of perceived exertion associated with a given intensity of exercise which would tell us [[Supraspinal CNS fatigue|supraspinal CNS fatigue]] is going on as that is an uncomfortable sensation. It leads to a reduction in motor unit recruitment at maximal efforts. This makes sense as sleep restriction is known to reduce motivation during exercise. which reduces levels of motor unit recruitment that is possible at muscular failure.
[Temesi 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23760468/)

This is confirmed here where we see sleep deprivation reduces voluntary activation as measured by central activation ratio, which is a method of assessing [[Glossary#CNS|CNS]] fatigue.
[VanHelder 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2657963/)

We see how insufficient sleep produces this supraspinal CNS fatigue effect by looking at the effects of sleep deprivation on pain. Insufficient sleep reduces pain tolerance threshold, which tells us it will similar reduce max tolerable perception of effort, directly producing supraspinal CNS fatigue. It will also increase the s, sensitivity to any other discomforting sensation (like perception of feeling out of breath), so it exacerbates the negative effects of common sources of supraspinal CNS fatigue during exercise.
[Staffe 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31800612/)

We again see lower motor unit recruitment levels from insufficient sleep, this reduces the number of muscle fibers that are trained by strength training, thus stopping the highest-threshold motor units from being recruited fduring maximum efforts, which causes those muscle fibers to atrophy.
[Skurvydas 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33122092/)
→ [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]

## Insufficient sleep reduces coordination

Sleep restriction reduces the level of coordination that can be achieved in a movement, thus reducing exercise performance.
[Mah 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8889916/)

## Insufficient sleep as a stressor

Insufficient sleep is a stressor that creates a large circulating [[Glucocorticoids|glucocorticoid]] response. When glucocorticoids are detected by muscle fibers, they reduce background rates of muscle protein synthesis and create oxidative stress that increases rates of muscle protein breakdown while also creating muscle damage. 

Here we see insufficient sleep reducing [[Glossary#MPS|MPS]], this will increase the rate of atrophy between workouts, which makes it more difficult to achieve net hypertrophy in the week. It also reduces the rate of muscle damage repair between workouts, which reduces the amount of volume we can recover from.
[Saner 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217042/)

Sleep restriction increases [[Glossary#MPB|MPB]] rates which will increase rate of atrophy between workouts which makes it, again, harder to get net hypertrophy over a week, especially when training frequency is lower, as trained muscle fibers are not constantly in a hypertrophy period.
[Mônico-Neto 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26513007/)

Sleep restriction produces muscle damage which increases post-workout fatigue, which reduces the amount of workout volume we can recover from.
[Yujra 2020a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31701551/)

## Insufficient sleep causes atrophy

Insufficient sleep causes atrophy directly due to it being a stressor. We see this happening in animal models here.
[Dattilo 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22334180/)

We see muscles that are predominantly fast twitch have way more atrophy than muscles that are predominantly slow twitch after insufficient sleep.
[Mônico-Neto 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33529759/)

This is because of a very large amount of atrophy in fast twitch muscle fibers and minimal changes to slow twitch fibers. Immobilization and caloric restriction don't cause the same fiber-type specific atrophy. To add, the atrophy from those interventions aren't apared by nutritional interventions while fast twitch fiber-specific atrophy from insufficient sleep seems to be greatly affected by dietary leucine.
[de Sá Souza 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26645537/)

Muscle size is maintained by the balance between [[Glossary#MyoPS|MYOPS]] rates and MPB rates, so either reductions in MYOPS rates or elevations in MPB rates could be responsible for atrophy. In humans, persistent sleep restriction reduces daily integrated rates of MYOPS but its effects on MPB are unclear.
[Saner 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32078168/)

[[Mechanical tension|Mechanical tension]] during strength training stimulates increases in muscle fiber size by elevating MYOPS rates, while MPB isn't affected much. Atrophy during detraining, bed rest, or immobilization is caused by a drop in MYOPS rates, while MPB isn't affected much.
[Brook 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/med/35606155)

It does seem like insufficient sleep causes an elevation in MPB rates based on animal studies, however. The increases in glucocorticoids caused by the insufficient sleep seem to cause catabolic signaling pathways causing atrophy.
[Mônico-Neto 2015](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/apnm-2015-0061)

Interestingly, insufficient sleep, is associated with inflammation, structural damage, and fibrosis that is more prevalent in slow twitch fibers than fast twitch fibers, even though the atrophy affects the fast twitch fibers to a greater extent.
[Yujra 2020b](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212440320308245)

In this way, the nature of the muscle damage response seems to reflect situations where muscle damage is not completely repaired after very intense eccentric training programs that cause a ton of oxidative stress and inflammation.
[[Impaired muscle regrowth]]

Several lines of evidence point to glucocorticoid production as the mechanism by which insufficient sleep causes atrophy and damage. To start, immobilization and caloric restriction cause atrophy of all fiber types similarly, glucocorticoids only produce atrophy of the most glycolytic muscle fibers (just like insufficient sleep).
[Dekhuijzen 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7759433/)

Secondly, while glucocorticoids are known for their catabolic activity that increases MPB rates, there is data showing they also decrease MPS rates. Which is the exact same with insufficient sleep.
[Shah 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10644539/)

Thirdly, the provision of dietary leucine can mitigate atrophy from glucocorticoid administration in the same way leucine mitigates atrophy that happens with insufficient sleep.
[Wang 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5293580/)

Fourthly, glucocorticoid administration causes inflammation and muscle damage that is more prevalent in slow twitch fibers, even though it also causes atrophy only noticeable in fast twitch fibers.
[Braunstein 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7349575/)

Observations of greater muscle damage in slow twitch fibers than fast twitch as a result of glucocorticoids can be supported by the fact that myofibrillar sensitivity is also more greatly impacted by glucocorticoids in slow twitch muscle compared to fast twitch muscle. Losses in myofibrillar sensitivity and muscle damage are both [[Calcium ion-related fatigue|calcium ion-related fatigue]] mechanisms. This suggests that glucocorticoids stimulate calcium ion-related fatigue mechanisms, likely by causing mitochondrial dysfunction that increases oxidative stress.
[Laszewski 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3976885/)

## Practical considerations

Training frequency is especially important when sleep is restricted. A faster rate of atrophy between workouts from reduced MPS and increased MPB means that it's very important to train muscles every 48 hours or 3 times a week.to make sure we still have net hypertrophy over the week.

Lower workout volumes are also likely necessary because the reduced MPS and additional damage created by oxidative stress means it will take longer to recover from a given workout volume. So, I would opt for single sets 3 times per week.
Compared with 3 sets per muscle 3 times a week, 1–2 sets per muscle 3 times a week is probably a better strategy while sleep restricted.

Proximity to failure also affects the amount of post workout fatigue we have, so since it is more difficult to recover because of the reduced MPS and additional damage, it makes sense to leave a rep in reserve.

Repetition range also affects the damage we experience, since it's harder to recover from a workout, it makes sense to train with heavier loads (1-5RM).

Since motor unit recruitment is impaired at maximum efforts, it will be helpful to use motivational techniques to help counter that such as listening to upbeat music, training with a gym partner, and trying to beat previous logbook numbers.
