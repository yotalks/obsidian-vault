> [!summary] Bottom line
> Exercise itself isn't a stressor, but stress from life events still slows recovery from a strength training workout. The likely reason is that stress suppresses the immune system, which runs the inflammatory response that has to finish before muscle damage can be repaired. Wounds heal more slowly in stressed people too.

## Stress slows workout recovery

Although exercise itself isn't a stressor → [[Exercise isn't a stressor]], stress from life events can still affect how well we recover from a strength training workout. Recovery from post-workout fatigue takes longer in humans and animals under more stress.

In 31 undergraduate resistance training students doing a 10RM leg press test plus 6 sets at 80–100% of their 10RM, those with more life event stress recovered maximal isometric force more slowly over the next 96 hours, even after adjusting for fitness, workload and training experience. Higher stress also meant worse recovery of perceived energy, fatigue and soreness.
[Stults-Kolehmainen 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24343323/)
→ [[Recovery between workouts]]

In rats, unpredictable sound stress didn't change pain thresholds by itself, but it prolonged the muscle tenderness after eccentric exercise. Removing the adrenal medulla reduced this effect, and giving stress levels of adrenaline mimicked it, so the sympathoadrenal stress response seems to stop pain nerves from registering recovery.
[Alvarez 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6882572/)

## Why it happens

Post-workout fatigue is made up of several fatigue mechanisms, all initially triggered by calcium ions building up during exercise → [[Calcium ion-related fatigue]]. One key mechanism is muscle damage. Damage is first caused by proteases called calpains and then by inflammatory processes, which can last several days after very fatiguing workouts.

The inflammatory processes are governed by the immune system. Stress suppresses the immune system, and this is most likely why muscle damage repair slows when stress in our daily life rises.

Psychological stress has been linked with dysregulation of several parts of the immune system, though the effects differ by situation and population, and in some contexts the immune response to stress is adaptive.
[Morey 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26086030/)

## Wound healing

Wounds heal more slowly in people who are more stressed. In a meta-analysis of 11 studies covering clinical wounds, punch biopsies, blisters and tape stripping, stress was associated with worse healing (r = −0.42, 95% CI −0.51 to −0.32), and 17 of 22 studies in the systematic review found stress linked with impaired healing or a disrupted healing biomarker.
[Walburn 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19686881/)

Wounds like contusions and lacerations involve muscle damage caused by calcium ion accumulation in the same way as exercise, so we can draw parallels between how stress affects wound healing and how it affects strength recovery after a workout.

## Conclusion

Stress from life events slows recovery from a strength training workout. Stress suppresses the immune system, and the immune system governs the inflammatory response to muscle damage, which has to finish before muscle damage repair can take place → [[Recovery between workouts]].
