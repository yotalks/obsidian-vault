> [!summary] Bottom line
> Fibers grow in cross-section from high active tension and in length from passive tension, and contraction types differ in which they provide. Fast concentrics provide neither. Slow concentrics and isometrics at short lengths give similar fiber hypertrophy and no sarcomerogenesis. Slow eccentrics and isometrics at long lengths add passive tension, and eccentrics add more. Not every eccentric lengthens fascicles though, and joint speed doesn't show what the fibers are doing.

## Two kinds of tension

Muscle growth happens after individual fibers are exposed to high enough [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]]:

- **Active tension** comes from actin-myosin cross-bridges when fibers are activated and either shorten or lengthen slowly. It stimulates fibers to grow in cross-section (fiber hypertrophy).
- **Passive tension** comes from titin in each sarcomere when its stiff segment is stretched past a certain length. It stimulates fibers to grow in length ([[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]]).

| Contraction | Active tension | Passive tension | Growth stimulus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fast concentric** | Too low | None | None |
| **Slow concentric** | High enough | None | Fiber hypertrophy |
| **Isometric at short lengths** | High enough | None | Fiber hypertrophy |
| **Isometric at long lengths** | High enough | Moderate | Fiber hypertrophy and sarcomerogenesis |
| **Slow eccentric** | High enough | High | Fiber hypertrophy and more sarcomerogenesis |
| **Fast eccentric** | Unclear | Highest | Mainly sarcomerogenesis |

## Fast concentric contractions

Sarcomeres aren't elongated, so there's no passive tension. Fast shortening means few cross-bridges are bound at once, so active tension stays below what's needed for growth, however hard you try and however many motor units are recruited. In single muscle fibers, the force-velocity relationship came from fewer myosin motors being attached at higher shortening speeds, rather than each motor pulling less.
[Piazzesi 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18022371/)
→ [[Increasing speed]]

Load on the fibers matters even when activation is identical. In rats stimulated with the same electrical pattern, isometric contractions caused twice the hypertrophy of contractions at 50–60% of isometric force.
[Eftestøl 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27488660/)

## Slow concentrics and isometrics at short lengths

Neither stretches sarcomeres, so there's only active tension. In a maximal effort, recruitment is high, so the fibers at the top of the motor unit pool get the stimulus too. Isometrics have slightly higher active tension because they involve no shortening, so more cross-bridges stay bound. Once tension is past the threshold for growth, going higher doesn't seem to add much, so the two give similar growth when other factors are equal.
→ [[Isometric training and hypertrophy]]

## Slow eccentrics and isometrics at long lengths

Both have active tension like the contractions above, and both also stretch activated sarcomeres, which adds passive tension and a stimulus for sarcomerogenesis. They aren't equal though. Eccentrics stretch titin further, so they create more passive tension and more total fiber tension. So they should give similar fiber hypertrophy but more sarcomerogenesis, and therefore more total growth.

In rats given electrical stimulation at 100 Hz for 3 weeks, gastrocnemius mass rose 17% with eccentric contractions versus 7% with isometric contractions. Peak torque and total torque over time were higher with eccentrics and tracked the gains, so the authors put the difference down to the loading rather than the contraction type itself.
[Ashida 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29074713/)

Eccentric training may also change the spring itself. Rats trained eccentrically on a treadmill for 8 weeks produced more passive force at every length, with a stiffer spring constant (1.71 vs 1.31 N/mm).
[Reich 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10848536/)

## Fast eccentrics

Titin is viscoelastic, so the force it gives depends on how it's stretched and not only how far. Stretched myofibrils showed stress relaxation and hysteresis, explained by a very small number of titin domains unfolding per molecule.
[Minajeva 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11222304/)

So fast eccentrics are expected to make more passive tension than slow ones, and more sarcomerogenesis. How much active tension they provide is unclear.

In 24 untrained people training the elbow flexors for 8 weeks per arm, eccentric training grew muscle thickness more than concentric training overall. Fast eccentrics (180°/s) beat both fast and slow concentrics, while slow eccentrics (30°/s) beat fast concentrics but not slow concentrics. Fast eccentrics also gained the most strength.
[Farthing 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12756571/)

Not every eccentric lengthens fascicles. Over 10 weeks, vastus lateralis fascicles grew about 14% only with 100% MVC eccentrics at 240°/s. There was no increase at 90°/s with 65% or 100% MVC, or at 90°/s at short lengths.
[Sharifnezhad 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24803466/)

Joint speed doesn't tell you what the fibers are doing either. Early in these contractions the fascicles shortened while the knee was bending, because the tendon stretched, and most of their lengthening came as knee force fell. Only in that last phase did the fast protocol lengthen fascicles faster than the others. The authors suggest muscle damage or titin signaling might explain the result.

## Why comparisons are hard

Neither kind of growth is guaranteed. A fiber at its maximum cross-section won't get thicker, and a fiber with enough sarcomeres that stretching it no longer strains titin won't add more. So contraction types are best compared in untrained fibers.

Fatigue also interferes, since some fatigue mechanisms reduce recruitment and others reduce active tension, and they may differ between contraction types. That's why animal models with electrical stimulation and short workouts give the cleanest comparisons, though they don't show what happens in voluntary training.
