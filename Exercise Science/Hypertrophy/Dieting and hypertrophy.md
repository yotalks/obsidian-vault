> [!summary] Bottom line
> Dieting lowers background muscle protein synthesis and muscle glycogen. Larger and longer deficits are more likely to be treated as a stressor, which also raises protein breakdown and makes dietary protein less effective. Together these speed atrophy between workouts, slow recovery and weaken each workout's stimulus. In a meta-analysis, a deficit of about 500 kcal a day was enough to prevent lean mass gains, though strength gains were unaffected.

## Two levels of effect

Caloric restriction works slightly differently depending on whether the body treats it as a stressor. When it isn't treated as a stressor, it lowers background muscle protein synthesis (MPS) and muscle glycogen. When it is, it also raises muscle protein breakdown (MPB) and reduces sensitivity to dietary protein. The size and length of the deficit likely decide which, with bigger, longer deficits being more stressful.

Across trials of resistance training for at least 3 weeks, training in an energy deficit impaired lean mass gains compared with training without one (effect size −0.57), but strength gains were similar. A deficit of about 500 kcal a day prevented lean mass gains.
[Murphy 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/)

## Lower protein synthesis

Lower MPS speeds atrophy between workouts, making net growth over the week harder, which is a reason to train more often when dieting → [[Training frequency and hypertrophy]]. It also slows the repair of muscle damage between workouts, which lowers how much volume you can recover from per workout at the same frequency → [[Training volume]].

In starved rats, an enzyme that breaks down branched-chain amino acids became less active after 2 and 4 days, sparing protein, but much more active after 6 days, pointing to faster protein breakdown as starvation goes on.
[Holeček 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11300223/)

## Lower glycogen

Lower muscle glycogen increases both supraspinal CNS fatigue and excitation-contraction coupling failure during training, which weakens the stimulus, especially in later sets as glycogen drops further → [[Supraspinal CNS fatigue]], [[Calcium ion-related fatigue]].

In starved mice, liver glycogen dropped within 8–12 hours while muscle glycogen was unchanged at 16 hours. Muscle glycogen then fell, mostly in type IIB fibers, and was almost gone after 4 days, as it was used to maintain blood glucose.
[Sakaida 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3631541/)

## Higher protein breakdown

Higher MPB speeds atrophy between workouts through [[Glucocorticoids|glucocorticoids]], which promote oxidative stress, again making net growth over the week harder and favoring more frequent training.

In rats, severe caloric restriction raised corticosterone, the rat equivalent of cortisol. Eating 40–50% of daily calorie needs or less increased oxidative damage in the liver, while moderate restriction (60–70%) increased antioxidant capacity.
[Stankovic 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23682026/)

In 15 overweight men combining 4 days of very low calorie intake with 8 hours of walking a day (a deficit of about 5,000 kcal a day), cortisol rose, and they lost 2.8 kg of lean mass along with 2.1 kg of fat.
[Calbet 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24602091/)

## Less response to protein

Reduced sensitivity to dietary protein makes eating protein much less effective at boosting the growth from strength training.
