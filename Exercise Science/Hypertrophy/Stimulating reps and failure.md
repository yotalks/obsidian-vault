> [!summary] Bottom line
> Not every set to failure has the maximum number of stimulating reps. Failure comes when perceived effort hits its tolerable maximum, not when motor unit recruitment does. Breathlessness, burning and inflammation add to perceived effort without adding neural drive, so later sets, high-rep sets, short rests and techniques like drop sets, rest-pause, pre-exhaustion, supersets and giant sets reach failure with lower recruitment and fewer stimulating reps.

## What causes failure

Since the stimulating reps model arrived, a common misunderstanding is that every set to failure includes the maximum number of stimulating reps. That isn't true because of how failure is reached.

From the outside, failure happens when the muscle can't produce enough force to lift the load. But experiments show that with more neural drive, the muscle could lift it once or even two or three more times. So the limit is the central nervous system's capacity to send neural drive and recruit motor units, not the muscle fibers → [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]].

The CNS stops increasing neural drive when we reach our maximum tolerable perception of effort, as described by the [[Psychobiological model of fatigue|psychobiological model of fatigue]].
[Marcora 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18618133/)

Sending neural drive to a muscle creates a proportional perception of effort in the sensory part of the brain. So as neural drive rises over a set, so does perceived effort, and once neural drive reaches the level that creates the maximum tolerable effort, it can't rise further. In 16 men lifting weights with fatigued and non-fatigued arms, perceived effort, muscle activity and a brain measure of motor command all rose together with load and fatigue, and perceived effort correlated with the motor command signal.
[de Morree 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22725828/)

## Why recruitment at failure varies

Other sensations also add to perceived effort without adding neural drive. The fatigue literature calls these [[Supraspinal CNS fatigue|supraspinal CNS fatigue]] mechanisms, because they lower the neural drive we can reach at maximum tolerable effort:
- **Breathlessness:** In 11 people doing isometric knee extensions, traditional sets lowered voluntary activation while sets with short rests inside them didn't, and the cardiovascular strain during traditional sets correlated with the drop in activation (r = −0.85). [Río-Rodríguez 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26982500/)
- **Burning from metabolites:** In 11 men, raising blood pH with sodium bicarbonate before a 2-minute maximal knee extension preserved voluntary activation afterward. Acidity may reduce central drive by activating group III and IV sensory nerves. [Siegler 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25727892/)
- **Inflammation:** damaged or long-working muscles release inflammatory signaling molecules that reach the brain and create feelings of fatigue and lethargy. [Vargas 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25164464/)

Spinal CNS fatigue can do the same, but it's much less well described. During a 4.5-minute sustained elbow flexion at 70% of maximum, motoneuron excitability fell by up to a third and stayed low into recovery, while at 20% it fell only in the motoneurons that were active.
[Brownstein 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33788627/)

## In practice

The first set of a workout may have the maximum number of stimulating reps, but later sets won't, especially with high-rep sets or short rest periods → [[Training volume]]. In complexes like drop sets, rest-pause, pre-exhaustion, supersets or giant sets, the first set may have the maximum number of stimulating reps, but the rest won't, and the further into the complex, the more unpleasant it feels and the harder it seems, the worse the stimulus gets → [[Junk volume]].
