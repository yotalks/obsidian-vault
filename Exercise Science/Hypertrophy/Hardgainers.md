> [!summary] Bottom line
> Hardgainers are beginners who gain very little muscle from strength training, but a badly built program can look the same. A program works only if it has enough workout stimulus (sets close to failure), enough frequency (each muscle at least twice a week) and enough recovery (not too much volume). To test whether you're a hardgainer, do one set to failure per muscle twice a week. If that doesn't build muscle after about three months, the problem is you rather than the program. Real hardgainers likely recruit fewer high-threshold motor units or have more slow twitch fibers, and the fix for both is training to maximize recruitment rather than adding volume.

## Hardgainer or bad program?

Hardgainers are beginners who gain very little muscle after starting a strength program. But a poorly structured program also fails to stimulate meaningful hypertrophy, so lack of progress could come from either. Every successful bodybuilding program has three features → [[Individual differences in muscle growth]].

## 1. Workout stimulus

Only the last few reps before failure stimulate much growth, so an inexperienced lifter who doesn't know to get that close to failure, or what it feels like, may stop sets before the stimulating reps → [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]. Extra sets can partly but not fully make up for it. The key is getting close to failure, not doing lots of sets.

In 18 recreationally trained men doing the same total volume twice a week for 6 weeks, 4 sets of 10 to failure grew the elbow flexors and vastus medialis, while 8 sets of 5 with the same load, stopping well short of failure, didn't.
[Karsten 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31365457/)

In 16 strength-trained people doing squats and bench presses 3 times a week for 6 weeks with similar volume, stopping sets at a higher velocity loss (closer to failure) grew the vastus lateralis 6% more than stopping at a lower velocity loss, though strength gains were similar.
[Myrholt 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36754062/)

## 2. Training frequency

Maintenance studies show that up to 4 sets to failure per muscle once a week maintains muscle rather than growing it, because of the atrophy between workouts → [[Detraining adaptations speed]]. So a bro-split training each muscle once a week can fail, especially if the workout stimulus is also suboptimal. By contrast, a single set to failure per muscle twice a week does produce hypertrophy.

In young women who had trained for 12 weeks, one session every 7 days for the next 12 weeks kept quadriceps size and strength unchanged, while one session every 14 days let them drop slightly, keeping about 94–96% of the gains.
[Mpampoulis 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11280775/)
→ [[Training frequency and hypertrophy]]

## 3. Recovery

Overreaching studies show progress stalls when training is extremely muscle-damaging, partly because fatigue lowers the stimulus and partly because repair takes resources away from growth → [[Junk volume]]. So too many sets could hold back growth, especially in people prone to muscle damage because they're more fast twitch and perhaps have naturally high voluntary activation.

In trained men whose volume rose from 10 to 32 sets per exercise a week over 6 weeks, lean mass corrected for fluid rose 1.18 kg in the first 3 weeks but only 0.25 kg (not significant) in the last 3, once volume passed about 20 sets per exercise a week.
[Haun 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30255024/)

In untrained people doing maximal eccentric biceps training every other day for 20 days, strength fell after 8 days and stayed down, suggesting recovery wasn't enough to repair the damage.
[Krentz 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20495928/)

## How to tell

People who don't grow after starting a program often call themselves hard gainers, but the program may be at fault. The most common errors are leaving too many reps in reserve, training each muscle only once a week, and doing so much volume that recovery doesn't happen between workouts. To check, try one set to failure per muscle twice a week. It has enough stimulus and frequency and is easy to recover from, so failing to grow on it for about three months would mean you're a hard gainer.

## Why some people are hardgainers

Whole muscle growth needs high single fiber [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]] and high motor unit recruitment, so that many fibers experience that tension. There are two obvious reasons a lifter on a good program might still grow slowly.

### Low motor unit recruitment

How many motor units people can recruit varies, so a low ability to recruit high-threshold motor units could make someone a hardgainer. In 13 men, 9 could voluntarily activate their quadriceps above 90% and the other 4 less, and the high activators showed a steeper rise in muscle activity with force.
[Herda 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23318003/)

The fix is to train in ways that maximize recruitment → [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]. Within a foolproof program, that means heavier loads, more stable and simpler exercises that use less total muscle mass (like single-joint, single-limb exercises), and boosting motivation with upbeat music, competitive training partners and trying to beat the logbook every session → [[Psychobiological model of fatigue]].

### More slow twitch fibers

Fibers grow until they reach a size limit set by the size principle, and the more oxidative (slow twitch) a fiber is, the smaller that limit. So muscles with more fast twitch fibers can grow more, and a lifter's fast twitch proportion predicts how much they grow → [[Individual differences in muscle growth]].

In 30 trained young men doing 6 weeks of high-volume training, the top 10 responders grew 10.7% while the bottom 10 shrank 2.1%. A higher starting proportion of type II fibers and smaller starting type II fiber size best predicted growth.
[Haun 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6445136/)

Converting slow fibers to fast is likely hard and slow, so there may not be much to do about fiber type directly. But better recruitment gives access to the fast twitch fibers a lifter does have, which raises growth that way.
