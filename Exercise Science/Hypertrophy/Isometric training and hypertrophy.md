> [!summary] Bottom line
> Isometric contractions stimulate hypertrophy the same way dynamic reps do. Growth needs high motor unit recruitment and high tension on each activated fiber. In an isometric contraction the fibers don't shorten, so tension on every activated fiber is high, and the only thing left to control is recruitment, which mostly comes from effort. A high-effort isometric held for more than a couple of seconds works like a heavy rep. The main practical problem is tracking progressive overload.

## What growth needs

Whole-muscle hypertrophy needs two things: high [[Increasing motor unit recruitment|motor unit recruitment]], so many fibers are activated, and high single fiber [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]], so the activated fibers get the growth stimulus. Recruitment has nothing to do with fiber tension. It only sets how many fibers are activated. Recruitment can be high while fiber tension is low, as in fast movements like jumping, which don't stimulate growth → [[Increasing speed]].

The force-velocity relationship is the main determinant of fiber tension. Fast shortening needs fast actin-myosin binding, which means earlier detachment, so fewer crossbridges are attached at any moment, and the attached crossbridges are what produce tension. So the faster a fiber shortens, the less force it produces. In single muscle fibers, each myosin motor kept a force of about 6 pN across a wide range of shortening speeds, and the force-velocity relationship came from fewer motors being attached at higher speeds.
[Piazzesi 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18022371/)
→ [[Mechanical tension]]

In rats given identical electrical stimulation patterns under anesthesia, isometric contractions caused twice the hypertrophy of contractions at 50–60% of isometric force, so the load on the fibers, not just their activation, drives growth.
[Eftestøl 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27488660/)

Relatively weak men doing maximal-effort jump squats with 0–30% of 1RM for 10 weeks improved jumping and sprinting as much as men doing heavy squats, but gained much less strength (squat 1RM +4.5% vs +31.2%).
[Cormie 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20139780/)

## Heavy reps vs isometrics

With heavy loads (1–5RM), high effort gives high motor unit recruitment while the slow movement keeps fiber tension high, which together give a meaningful stimulus. Moderate (6–15RM) and light (16–30RM) loads do the same, but only in the final reps of a set.

In an isometric contraction, fiber tension is always high because shortening velocity is zero, so most actin and myosin can bind to form cross bridges. The only factor left that affects the stimulus is motor unit recruitment, which is largely, but not entirely, set by effort. So any high-effort isometric held for more than a couple of seconds stimulates hypertrophy just like a heavy rep. Low-effort isometrics act like the early reps of a moderate or light set, until fatigue builds up enough to require more effort and so more recruitment.

Over 12 weeks, 40 isometric knee extensions 3 times a week, ramped up to 75% of maximum and held for 3 seconds, grew the quadriceps 8.1%, versus 2.6% for explosive 1-second contractions done as fast and hard as possible.
[Balshaw 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27055984/)

After 4 weeks of the same two kinds of training, maximal force rose 21% with the sustained contractions and 11% with the explosive ones, while only the explosive training improved early force (at 100 ms).
[Tillin 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24292019/)

In 7 people training each leg 3 times a week for 14 weeks at 70% of maximum, four 30-second isometric contractions increased muscle cross-sectional area, while 4 sets of ten 3-second contractions didn't. Isometric strength also rose more with the longer contractions (55% vs 32%).
[Schott 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8549577/)

## In practice

The only real challenge with isometric training is that there's no easy way to track progressive overload, which is why it's rarely programmed → [[Flexing and co-contraction training]].
