> [!summary] Bottom line
> Muscles grow by individual fibers getting thicker (hypertrophy) and longer ([[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]]), and each fiber responds only to the stimulus it senses itself. Fibers that are activated and produce high active tension thicken, and fibers whose sarcomeres are stretched far enough lengthen. So growth is fiber-specific, which is why it's uneven across a muscle's regions.

## Two ways fibers grow

Whole muscle growth happens mainly through two adaptations in individual fibers: more cross-sectional area (hypertrophy) and more length (sarcomerogenesis). Both are triggered by stimuli the individual fibers detect themselves, so not every fiber in a muscle adapts after a workout, only the ones that got the right stimulus.

## Hypertrophy is fiber-specific

Hypertrophy adds myofibrils in parallel to a fiber's existing sarcomeres. Myofibrils are added when mechanoreceptors at the fiber's membrane detect high active [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]] from actin-myosin cross bridges, which happens when a fiber is activated and shortens slowly. So only fibers activated during a contraction grow.

Mechanical signals are the leading candidates for the hypertrophy stimulus, with proposed sensors including a filamin-C-BAG3 pathway, nuclear deformation and the costameres, which link the sarcomeres to the cell membrane. Muscle damage probably isn't needed for hypertrophy.
[Wackerhage 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335577/)

Costameres transmit force sideways from the sarcomeres to the membrane and surrounding matrix, and relay mechanical signals into the cell, which can regulate myofibril growth.
[Peter 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3770312/)

Tension depends on how many cross bridges are attached: in single fibers, each myosin motor's force stayed about the same at different speeds, and slower shortening meant more motors attached.
[Piazzesi 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18022371/)

In rats given identical electrical activation, isometric contractions caused twice the hypertrophy of contractions at 50–60% of isometric force, so the tension a fiber experiences, not just its activation, drives growth → [[Isometric training and hypertrophy]].
[Eftestøl 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27488660/)

Hypertrophy happens preferentially in the regions of a muscle where fibers are activated. In 12 men, one session of elbow extensions activated a smaller share of the distal triceps than its middle and proximal regions on MRI, and in another 12 men, 12 weeks of the same training grew the distal triceps less too.
[Wakahara 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21858666/)

## Sarcomerogenesis is fiber-specific

Sarcomerogenesis adds new sarcomeres to the ends of existing fibers. They're added when titin inside a fiber is stretched far enough to generate enough passive tension, which happens when the fiber's sarcomeres are stretched past a threshold length. So only fibers whose sarcomeres stretch that far get longer.

In mice with a stiffer titin spring, the extra passive tension led to about 30% more sarcomeres in series.
[Brynnel 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6300359/)

A computer model of muscle growth driven by passive stretch predicted that after a limb is lengthened, sarcomeres first stretch from 3.09 to 3.51 µm and then return to their original length within two weeks as sarcomeres are added, matching experimental data.
[Zöllner 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23049683/)

Sarcomerogenesis happens preferentially where fibers lengthen the most during eccentric contractions. In rabbits given 6 weeks of eccentric exercise with identical whole-muscle stretch, sarcomere addition differed between protocols and wasn't uniform across the muscle. It was best predicted by peak torque and fiber strain, not by how much the whole muscle-tendon unit stretched.
[Butterfield 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16133258/)
