> [!summary] Bottom line
> Slowing the lifting (concentric) phase makes a set take longer to reach failure, but it doesn't increase the hypertrophy stimulus. Lifting more slowly than you can means using less force, which means activating fewer fibers. The stimulating reps at the end of a set already need maximal effort, so they can't be slowed deliberately, and only the non-stimulating early reps can. Trials find similar growth with rep durations from 0.5 to 8 seconds.

## The claim

An influential group of bodybuilders held that slowing the concentric phase increases the hypertrophy stimulus of a set by increasing time under tension (TUT). Slowing the tempo does make a set with a given percentage of 1RM take longer to reach failure, but it doesn't improve the stimulus. Long-term training studies show this, and it follows from basic muscle physiology.

Comparing three tempos over 5 sets, a regular tempo (2 s down, 2 s up) allowed more reps per set, while slower tempos (5 s down and 3 s up, or 6 s down and 4 s up) gave more total time under tension.
[Wilk 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29922395/)

In a meta-analysis of 8 studies with sets taken to failure, growth was similar with repetition durations from 0.5 to 8 seconds, and very slow durations (over 10 seconds per rep) seemed inferior.
[Schoenfeld 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/)

## Why slowing down doesn't help

To slow the lifting phase, you have to reduce the bar's acceleration, and since F = ma, that means reducing whole muscle force. Whole muscle force depends on the [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]] of each fiber and the number of activated fibers. Slower shortening increases each fiber's tension, so the only way to reduce whole muscle force while slowing down is to activate fewer fibers, by lowering central motor command, which feels like less effort. So deliberately slow reps are done with submaximal [[Increasing motor unit recruitment|motor unit recruitment]], leaving some fibers at the top of the motor unit pool inactive, and inactive fibers can't be stimulated to grow → [[Fiber-specific muscle growth]].

## Stimulating reps can't be slowed

With moderate (6–15RM) and light (16–30RM) loads, a set has reps that don't stimulate hypertrophy and reps that do. The stimulating reps come at the end of the set, where recruitment is high, so many fibers are active, and the bar moves slowly, so fiber tension is high. Those reps need maximal recruitment to complete, so they can't be slowed deliberately: lowering motor command to slow them down would make them fail. So the only reps that can be slowed are the ones outside the stimulating zone, which can't stimulate hypertrophy anyway.
