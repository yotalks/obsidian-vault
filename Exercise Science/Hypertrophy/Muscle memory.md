> [!summary] Bottom line
> Regaining lost muscle is faster than building it the first time. Two mechanisms are proposed: the ability to recruit high-threshold motor units, which isn't lost during detraining, and retained myonuclei. A third, epigenetic changes, is still being studied. Muscle growth may be about twice as fast the second time, more than better recruitment alone could explain, but a human trial found a previously trained leg didn't regrow faster than an untrained one.

## Muscle memory

Bodybuilders have long noticed that regaining lost muscle is easier and faster than gaining it the first time, which is called muscle memory. Researchers first identified two mechanisms. More recently they've studied a third, epigenetic changes to muscle fibers that may prime them for faster growth after detraining, though a link between those changes and faster regrowth hasn't been shown conclusively.

A review describes two kinds: cellular muscle memory (myonuclei) and epigenetic muscle memory, and how they might work together.
[Sharples 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37154489/)

## 1. Retained motor unit recruitment

When we start training, our ability to recruit high-threshold motor units is limited, so even maximal efforts can't switch on the highest motor units with the most responsive fast twitch fibers. Over time that ability improves, which raises both the potential and the rate of growth, because each fiber grows independently and more activated fibers means faster hypertrophy → [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]].

This ability doesn't decline with detraining, so muscle lost during a break comes back faster because the most responsive fibers are still accessible → [[Fiber-specific muscle growth]].
→ [[Detraining adaptations speed]]

In 8 people doing 3 months of isometric knee extension training followed by 3 months off, strength rose 41% and neural activation 9%. During detraining, muscle size returned to baseline within a month and tendon stiffness within two, but strength and neural activation didn't fall.
[Kubo 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996769/)

After 6 weeks of one-leg calf training, strength and voluntary activation rose in both the trained and untrained legs, and the untrained leg's gains didn't fall during 6 weeks of detraining.
[Shima 2002](https://europepmc.org/article/med/11990741)
→ [[Detraining adaptations speed]]

## 2. Retained myonuclei

Growing fibers add myonuclei, and these aren't completely lost during a break, so retraining can grow fibers faster without adding new ones. In this model, untrained fibers recruit myonuclei from satellite cells before growing, keep them even through severe atrophy, and grow faster when overloaded again. In humans myonuclei are stable for at least 15 years.
[Gundersen 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26792335/)

This is the more popular hypothesis, but muscle memory seems to happen even when myonuclei aren't added or lost, which suggests retained access to high-threshold motor units matters more. In 19 people who trained one leg for 10 weeks, detrained for 20 and then retrained both legs for 5, the trained leg's fiber size, muscle thickness and strength rose but myonuclear number didn't change. After detraining, muscle size was back to baseline but strength stayed about 60% elevated, and during retraining the previously trained leg gained muscle thickness and strength similarly to the untrained leg.
[Psilander 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30991013/)

## How much faster?

Long-term training studies suggest muscle grows about twice as fast during retraining as during initial training. How much of that comes from recruitment versus other mechanisms is unclear, but recruitment alone is unlikely to explain it. Doubling the area of fibers that can respond would need beginners to have voluntary activation deficits of 25–30% and trained lifters to reach full activation.

In reality, beginners have deficits of around 15–20% in the largest muscles, and trained lifters still have small ones. Within 12 men, the quadriceps had the largest activation deficit (15.5%), compared with 5% in the elbow flexors and plantar flexors and 1.3% in the dorsiflexors.
[Behm 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11870692/)

So muscle memory is likely multi-factorial, with two or three underlying mechanisms.
