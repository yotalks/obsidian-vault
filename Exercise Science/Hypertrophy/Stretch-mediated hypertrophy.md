> [!summary] Bottom line
> Not every muscle grows better when trained at long lengths. A muscle needs two things for stretch-mediated hypertrophy: good enough leverage at long lengths to be activated there, and fibers with few enough sarcomeres in series that stretching them strains titin and makes passive tension. The quads, hamstrings, glutes and pecs meet both, while muscles like the brachioradialis, anterior delts, lats and soleus are hard to activate when stretched. The biceps, triceps and traps are thought to have too many sarcomeres in series.

## What it is

Stretch-mediated hypertrophy is muscle growth caused by stretching forces, or passive [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]], on muscle fibers. Passive tension comes from stretching titin, the giant spring-like protein inside each sarcomere. Unless static stretches go to extreme ranges of motion, the only way to stretch titin far enough is to activate the fibers before stretching them, because activation changes titin's conformation so even small stretches of the fiber stretch titin a long way.

One hypothesis is that calcium-dependent binding of titin to the thin filaments stiffens it during activation, and that cross bridges wind titin onto the thin filaments, storing energy during force development and active stretch.
[Nishikawa 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3259925/)

In mice with a shortened titin spring, titin turned out to be the main source of muscle's passive stiffness, and the stiffer titin led to about 30% more sarcomeres in series (longitudinal hypertrophy).
[Brynnel 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6300359/)

## Requirement 1: activation at long lengths

The first requirement is leverage. Under [[Neuromechanical matching|neuromechanical matching]], the nervous system recruits the muscles with the best leverage for the movement at each joint angle, so a muscle with poor leverage at long lengths won't be activated there and gets no stretch-mediated stimulus.
[Hudson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985474/)

Common examples:
- the gluteus maximus in squats, because the adductor magnus has better leverage → [[Gluteus maximus training]]
- the brachioradialis in biceps curls, because the biceps brachii has better leverage → [[Elbow flexor training]]
- the anterior deltoid in wide-grip overhead presses, because the middle deltoid has better leverage → [[Deltoid training]]
- the latissimus dorsi in narrow-grip pull-downs, pull-ups and pull-overs, because the costal pectoralis major, triceps long head and posterior deltoid have better leverage → [[Latissimus dorsi training]]
- the soleus in calf raises, because the gastrocnemius has better leverage → [[Calves training]]

The gluteus maximus has its best leverage and the plateau of its length-tension relationship at short lengths, while the gastrocnemius has both at long lengths.
[Hale 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21145061/)

## Requirement 2: few sarcomeres in series

Even a muscle activated at long lengths may not get stretch-mediated hypertrophy, because passive tension is generated inside individual sarcomeres, the units lined up along a fiber like the carriages of a train. When a fiber has few sarcomeres in series, stretching it stretches each sarcomere, and its titin, a lot, producing high passive tension. When a fiber has many, each sarcomere stretches only a little and produces little passive tension. So the number of sarcomeres in series sets how much stretch-mediated hypertrophy is possible.

Measured during surgery, human vastus lateralis sarcomeres were about 3.2 µm long with the knee bent 45° and 3.8 µm at 90°, and vastus medialis sarcomeres 3.6 to 4.1 µm.
[Son 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29258751/)

Musculoskeletal modelling has been used to estimate the optimal fiber lengths of 26 upper limb muscle groups.
[Garner 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12627828/)

Training at long lengths adds sarcomeres in series ([[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]]). Rats walking downhill, with eccentric contractions at long lengths, gained serial sarcomeres in their knee extensors, while rats walking uphill lost them.
[Butterfield 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947030/)

In people, 3 weeks of eccentric hamstring training with the hip flexed 80° lengthened fascicles 9.3%, versus 4.9% with the hip straight.
[Guex 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4971444/)

## Which muscles

- **Probably can't:** the biceps brachii, triceps brachii and trapezius, which have too many sarcomeres in series.
- **Definitely can:** the quadriceps, hamstrings, gluteus maximus and pectoralis major.
- **May a little:** the latissimus dorsi, deltoids and calves (the calf data conflict).

A review of range-of-motion studies found that muscles working on the descending limb of their length-tension curve grew more with larger ranges of motion, while muscles that don't reach the descending limb grew similarly either way.
[Ottinger 2023](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000737)
## Conclusion

For a muscle to get stretch-mediated hypertrophy, it needs good leverage in stretched positions so it's activated at long lengths, and fibers with few sarcomeres in series so its sarcomeres can generate a lot of passive tension. The most stretch-mediated hypertrophy likely happens in the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, hamstrings and pectoralis major.
