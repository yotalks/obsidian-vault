> [!summary] Bottom line
> Full range of motion doesn't always grow more muscle than partials. Two things decide it. First, neuromechanical matching: peak effort at different joint angles favors different muscles or regions, so a partial with its peak effort where the full range peaks grows the same muscles. Second, sarcomerogenesis: stretched-position effort can add growth, but only in muscles that reach the descending limb of their length-tension curve, and mainly in untrained lifters. So a stretched-position partial can match or beat full range, while a contracted-position partial may fall short.

## Why the old view changed

For years, hypertrophy researchers said full range of motion (ROM) always grows more muscle than partial ROM. That assumed training status, muscle group and resistance profile don't affect the result. Physiologists predicted otherwise, and later research agreed. The differences come from [[Neuromechanical matching|neuromechanical matching]] and [[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]] ([[Stretch-mediated hypertrophy|stretch-mediated hypertrophy]]).

## 1. Neuromechanical matching

Different parts of a joint's range of motion preferentially activate different muscles or muscle regions. So if a full ROM exercise has its peak effort at a different joint angle from a partial ROM exercise, they'll grow different muscles or regions.
[Hudson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985474/)
→ [[Neuromechanical matching]]

For example, flat bench presses with full ROM preferentially activate the pectoralis major, while contracted-position partials (the top half) preferentially activate the triceps. Stretched-position partials still favor the pecs, so they should grow muscle much like the full ROM version → [[Pectoralis major training]].

In 19 men doing 10RM bench presses, the top-half range produced the highest mean triceps activity, with higher peak activity in the triceps long and lateral heads. Both half ranges gave higher mean pectoralis major and anterior deltoid activity than full range, and peak activity in one region of the sternocostal pecs was highest in the top half.
[Fischer 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12019332/)

## 2. Sarcomerogenesis

Exercises with peak effort in the stretched position can stimulate sarcomerogenesis in some muscles and in untrained lifters. It's stimulated separately from adding myofibrils, so it can add to overall growth. But it doesn't happen in muscles whose sarcomeres don't reach the descending limb of the length-tension relationship, or in trained lifters already used to stretched-position exercises. So a full ROM exercise with peak effort in the stretched position, done by an untrained lifter in a responsive muscle, could grow more than a contracted-position partial, but about the same as a stretched-position partial.
→ [[Stretch-mediated hypertrophy]]

A review of range-of-motion studies found that muscles working on the descending limb of their length-tension curve grew more with larger ranges of motion, while muscles that don't reach it grew similarly either way.
[Ottinger 2023](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000737)

For example, in untrained lifters doing knee extensions, full ROM and stretched-position partials should both combine myofibril addition with sarcomerogenesis, while contracted-position partials only add myofibrils → [[Quadriceps training]]. In 45 untrained women training knee extensions, partials from 100° to 65° of knee flexion (the stretched half) grew the quadriceps more than full ROM (100° to 30°) in several regions, while partials from 65° to 30° (the contracted half) grew no more than the untrained control group at the 60% and 70% regions of the thigh.
[Pedrosa 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33977835/)

## Conclusion

Range of motion affects growth because neuromechanical matching favors different muscles or regions depending on where peak effort happens, and because sarcomerogenesis only happens with peak effort in stretched positions, and then only in untrained lifters training certain muscles.
