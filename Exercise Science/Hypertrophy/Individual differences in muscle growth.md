> [!summary] Bottom line
> Two untrained lifters can grow at very different rates for two obvious reasons. One may already have well-developed muscle from manual labor or sports, leaving less room to grow. The other may have more slow twitch fibers, which grow more slowly and top out smaller because their size is capped by oxidative capacity.

Why some people grow muscle faster than others isn't fully resolved, but there are two clear reasons rates could differ between two untrained people.

## Starting point

Someone who has never lifted may still have well-developed muscle from manual labor or strength-heavy sports like rock climbing or gymnastics. The muscle fibers of their early high-threshold motor units may already be fully grown, so only the fibers of the later high-threshold motor units still have room to grow. They start from a higher point and grow more slowly.
→ [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]

Differences in hormones don't explain it either. In trained men, the rise in anabolic hormones after a workout wasn't related to how much muscle or strength they gained.
[Morton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27174923/)

## Fiber type

The other reason is a higher proportion of slow twitch fibers. Fast twitch fibers grow faster and reach larger maximum sizes than slow twitch fibers. In rats, the fast plantaris muscle grew faster than the slow soleus after the same overload, with bigger increases in ribosomes, protein synthesis and satellite cells.
[Roberts 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31850817/)
### The size principle

Fiber size is limited by oxidative capacity. A fiber always needs to keep some ATP available to survive, so its rate of ATP use (by excitation-contraction coupling and crossbridge cycling) can't exceed its rate of ATP production, which depends on oxygen and nutrients diffusing in from the capillaries.
- Supply depends on diffusion across the cell membrane, so it scales with the fiber's **surface area**.
- Demand depends on the amount of myofibrils, so it scales with the fiber's **volume**.

As a fiber grows, its volume rises faster than its surface area, so at some diameter ATP demand would outstrip supply. Fibers tend to stop growing at that diameter, since growing further would risk running out of ATP and dying. Slow twitch fibers use oxygen faster, so they hit this limit at a smaller diameter. Across animals from mice to elephants, fiber diameter closely tracks oxidative capacity.
[van der Laarse 1998](https://brill.com/view/journals/njz/48/3/article-p213_2.xml)

### Fiber type runs across the whole body

People who have mostly slow fibers in one muscle tend to have mostly slow fibers in others too (median correlation 0.42 across muscles). So a slow-twitch-dominant lifter can grow more slowly across the whole body, not just in one muscle.
[Vikne 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22431086/)
