> [!summary] Bottom line
> There's no leverage data for the trapezius, so how to train it rests on anatomy and EMG. The upper trapezius attaches to the clavicle, not the scapula, and its fibers run mostly sideways, so it can't lift the scapula directly the way it's usually drawn. It's highly active across many shoulder movements, so pressing and pulling probably train it enough. The middle and lower trapezius pull the scapulae back (retraction), so shrug variations that retract the scapulae should train them. Many surface EMG studies labeled "upper trapezius" may actually be recording the middle region.

## Little to go on

The best guide to which exercise trains a muscle region is how its [[Internal moment arm|leverage]] changes across joint angles. No such data exists for the trapezius, so choosing exercises for it has to rely on anatomy and [[EMG for exercise selection|EMG]] studies.

## Anatomy

Dissections show three groups of fibers:
- **Upper:** fibers from the skull and the ligament along the back of the neck (ligamentum nuchae) run down but mostly sideways to insert on the clavicle, not the scapula.
- **Middle:** fibers from the C7 and T1 vertebrae run sideways to the acromion and the spine of the scapula. These were the largest by volume.
- **Lower:** fibers from the thoracic vertebrae converge on the inner end of the scapula's spine.

Because the upper and middle fibers run mostly sideways, the authors concluded they can't lift the scapula directly, as textbooks usually show. Instead they pull the scapula and clavicle backward, or raise the scapula indirectly by rotating the clavicle around the joint where it meets the sternum. By balancing these forces, the trapezius also takes compressive load off the neck.
[Johnson 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916077/)

## Problems with trapezius EMG

The standard surface EMG guideline (SENIAM) places the "upper trapezius" electrode halfway along the line from the acromion to the C7 vertebra.
[Reynard 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6106764/)

By the anatomy above, the fibers along that line come from C7 and run to the acromion, which makes them middle fibers. So surface EMG studies that follow this guideline and report the upper trapezius may mostly be recording the middle trapezius. Fine-wire and high-density EMG studies don't rely on that single electrode site.

The upper trapezius doesn't act as one block either. With 8 electrode pairs spaced across it from the clavicle back to the scapula, the part nearest the clavicle was most active when raising the arm out to the side to 90°, while the parts further back were most active both in that and in shrugging.
[Jensen 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20719653/)

At low activation levels, activity also differed between electrode positions, so the authors recommend recording from several positions on the upper trapezius.
[Jensen 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9349648/)

## Upper trapezius

The upper trapezius is highly active across shoulder movements, not just shrugging:
- With fine-wire and surface EMG in 26 people, upper trapezius activity was high and similar in a weighted shrug with the arms at the sides, a shrug with the arms overhead, and scapular retraction with the arms overhead.
[Castelein 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26409441/)
- With high-density EMG in 20 people, the upper trapezius region was more active when pulling upward while lying face down with the arm overhead (the lower trapezius test) than when pulling the arm toward the ear (the upper trapezius test), even though the second test produced about 8 times the force (435 N vs about 50 N).
[Henderson 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8530421/)

Neither study measured presses or rows. That pressing and pulling train the upper trapezius enough is an inference from how active it is across shoulder movements.

## Middle and lower trapezius

The middle and lower fibers also run mostly sideways but insert on the scapula, so they're well placed to retract it. Shrugging also involves some retraction.

Both regions were most active in retraction tasks:
- The middle and lower trapezius (and the rhomboids) were significantly more active in overhead retraction than in shrugs. [Castelein 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26409441/)
- The middle and lower regions were more active in face-down pulls with the arm out to the side or overhead than in the shrug-like upper trapezius test. [Henderson 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8530421/)

## In practice

Retraction can be trained horizontally or vertically:
- **Kelso shrug:** retract the scapulae in a chest-supported or T-bar row machine, using a bit more weight than for rows. It's easy to set up.
- **Conventional shrug:** needs much heavier loads, so it takes longer to set up.

Wide-grip rows probably aren't enough for the middle and lower trapezius, because the trapezius isn't what limits the lift. You can retract more weight in a Kelso shrug than you can row in the same machine.
