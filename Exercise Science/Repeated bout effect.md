> [!summary] Bottom line
> One workout sharply reduces the fatigue and soreness from the next similar workout, by reducing muscle damage and excitation-contraction coupling failure rather than CNS fatigue. After workout two, further reductions are small.

The repeated bout effect is the observation that a single strength training workout reduces the amount of sustained fatigue (and muscle soreness) that is produced by a subsequent, similar strength training workout for the same muscle group.
[Hyldahl 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27782911/)

The repeated bout effect refers to the effect produced by a single workout. Therefore, anyone who has completed a single workout has experienced the repeated bout effect. It is NOT the case that more well-trained lifters have a greater repeated bout effect than less well-trained lifters. If they both have done a single workout, they both have the same repeated bout effect. And we know that doing a single workout is certainly not enough to stop post-workout fatigue from happening.
[Nosaka 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11782649/)

## Which post workout fatigue mechanisms underpin the RBE?

Post workout fatigue come from muscle damage, excitation-contraction coupling failure, and central nervous fatigue ([[Glossary#CNS|CNS]]) fatigue. CNS fatigue after a strength training workout does not seem to be much lower in trained individuals compared to in untrained individuals.
[Fernandes 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6628445/)

Post-workout CNS fatigue is caused by the inflammatory response to muscle damage. Therefore, this tells us either that the amount of muscle damage experienced by untrained and trained individuals is similar, or that the inflammatory response per unit of muscle damage that is experienced after a strength training workout is greater in trained individuals than in untrained individuals. This question can be answer because researchers have shown that the structural damage is greater in untrained individuals than in trained individuals after a strength training workout.
[Gibala 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7759443/)
→ [[Impaired muscle regrowth]]

Aligned with this, rodent studies have shown that reductions in the amount of muscle damage after a workout are reduced in tandem with the repeated bout effect.
[SCIRP rodent study](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=106270)

Also, the inflammatory response after a strength training workout is greater after a repeated bout of exercise compared to after the initial bout. This explains why CNS fatigue is similar in trained vs. untrained lifters despite untrained lifters having more muscle damage.
[Deyhle 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4709832/)

Lastly, we have data telling us that excitation-contraction coupling failure is reduced as a result of the repeated bout effect. This means the repeated bout effect involves a reduction in both peripheral muscular fatigue mechanisms but not in CNS fatigues.
[Janecki 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5131224/)

Also, high intensity exercise can damage the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which is likely what causes the excitation contraction coupling failure to produce a fatiguing effect.
[Place 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26575622/)

Also, sarcoplasmic reticulum damage doesn't happen after repeated bouts and has been linked to improvements in fatigue resistance.
[Schlittler 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31250088/)

## How do changes in calcium ion related fatigue occur?

The two muscular fatigue mechanisms are both caused by the accumulation of calcium ions inside the muscle fiber. Therefore, anything that reduces the accumulation of calcium ions will produce a repeated bout effect.
[Takagi 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29595913/)

Some data has shown that the repeated bout effect involves a reduction in the amount of muscle fiber stretch. This would reduce the amount of calcium ions released into the cytoplasm in eccentric contractions through stretch-activated ion channels.
[Lau 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6195649/)

[[Sarcomerogenesis|Sarcomerogenesis]] might cause the same effect by causing less lengthening of individual sarcomeres. Interesting that while eccentric training causes a repeated bout effect for eccentric workouts, a sustained block of concentric training removes this effect in this respect.
[Kawashima 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33764172/)

We also see here the repeated bout effect could involve an increase in the amount of calcium ions that are removed from the cytoplasm by the mitochondria, because this could easily occur through small shifts in muscle fiber type from the highly glycolytic type IIX muscle fibers to the less glycolytic and more oxidative type IIA muscle fibers. Data has shown that type IIX muscle fibers are directed to start changing type immediately after even just one strength training workout.
[Andersen 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28508505/)

They've also shown that muscle fibers do show a shift in muscle fiber type (such that the oxidative capacity of the muscle fiber is increased) in tandem with the repeated bout effect.
[Takagi 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27469054/)

## Multiple repeated bout effect

The [[Glossary#RBE|RBE]] is the technical term given to the observation that a second workout produces mush less fatigue (as well as much less [[Glossary#DOMS|DOMS]]) than an initial workout. The RBE has been very well studied but much less is known about the changes in the amount of fatigue and DOMS that occur over subsequent workouts.

The RBE is the well described observation that post workout fatigue reduces a lot from workout one to workout two. It does NOT tell us about changes in post-workout fatigue from workout two onwards.

Post workout fatigue doesn't reduce from workout two to workout three. This tells us that the RBE is mainly a phenomenon that describes the reduction in fatigue (and DOMS) from workout one to workout two. It doesn't describe the continuous process that leads to progressively less fatigue over a series of workouts at the start of a strength training program.
[Lindsay 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33337696/)

Although post-workout fatigue doesn't appear to reduce from the second to the third workout, there is data showing a general trend for a small reduction in post-workout fatigue from the first workout to subsequent workouts. In contrast, DOMS appears to have the vast majority of the changes from workout one to workout two and only minimal changes after.
[Chen 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19263073/)

There are three main post-workout fatigue mechanisms after any fatiguing bout of exercise: myofibrillar damage, excitation-contraction coupling failure, and [[Supraspinal CNS fatigue|supraspinal CNS fatigue]]. Researchers have often noted that the amount of myofibrillar damage is much reduced entrained individuals compared to in untrained, which suggests that it could contribute to the multiple RBE.
[Gibala 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10958167/)

In contrast, supraspinal CNS fatigue doesn't seem to vary greatly between trained and untrained, which strongly tells us that it cannot contribute meaningfully to the multiple RBE.
[Fernandes 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6628445/)

It seems likely that the maintenance of similar levels of supraspinal CNS fatigue in trained individuals as in untrained is the result of a greater sensitization of the inflammatory response to the presence of myofibrillar damage. Thus, the inflammatory response in trained subjects that causes supraspinal CNS fatigue is greater for a given amount of myofibrillar damage.
[Deyhle 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4709832/)

Data has also shown that [[Glossary#ECCF|ECCF]] doesn't reduce very much over a series of workouts at the start of a strength training program. Rather, it remains at a fairly consistent level despite overall post-workout fatigue reducing. This can be seen by the lack of change in low frequency fatigue despite high frequency fatigue reducing progressively over a series of the workouts This tells us that ECCF doesn't contribute tot the multiple RBE either.
[Ingalls 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15121748/)
