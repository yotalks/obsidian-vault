> [!summary] Bottom line
> The lats have two regions that need different exercises. The upper (thoracic) region has its best leverage for pulling the arm down in front of the body, peaking about 45° from the anatomical position, so narrow-grip rows train it best. The lower (lumbopelvic) region has its best leverage for pulling the arm down to the side, peaking about 60°, so wide-grip pull-ups and pulldowns train it best.

## Regions act separately

The lat isn't activated as one block. Different regions of it are activated with some independence, depending on which has the best leverage for the task. The lower region was preferentially activated during adduction.
[Paton 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7498076/)
→ [[Neuromechanical matching]]

## Two regions

In cadaver measurements, moment arms differed between regions of the lat, and its middle and lower fibers (from the lumbar spine and iliac crest) were among the most effective shoulder adductors.
[Ackland 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2644775/)

- **Upper (thoracic) region:** good leverage for shoulder extension in the sagittal plane (pulling the arm down in front of you), peaking at about 45° from the arms-at-sides position. Its leverage drops quickly once the arms go above shoulder height, and is almost gone by 120°. So narrow-grip rows train it best, especially with a resistance profile that's hardest at about 45° of shoulder elevation.
- **Lower (lumbopelvic) region:** good leverage for shoulder adduction in the frontal plane (pulling the arm down to your side), peaking at about 60° from the arms-at-sides position. All wide-grip pull-ups and pulldowns suit it, especially with a resistance profile that's hardest at about 60° of shoulder elevation, roughly when the hands are at eye level.

Lat activation does change with shoulder angle: in pullovers and pulldowns held at five positions from 0° to 180°, lat EMG differed significantly between positions, highest with the arm between the side and 45° and lowest at 135–180°, overhead.
[Teixeira 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9362894/)
