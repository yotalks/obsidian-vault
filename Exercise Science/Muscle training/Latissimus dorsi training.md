> [!summary] Bottom line
> The lats have two regions that need different exercises. The upper (thoracic) region has its best leverage for pulling the arm down in front of the body, peaking about 45° from the anatomical position, so narrow-grip rows train it best. The lower (lumbopelvic) region has its best leverage for pulling the arm down to the side, peaking about 60°, so wide-grip pull-ups and pulldowns train it best.

## Regions act separately

The lat isn't activated as one block. Different regions of it are activated with some independence, depending on which has the best leverage for the task. The lower region was preferentially activated during adduction.
[Paton 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7498076/)
→ [[Neuromechanical matching]]

## Two regions

In cadaver measurements, moment arms differed between regions of the lat, and its middle and lower fibers (from the lumbar spine and iliac crest) were among the most effective shoulder adductors.
[Ackland 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2644775/)

- **Upper (thoracic) region:** good leverage for shoulder extension in the sagittal plane (pulling the arm down in front of you), peaking at about 45° from the arms-at-sides position. Its leverage drops quickly once the arms go above shoulder height, and is almost gone by 120°. So narrow-grip rows train it best, especially with a resistance profile that's hardest at about 45° of shoulder elevation.
- **Lower (lumbopelvic) region:** good leverage for shoulder adduction in the frontal plane (pulling the arm down to your side), peaking at about 60° from the arms-at-sides position. All wide-grip pull-ups and pulldowns suit it, especially with a resistance profile that's hardest at about 60° of shoulder elevation, roughly when the hands are at eye level.

Lat activation does change with shoulder angle: in pullovers and pulldowns held at five positions from 0° to 180°, lat EMG differed significantly between positions, highest with the arm between the side and 45° and lowest at 135–180°, overhead.
[Teixeira 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9362894/)

## Home gym back exercises

The best exercise for a muscle or region requires maximal effort in the position where that muscle or region has the most leverage. The brain sends neural drive to whichever muscle does the movement most efficiently, and since joint torque is muscle force times leverage, a muscle with better leverage makes more torque for the same force → [[Neuromechanical matching]].
[Hudson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985474/)

- **Upper latissimus dorsi:** works more pulling in the sagittal plane (arms moving down in front of the body), with peak leverage at 45–60° from the arms at the sides. A narrow-grip row, like an incline chest-supported dumbbell row or a narrow-grip inverted row with a weighted vest, is ideal.
- **Lower latissimus dorsi:** works more pulling in the frontal plane (arms moving down out to the sides), with peak leverage at 60–90°. A wide-grip pull-up is better.
- **Posterior deltoid:** pulls down in the sagittal plane and back in the transverse plane, with the best leverage of any muscle through the whole range in both. So any narrow-grip row trains it along with the upper lats → [[Deltoid training]].
- **Trapezius:** mainly pulls the shoulder blades back and together rather than up, so it doesn't need vertical barbell shrugs. Horizontal shrugs work instead: from the arms-extended position of a chest-supported dumbbell row or narrow-grip inverted row, shrug the shoulder blades back. This needs more weight than the row but far less than vertical barbell shrugs → [[Trapezius training]].

In cadaver shoulders, the posterior deltoid and teres major had the largest shoulder extensor moment arms, and the middle and lower lats were among the most effective adductors.
[Ackland 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)

The upper and middle trapezius fibers run mostly horizontally, which rules out elevating the scapula as commonly shown. Instead they draw the scapula and clavicle backward, or raise the scapula by rotating the clavicle.
[Johnson 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916077/)
