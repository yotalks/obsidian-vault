> [!summary] Bottom line
> Lean mass gains rise steeply with protein up to about 1.2–1.6 g/kg of body weight a day and much more slowly after that. About 1.4 g/kg/day is a reasonable minimum when training for hypertrophy, with diminishing returns above it, and about 1.2 g/kg/day is likely enough for untrained people. Tracer studies put bodybuilders' requirement at about 1.7–2.2 g/kg/day, and going far above that doesn't add muscle.

## Dose-response

A meta analysis looked at protein intakes in subgroups based on physical activity and created dose-response curves for protein intakes as they relate to changes in fat free mass between trained and untrained subjects. [Tagawa 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33300582/)

Across 105 trials (5,402 people), each extra 0.1 g/kg/day of protein was linked to 0.39 kg more lean mass below a total intake of 1.3 g/kg/day, and 0.12 kg more above it. Several of the authors work for Meiji, a Japanese food and dairy company.

 Based on these dose-response curves, no matter what, it's ideal to get 1.2g/kg body weight per day. But, if you're training, the benefits seem to linearly increase with higher protein intakes.

## Where gains plateau

These results are somewhat consistent with this regression analysis of a previous meta-analysis that found changes in FFM plateaued at around 1.6g/kg bw per day for trained subjects.
![[Pasted image 20261102005206.png]]
[Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)

Across 49 trials (1,863 people), protein supplementation added 0.30 kg of fat-free mass during resistance training, with no further gains above about 1.62 g/kg/day, and it helped resistance-trained people more than untrained people. The senior author has received grants and fees from the US National Dairy Council, which supported trials in the analysis.

In that regression the break point was 1.62 g/kg/day, with a 95% CI of 1.03 to 2.20, so some people may benefit from up to about 2.2 g/kg/day.

## Requirements from tracer studies

The indicator amino acid oxidation (IAAO) method estimates protein requirements by finding the intake where oxidation of a labeled amino acid stops falling. In 8 healthy young men, it put the average requirement at 0.93 g/kg/day and the population-safe intake (RDA) at 1.2. Reanalyzing older nitrogen balance data with the same two-phase regression gave similar values (0.91 and 1.0). Both are well above the official average requirement of 0.66 and RDA of 0.80 g/kg/day.
[Humayun 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17921376/)

In 8 young male bodybuilders on a rest day, the IAAO method put the average requirement at 1.7 g/kg/day and the upper 95% CI (used for an RDA) at 2.2 g/kg/day, about 2.6 times the official recommendation.
[Bandegan 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28179492/)

So the upper limit matches the 2.2 g/kg from the Morton regression, but 1.6 g/kg falls just below the IAAO average requirement.

## Very high intakes
→ [[Protein and testosterone]]

In resistance-trained men and women told to keep their training and diets otherwise the same, eating 4.4 g/kg/day of protein for 8 weeks (about 307 g a day, versus 1.8 g/kg in the control group) didn't change body weight, fat mass or fat-free mass, despite the extra calories.
[Antonio 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24834017/)

In a follow-up with a periodized heavy training program, the high-protein group (3.4 g/kg/day) lost more fat (1.7 vs 0.3 kg) than the normal-protein group (2.3 g/kg/day), with the same gain in fat-free mass (1.5 kg each) and strength. But 93% of the high-protein group were compliant with the training program, versus 78% of the normal-protein group, so the fat loss could have come from better training adherence rather than the extra protein. Randomization was also deliberately unequal (31 vs 17 finished), and the high-protein group had more training experience (4.9 vs 2.4 years). A beef protein company donated protein for the study.
[Antonio 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26500462/)

Intakes above about 3 g/kg/day don't seem to build more muscle, and any benefit for fat loss is uncertain.

## Plant protein

At around 1.6 g/kg/day, vegans and omnivores gain the same muscle → [[Plant vs animal protein]].

## Conclusion

We can average this and say around 1.4g/kg bw per day is a reasonable minimum target if you're training for hypertrophy. There are probably benefits with higher intakes, though diminishing returns. If one is sedentary or untrained, 1.2g/kg/day is likely enough.
