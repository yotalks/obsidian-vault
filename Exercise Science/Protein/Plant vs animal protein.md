> [!summary] Bottom line
> Animal protein can raise muscle protein synthesis more than plant protein at a single moderate meal, but that's a short-term marker. When total protein is high, around 1.6 g/kg a day, vegans and omnivores gain the same muscle and strength, and soy builds muscle as well as whey. Plant proteins are only a few percent less digestible than animal proteins, and some, like corn, have more leucine than whey.

## Muscle growth

Nineteen habitual vegans and 19 omnivores, all young untrained men, did 12 weeks of supervised resistance training with protein brought up to 1.6 g/kg a day with soy (vegans) or whey (omnivores). Both groups gained leg lean mass (1.2 kg each), muscle and fiber cross-sectional area, and leg press strength, with no differences between them.
[Hevia-Larraín 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33599941/)

A meta-analysis of 9 trials (266 people) found supplementing resistance training with soy protein gave the same gains in strength and lean mass as whey or other animal proteins (beef, milk or dairy).
[Messina 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722584/)

## Muscle protein synthesis

In 16 adults aged 65–85, a whole-food meal with lean ground beef raised muscle protein synthesis about 47% more over 6 hours than a vegan meal with the same protein and calories (0.45 g protein/kg).
[Pinckaers 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37972895/)

That was one meal, so whether it matters depends on total daily protein. At low protein intakes plant versus animal sources may make some difference, but at high protein (and leucine) intakes they matter very little, if at all. Older adults often don't eat that much protein, though.

Acute muscle protein synthesis is a surrogate marker, and trials measuring actual changes in lean mass outrank it. In 23 untrained young men, muscle protein synthesis after their first workout didn't correlate with how much their quadriceps grew over 16 weeks of training (r = 0.02–0.16).
[Mitchell 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24586775/)

## Leucine and amino acids

Leucine triggers muscle protein synthesis, and the ISSN suggests 700–3,000 mg of leucine per meal along with the other essential amino acids.
[Jäger 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)

Plant protein isolates have less leucine on average than animal proteins (7.1% vs 8.8%), and less lysine and methionine, but they vary a lot. Corn protein is 13.5% leucine, more than whey (11.0%), and potato (8.3%) has more than casein (8.0%) and egg (7.0%). Hemp and lupin don't meet the WHO leucine requirement. Combining plant proteins, or blending plant and animal proteins, can give an amino acid profile close to animal protein. Two authors have received fees from dairy and nutrition companies.
[Gorissen 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30167963/)

So if leucine percentage were all that mattered, corn protein would beat whey.

## Digestibility

Plant proteins are often said to be much less digestible than animal proteins, but in humans, using true ileal digestibility (what's absorbed by the end of the small intestine), the differences are only a few percent:

| Protein | True ileal digestibility (%) |
|---|---|
| Milk protein | 95 |
| Casein | 94.1 |
| Soy protein | 91.5 |
| Pea protein | 89.4–91.5 |
| Wheat gluten | 85.0–90.3 |
| Lupin protein | 90.0 |
| Rapeseed protein | 84 |

Eggs (91%) and meat (90–94%) are in the same range. Most earlier estimates came from rats or less precise fecal measurements.
[Tomé 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/156482651303400225)

## Protein quality scores

A 2011 FAO expert consultation recommended the DIAAS (digestible indispensable amino acid score) to replace the PDCAAS. It scores each essential amino acid's digestibility at the end of the small intestine, which better reflects what's absorbed than digestibility across the whole gut.
[FAO 2013](https://www.fao.org/ag/humannutrition/35978-02317b979a686a57aa4593304ffc17f06.pdf)

Protein quality matters most when total protein is scarce. Once intake is high, the trials above show the source makes little difference to muscle growth.
