> [!summary] Bottom line
> The biceps and brachialis have their best leverage with the elbow straighter, and a separate supination exercise may train the biceps' supinator role better than twisting during curls. The brachioradialis has its best leverage with the elbow more bent and the forearm pronated, so pronated-grip or EZ-bar curls against bands train it best. Shoulder position matters a little too: incline curls grew the biceps slightly more, and preacher curls grew the brachialis and brachioradialis slightly more.

## Leverage changes with elbow and forearm position

The elbow flexors are four muscles: the long and short heads of the biceps, the brachialis and the brachioradialis. Their moment arms vary by at least 30% across the elbow's range of motion. The biceps' flexion moment arm peaks with the elbow straighter and is larger with the forearm supinated, while its supination moment arm shrinks as the elbow straightens.
[Murray 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)
→ [[Neuromechanical matching]]

## Biceps and brachialis

The biceps (both heads) and brachialis have better leverage than the brachioradialis for elbow flexion when the elbow is extended.

The biceps is also a forearm supinator, with some motor units dedicated to that job. Both heads were activated most when flexing and supinating at the same time, more than either task alone.
[Pérot 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20719658/)

So starting each curl with the palm facing in (neutral) and turning it up (supinating) as you lift may involve the biceps more. A separate forearm supination exercise would work better, however.

## Shoulder position: preacher vs incline curls

The biceps crosses both the shoulder and the elbow, while the brachialis and brachioradialis cross only the elbow. Preacher curls put the shoulder in flexion, which shortens the biceps at the shoulder, while incline curls put the shoulder in extension, which lengthens it. So shoulder position could shift which elbow flexor does most of the work.

In 21 untrained adults who trained one arm with incline dumbbell curls and the other with preacher curls for 12 weeks, MRI showed:
- the biceps grew more with incline curls (+22.1% vs +18.9%)
- the brachialis (+21.1% vs +17.1%) and brachioradialis (+17.6% vs +13.5%) grew more with preacher curls
- total elbow flexor growth didn't differ (+17.6% with incline vs +19.2% with preacher)

The differences were small to moderate.
[Kobayashi 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42829982/)
![[Pasted image 20261025192837.png]]

In 63 young women training for 8 weeks, incline curls grew the upper (proximal) elbow flexors more, and preacher curls grew the lower (distal) part more. Total elbow flexor thickness was measured rather than individual muscles, and each exercise gained the most strength in itself.
[Kassiano 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39809454/)

With cable curls matched for resistance profile, shoulder position made no significant difference. In 15 young men training one arm with preacher cable curls (shoulder flexed about 50°) and the other with Bayesian cable curls (shoulder extended about 50°) for 10 weeks, both grew similarly. The trends matched the dumbbell study, with slightly more biceps growth in the Bayesian arm (9% vs 6–7%) and slightly more brachialis growth in the preacher arm (10% vs 8%), but none of these differences were significant.
[Attarieh 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11906226/)

### Swelling after preacher-position eccentrics

Two MRI studies had men do 5 sets of 6 maximal eccentric contractions on a dynamometer, in a preacher-curl position, from 90° of elbow flexion to full extension:
- Right after the exercise, the brachialis cross-section grew, while T2 (an MRI marker of fluid shifts and muscle use) rose in both the biceps and brachialis. By 5 days after, only the lower brachialis still showed raised T2. [Tsuchiya 2015](https://doi.org/10.3233/IES-150578)
![[Pasted image 20261025193030.png]]
- In the days after the eccentrics, only the brachialis showed delayed swelling, while T2 rose in both the biceps and brachialis. When the other arm did the same task concentrically, the biceps and brachialis both swelled right after. [Ochi 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27632383/)
![[Pasted image 20261025193225.png]]

The brachialis swelling suggests it did more of the work, or took more damage, in this position. Neither study compared it with a shoulder-extended position, though, and the biceps' raised T2 shows it was active too.

### Why it might happen

One explanation is that flexing the shoulder shortens the biceps enough to reduce how much force it can contribute at the elbow, so the brachialis takes over more of the load. This hasn't been measured directly. The hamstrings show a similar pattern: with the hip extended, as in Nordic curls, the knee-only biceps femoris short head grew more, while with the hip flexed, the two-joint hamstrings grew more (see [[Hamstring training]]).
[Maeo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38857522/)

## Brachioradialis

The brachioradialis has better leverage than the biceps and brachialis for elbow flexion when the elbow is more bent and the forearm is pronated (palms down). So pronated-grip or EZ-bar curls against elastic bands train it well.
