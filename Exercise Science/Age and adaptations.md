> [!summary] Bottom line
> Older adults gain muscle and fascicle length more slowly, most likely because they recover more slowly, but they keep the capacity to adapt and maintain muscle on the same small doses as young people. Tendon stiffness and fiber type changes look similar to young people's.

## Hypertrophy

The rate of muscle growth is slower in older people than young people, even in muscles that don't experience stretch-mediated hypertrophy (biceps)
[Welle 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8914498/)

We see type I and type II fibers have less increases in [[Glossary#CSA|CSA]] in old people vs. young. We also see type II fibers grow more than type I fibers in this study.
[Kosek 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16614355/)

Exactly why old people grow less isn't clear, but the most common hypothesis is that old people recover more slowly than younger people, which impairs their ability to perform later workouts in a training program.
[[Age and recovery]]

An interesting finding is that older people display a smaller repeated bout effect than younger people when they work out for the first time. This would make them more susceptible to muscle damage for a long period of time and accumulate more post-workout fatigue over time. This means older people are much more susceptible to [[Calcium ion-related fatigue|calcium-ion related fatigue]] resistance adaptations.
[Lavender 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16767435/)
→ [[Repeated bout effect]]

Some researchers argue that older people's muscle fibers are less sensitive to the [[Mechanical tension|mechanical tension]] stimulus. But we see older people are able to still maintain muscle mass with 3 sets once a week, just like young people. If they were less sensitive, this would lead to atrophy, they would require more for maintenance.
[[Trappe 2002]]
→ [[Detraining adaptations speed]]

## Sarcomerogenesis

In human studies, we see increases in fascicle length during eccentric-only training is slower in old people than young. Which means the rate of [[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]] is slower. But over 8 weeks of training, the relative increases in fascicle length are not too different between the two, due to the earlier plateau of the younger lifters. This means the capacity of sacromerogenesis is slower, but not removed in older people.
[Quinlan 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34196903/)

Rodent models though find that the capacity of sarcomerogenesis with eccentric-only training might be entirely lost or at least greatly reduced in old animals compared to young. If sarcomerogenesis is similarly negatively affected by a reduced recovery capacity as hypertrophy, then it's likely that the use of electrically-stimulated eccentric contractions in the animals, which are known to cause a lot more muscle damage than voluntary conditions.
[Hinks 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38511212/)

## Fiber type shifts

There is not muscle data that compares fiber type shifts between young and old people during a strength training program, The data we have shows no meaningful effects of age on this adaptation. But a time-course study would be necessary to be confident about this observation.
[Kosek 2006](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.01474.2005?rfr_dat=cr_pub++0pubmed)

## Tendon stiffness

There isn't much data on increases in tendon stiffness between old and young people, but the data we have shows age doesn't really affect this adaptation taking into account that increases in tendon stiffness happen for longer before plateauing in older people because they had a lower baseline due to their lower levels of physical activity.
[Quinlan 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34196903/)

We also see that older people are similarly dependent on load for getting increases in tendon stiffness, with only moderately-heavy or heavy loads being able to make this adaptation.
[Grosset 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4082599/)
