> [!summary] Bottom line
> Tendons and ligaments respond to strength training mainly by getting stiffer, with only small size changes. Stiffness rises quickly over the first weeks and months, then plateaus. It depends on load, not contraction type: heavy (1–5RM) or moderately heavy (6–10RM) loads stiffen tendons, while loads lighter than about 12–15RM don't. Exactly which changes inside the tendon cause this is still unclear.

## Stiffness

Stiffness describes how well a material resists being stretched. A stiff material needs a large pulling force to stretch a little, while a compliant one stretches a lot under a small force. Stiffness (k) is the force applied (F) divided by the change in length (ΔL), so a high value means the structure can take high forces without lengthening much.

Tendons and ligaments are damaged by strain (how much they lengthen), not by the force they transmit. A stiffer tendon lengthens less under a given load, so stiffness adaptations directly protect against rupture, and presumably the same holds for ligaments.
[Provenzano 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11744679/)

## Size barely changes

In a meta-analysis of exercise studies, tendons were very responsive to loading, but the effect on tendon cross-sectional area was small (standardized effect 0.24), and stiffness gains seemed to come more from changes in the tendon material than from its size.
[Bohm 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/)
Even after 4 years of resistance training, with 58% more strength and 56% more muscle than untrained men, patellar tendon size didn't differ.
[Massey 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29253326/)

## Time course

Stiffness rises quickly early on and then plateaus. In 12 weeks of heavy eccentric calf training, Achilles tendon stiffness rose 51% by week 4 and 82% by week 12, with tendon size only increasing from week 8.
[Geremia 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29858689/)
Both 12 weeks and 4 years of training produced similar increases in high-force stiffness, though only years of training reduced tendon strain across the whole force range.
[Massey 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29253326/)

Once training stops, stiffness gains are lost in about 2 months (see [[Detraining adaptations speed]]).

## Load, not contraction type

Contrary to popular belief, tendon stiffening isn't specific to eccentric or concentric training, but it is specific to load. In the meta-analysis, stiffness gains depended on loading intensity but not on contraction type.
[Bohm 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/)
Tendons don't get stiffer with loads lighter than about 12–15RM; it takes heavy (1–5RM) or moderately heavy (6–10RM) loads.
[Malliaras 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23773532/)
[Tsitsilonis 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24796826/)
Training one leg at high tendon strain and the other at low strain increased stiffness only in the high-strain leg, so the strain has to exceed what everyday activity produces.
[Arampatzis 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20863501/)

## What changes inside the tendon

The adaptations behind stiffer tendons are still unclear. Proposed changes include:
1. Denser collagen fibrils.
2. More cross-links (enzymatic and non-enzymatic) between neighboring collagen molecules.
3. More proteoglycan connections between neighboring collagen fibrils.
4. Changes in the crimp angle of the collagen strands.
5. Shifts in the proportions of different collagen types.

Few long-term studies have tracked these alongside stiffness. In older adults, heavy training maintained tendon stiffness while moderate training didn't, yet collagen content, fibril structure and enzymatic cross-links didn't change with either.
[Eriksen 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30704412/)
