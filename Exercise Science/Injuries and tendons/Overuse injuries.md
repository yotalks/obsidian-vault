> [!summary] Bottom line
> Overuse injuries are often modeled as mechanical fatigue: damage that builds up from repeated loading until a tissue fails under a normal load. That fits muscle strains and bone damage well, where heavier loads matter most. Tendons need a correction, since light loads stretch them more and so do more damage per rep than expected. And muscle fibrosis doesn't fit at all: it comes from incomplete recovery and inflammation, not mechanical fatigue.

## Mechanical fatigue

Overuse injuries usually show up as pain with signs of tissue damage, in muscle, connective tissue or bone, after long periods of doing the same or similar movements. They're often modeled as mechanical fatigue.
[Edwards 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001271/)

Mechanical fatigue is a materials science term, unrelated to physiological fatigue. It's the damage that builds up in a stretchy structure under repeated, cyclical loading, changing its material properties over time. The structure becomes more compliant and can then fail unexpectedly during activities that used to be fine. Both the size of the load and the number of repetitions add to mechanical fatigue, with load size usually the bigger factor.
[Gallagher 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27376409/)

## Where it fits

**Muscle strains** most likely come from mechanical fatigue, separate from post-workout fatigue. Repeated small tears at the muscle-tendon junction, from high loads in stretched positions, build up into a local weak spot that lets a large rupture happen within the normal range of motion. When muscles were pulled to failure, all of them tore at the muscle-tendon junction.
[Garrett 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3674268/)
And a muscle stretched just short of rupture tore at only 63% of its normal peak load afterward, making it far more prone to re-injury.
[Taylor 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8465911/)

**Bone damage** also seems to follow mechanical fatigue, with heavier loads the main factor. In rats doing a repetitive pulling task for 18 weeks, high-force pulling caused bone loss, microcracks and bone cell death, while low-force pulling with the same repetitions built bone.
[Barbe 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5878749/)

## Where it doesn't

**Tendinopathies** tend to develop more from high-repetition than high-load activity. That's probably because connective tissue is viscoelastic: it stretches and recoils more under light loads than heavy ones. So each light-load rep is more damaging than the model predicts and each heavy-load rep much less, which shrinks the role of load size for connective tissue.

**Muscle fibrosis** and other overuse injuries to muscle that aren't strains don't fit the model at all. Local fibrosis is more likely caused by incomplete recovery from post-workout fatigue mechanisms and the inflammation that comes with them. In rats, oxidative stress before a muscle injury amplified inflammation, inhibited regeneration and increased fibrotic scarring (see also [[Impaired muscle regrowth]]).
[Ghaly 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884092/)
