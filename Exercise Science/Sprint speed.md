> [!summary] Bottom line
> Sprint speed depends on the horizontal impulse you can produce in each ground contact, repeated over many strides. Two hip-led sequences make that happen. To push, the hip extensors drive while the knee flexors and ankle absorb force eccentrically. To reposition the leg for the next contact, the hip flexors drive while the knee extensors absorb force eccentrically. The hip muscles work at moderate speeds, between maximum force and maximum velocity. So training should combine heavy strength work for the hip extensors and flexors, eccentric-only work for the knee flexors and extensors, plyometrics for the calves, and sprinting for maximum hip speed.

## Repeated horizontal impulses

How fast we sprint is determined by the horizontal impulse produced during ground contact, repeated stride after stride. That makes sprinting different from jumping or throwing, which depend on a single vertical or horizontal impulse. In sprint acceleration, propulsive impulse explained 57% of the variation in sprint speed, and greater propulsion went with faster hip extension of the stance leg.
[Hunter 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16131703/)

Sprinting therefore needs two proximal-to-distal sequences: one that produces each horizontal impulse, and one that repositions the leg to produce the next impulse as soon as possible. At speeds above about 7 m/s, running faster depends more on swinging the legs faster through the air than on pushing harder into the ground.
[Dorn 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22573774/)

## Producing the impulse

The push starts with hip extension, but unlike vertical jumping, the next joint action is knee flexion rather than knee extension. That's because it begins at the end of the swing phase, when the hip is flexing and the knee is extending.
- The **hip extensors** work mainly concentrically, producing power in the middle of the force-velocity range.
- The **knee flexors** work eccentrically, absorbing energy from the hip and passing it down into the ground through the ankle.
- The **ankle** also works eccentrically, absorbing the very large ground contact forces.

## Repositioning the leg

The recovery starts with hip flexion, followed by knee extension. That's because the push ends with the hip still extending and the knee still flexing, so recovering the leg means the hip starts flexing and the knee starts extending.
- The **hip flexors** work mainly concentrically, again producing power in the middle of the force-velocity range.
- The **knee extensors** work eccentrically, absorbing energy from the hip.

As running speed rises, the work and torque at the hip and knee during late swing increase the most. Hip positive work during swing, and knee negative (absorbing) work during swing, keep increasing beyond about 5 m/s, while the ankle's work levels off.
[Schache 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131859/)
[Schache 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26056240/)

## Training for sprint speed

A program to improve sprinting should target the key parts of both sequences.

**For the push (horizontal impulse):**
- **Heavy strength training for the hip extensors:** hip thrusts for the glute max, 45° back extensions for the hamstrings, and stiff-legged deadlifts for the adductor magnus (see [[Gluteus maximus training]] and [[Hamstring training]]).
- **Eccentric-only training for the knee flexors:** Nordic curls, or eccentric-only leg curls, like seated leg curls lifting with two legs and lowering with one.
- **Maximum speed for the hip extensors:** most likely best trained by sprinting, or assisted sprinting where possible.

**For repositioning the leg:**
- **Heavy strength training for the hip flexors:** one exercise with peak effort near full hip extension, for the rectus femoris, and one with peak effort near 90° of hip flexion, for the iliacus and psoas major. The rectus femoris does most of the work up to about 60° of hip flexion and the iliopsoas beyond that (see [[Quadriceps training]]). Good heavy hip flexor exercises are uncommon.
- **Eccentric-only training for the knee extensors:** reverse Nordic curls, or eccentric-only leg extensions, lifting with two legs and lowering with one.
- **Maximum speed for the hip flexors:** again, most likely best trained by sprinting or assisted sprinting.

The calves are likely best trained with plyometrics.

For the neural and fiber qualities that make movement faster, see [[Increasing speed]].
