> [!summary] Bottom line
> Both inactivity and excessive muscle damage can leave extra collagen inside muscle that doesn't fully go away, adding mass without strength. Excess inflammation and oxidative stress, as in overreaching blocks, may drive it.

Muscle is very resilient and plastic but we do see that both inactivity and excessive muscle damage or injury can cause muscles to accumulate more connective tissue than they contained before, worsening their strength to size ratio.

## Muscle collagen increases after inactivity

Many studies found that periods of inactivity lead to atrophy, and that this atrophy comes with an increase in the amount of intramuscular collagen. This collagen seems to accumulate on the endomysium (the collagen that surrounds single muscle fibers) as well as the perimysium (the collagen that surrounds muscle fascicles). The accumulation of collagen happens in tandem with signs of muscle fiber degeneration and inflammation.
[Jozsa 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3169207/)

This accumulation of collagen does not seem to go away completely after subsequent blocks of training. Moreover, the maladaptation increases the mass of the muscle without also increasing its strength, which likely negatively affects the strength to weight ratio and muscular function.
[Shimkus 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326950927_Responses_of_skeletal_muscle_size_and_anabolism_are_reproducible_with_multiple_periods_of_unloadingreloading)

## Muscle collagen content increases after excessive muscle damage

Many studies found that muscle damage or injury can also lead to an increases in intramuscular collagen. the amount of collagen that is deposited is increased when the level of oxidative stress is increased, which in turn increases the inflammatory response.
[Ghaly 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884092/)

We see again that this accumulation of collagen doesn't seem to go away entirely after subsequent recovery periods. There is a sustained increase in mass of the muscle that occurs without an in tandem increase in strength, which worsens the strength to weight ratio and muscular function.
[Vignaud 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20467471/)

## Why does this happen?

After high intensity exercise, there is often an inflammatory response. This inflammatory response is an integral part of the muscle damage repair process. the greater the muscle damage from the exercise, the greater the inflammatory response.
[Paulsen 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20010127/)

Even though the inflammatory response is a key element of the muscle damage repair process, it also causes more muscle damage in the days after the workout, possibly by increasing the levels of oxidative stress inside the muscle.
[Pizza 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1665528/)

Importantly, while this inflammatory response is needed for repairing the muscle damage from the exercise, when the level of inflammation is too high, it seems to increase the amount of muscle collagen that is deposited inside the muscle.
[Ghaly 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884092/)

Overreaching and overtraining has often been observed to happen with increased levels of oxidative stress, which is a key part that increase the inflammatory response after training. So it is possible that athletes are at a greater risk of experiencing an increase in muscle collagen content (and therefore a worse strength to weight ratio) when they do very high volume or very high frequency training blocks that are intended to produce overreaching.
[Cheng 2020](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Intramuscular-mechanisms-of-overtraining-Cheng-Jude/6a4e92aae5380d16244d9332541b07c975706809)
