> [!summary] Bottom line
> Older muscle takes similar damage but recovers more slowly, likely because of blunted inflammation, and is more prone to strain injury. Older lifters do better with lower volumes, heavier loads, a couple of reps in reserve, and frequency matched to volume.

## Old people recover more slowly than young people

When older and younger animals are subject to muscle-damaging eccentric-only training workouts, they have similar post-workout fatigue and muscle damage markers immediately post-workout but the older animals recover much more slowly.
[Rader 2006](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00663.2005)

We see a similar thing in human studies.
[Dedrick 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2347319/)

It is often said that elderly people experience more muscle damage, but we do see they have a lower capacity for motor unit recruitment which means they're not able to recruit as many of those more easily-damaged type II fibers, so the muscle damage they experience is going to be quite similar to young people.
[Rozand 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8015244/)

The slower rate of recovery in older people can likely be explained by reduced or dysregulated inflammatory function. We see research shows that the secondary muscle damage effect is blunted in older animals. The accumulation of inflammatory markers is reduced and the removal of damaged muscle fibers after is much less at the same time-point. This clearly indicates that the process is happening a lot more slowly in older muscle tissue.
[Sloboda 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29684112/)

We see the same thing happening with [[Glossary#DOMS|DOMS]] in older animal populations which could provide some more support for a link between inflammation and DOMS.
[Taguchi 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17156878/)

## Old people are more susceptible to strain injury than young people

In animal models with maximal levels of muscle activation through means of electrical stimulation, older muscle has more muscle damage after being subjected to a single, maximal eccentric contraction through a large ROM compared to younger muscle. This mechanical damage is more like mild muscle strain injury than exercise-induced muscle damage.
[Brooks 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8961197/)

The capacity of muscle fibers to transmit forces laterally is the key mechanism that prevents them from being overstretched and damaged during eccentric contractions. Older muscle lacks dystrophin, which is an essential component of the costameres that allows for lateral force transmission to happen. Because of this, it tends to have fibrosis in response to severe injury to the muscle fibers, which is increased collagen content.
[Ramaswamy 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3060596/)
→ [[Impaired muscle regrowth]]

## Old people cannot always train the exact same as young people

The ability to recover from each workout is a key factor for a successful strength training program. Since older subjects can't recover as fast, they don't have the ability to train with the same workout volumes as frequently. So, when doing high volumes, they don't make progress when training too often.
[Cutlip 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17111012/)

When frequency is reduced for the same volume, the old subjects are able to recover and make progress with strength gains and hypertrophy. We also have other studies have the same positive effects with lowering frequency for same volume. So it's not necessarily to just adjust training frequency but to take into account both the volume and frequency.
[Rader 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206483/)
[Rader 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29438735/)
→ [[Training frequency and hypertrophy]]

In practice, older people should train with lower workout volumes (ideally single sets per exercise), training with heavier loads (4-6 rep range for example), leaving a couple reps in reserve, minimizing the intensity and duration of the eccentric phase, and picking exercises that are hardest in either the contracted or mid position (as long as muscle has good leverage there still).
