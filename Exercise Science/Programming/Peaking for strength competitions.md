> [!summary] Bottom line
> Peaking means letting accumulated post-workout fatigue clear without losing any key adaptations. Programs that build up a lot of fatigue need a long peaking phase (4–6 weeks), and programs that build up little can use a short one (1–2 weeks). Neural adaptations fade slowly, so the training only needs to protect hypertrophy and sarcomeres, which takes very little volume. There's no need to bring volume down gradually, since a peaking phase can start at minimal volume.

Peaking for a strength sports competition involves allowing all of the previously accumulated post-workout fatigue to dissipate while simultaneously avoiding the loss of any key adaptations.
→ [[Accumulating post-workout fatigue]]

## How long to peak

The exact duration of a peaking phase depends partly on the type of training that was done immediately beforehand. A training program that has accumulated a lot of post-workout fatigue will need a long peaking phase (4–6 weeks). In contrast, a training program that has accumulated little post-workout fatigue can easily use a short peaking phase (1–2 weeks).

Strength athletes tend to be afraid of tapering and often choose very short peaking phases. Across seven studies of weight lifting sports, tapers typically lasted 5–9 days, though longer in powerlifting, and athletes stopped training altogether 1.5–4 days before competing.
[Winwood 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42611173/)

In 364 North American powerlifters, the most common approach was a 7–10 day step taper that cut volume by 41–50%.
[Travis 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34846328/)

However, a properly designed peaking phase shouldn't lead to any strength loss even when performed for several weeks. So the same peaking phase can be used for anywhere from 1 to 6 weeks and doesn't need to be varied.

There's also no need to taper volume down progressively. A peaking phase can simply start with minimal volume.

## What needs protecting

Peaking phases should aim to avoid the loss of all relevant strength-related adaptations while still letting accumulated post-workout fatigue dissipate. Neural adaptations are lost very slowly, while peripheral adaptations are lost very quickly. So peaking phases need to be designed to avoid losing hypertrophy and [[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]]. Neural adaptations like coordination and motor unit recruitment will remain unchanged even if the peaking phase doesn't deliberately stimulate them.
→ [[Detraining adaptations speed]]

### Neural adaptations

After 3 months of isometric knee extension training, 8 people stopped for 3 months. Their strength and neural activation didn't change, even though muscle size was back to pre-training levels after 1 month and tendon stiffness after 2 months.
[[Kubo 2010]]

Training one leg also raises strength in the other, untrained leg through neural changes. In 9 men, that leg's strength, voluntary activation and EMG didn't change significantly over 6 weeks of detraining.
[Shima 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11990741/)

In 12 power athletes who stopped resistance training for 14 days, bench press (−1.7%), squat (−0.9%) and vertical jump (+1.2%) didn't change significantly. Eccentric knee extension force did fall (−12%), as did Type II fiber area (−6.4%).
[Hortobágyi 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8371654/)

### Peripheral adaptations

Hypertrophy starts to go within weeks. In men who gained 17% in fiber area over 90 days of training, fiber area gradually fell over the following 90 days without training.
[Kadi 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1665027/)

After 6 weeks of arm training, 10 men given a placebo lost 2.4% of muscle thickness in the first week off at one of three measured sites. 
[Inoue 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39076851/)

Sarcomeres go faster. After 7 days of stretch-induced growth in chickens, the muscle was fully back to normal weight within 13 days of removing the stretch.
[[Sparrow 1982]]

In humans, biceps femoris fascicles that grew 23–24% over 6 weeks of Nordic hamstring training shrank by 15–17% after 2 weeks off, though eccentric strength held for at least 4 weeks.
[[Presland 2018]]

Weighted Nordic training lengthened fascicles by 1.57 cm, and 0.96 cm of that was gone after a single week off.
[Pollard 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30629773/)

Fascicles go before muscle size does. After 6 weeks of Romanian deadlifts or Nordics, fascicle length returned to baseline after 2 weeks off, while hamstring cross-sectional area stayed elevated.
[Crawford 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40085810/)

## Example peaking phases

Maintaining hypertrophy and sarcomerogenesis in a muscle requires less than 1 set to failure twice per week. Effective peaking phases for strength athletes can be built with full-body (2 or 3 times per week) or upper-lower (4 workouts per week) routines.
→ [[Detraining adaptations speed]]

Taking a powerlifter as an example, with RIR meaning reps in reserve:

**Full-body 2 times per week**

| Workout | Main lifts | Accessories |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workouts 1 and 2 | 1 set of 5RM with 2–3 RIR of squat, bench and deadlift | 1 set to 1–2 RIR with 8–10RM of any important accessory exercises |

**Full-body 3 times per week (ABA, then BAB)**

| Workout | Main lifts | Accessories |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 1 set of 5RM with 2 RIR of squat | 1 set to 1 RIR with 8–10RM of squat accessory exercises |
| B | 1 set of 5RM with 2 RIR of bench and deadlift | 1 set to 1 RIR with 8–10RM of any bench and deadlift accessory exercises |

**Upper-lower 4 workouts per week**

| Workout | Main lifts | Accessories |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upper | 1 set of 5RM with 2–3 RIR of bench | 1 set to 1–2 RIR with 8–10RM of any bench accessory exercises |
| Lower | 1 set of 5RM with 2–3 RIR of squat and deadlift | 1 set to 1–2 RIR with 8–10RM of any squat and deadlift accessory exercises |
