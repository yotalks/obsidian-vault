> [!summary] Bottom line
> Drop sets probably cause about as much post-workout fatigue as one set to failure with the last load in the sequence. Since that last drop usually uses a light load, and light loads to failure cause far more muscle damage and soreness than moderate or heavy loads, drop sets likely cause more post-workout fatigue than straight sets with moderate loads. Long-term growth and strength gains are similar to traditional sets.

## What drop sets do

A drop set is a set to failure followed straight away by more reps with a lighter load, often repeated two or three times.

Across 12 studies (274 people), drop sets gave similar long-term hypertrophy (SMD 0.04, 95% CI −0.29 to 0.36) and strength gains (SMD −0.04, −0.34 to 0.26) as traditional sets, but acutely raised perceived exertion (SMD 1.62, 0.33 to 2.91) and lactate (SMD 0.67, 0.20 to 1.14).
[Havers 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41920484/)

The reps after the first set add little stimulus → [[Junk volume]], [[Stimulating reps and failure]].

## Why light loads cause more post-workout fatigue

Contrary to popular belief, lighter loads cause far more post-workout fatigue than heavier loads when we do the same number of sets to failure. Studies that find less fatigue with lighter loads do the light workout with far more reps in reserve → [[Fatigue across rep ranges]].

In 11 trained men doing knee extensions to failure, 40% of maximum caused more fatigue than 80%, and the leg still hadn't recovered after 48 hours, while it had recovered from the heavier session by 24 hours.
[Farrow 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32401690/)

In 15 trained men doing 4 sets of leg extensions to failure at 30% or 80% of 1RM, torque was reduced more right after the light session.
[Haun 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28963127/)

In 12 trained men, 8 sets of 10 impaired isometric strength for 72 hours, while 8 sets of 3 with heavier loads didn't, though both raised markers of muscle damage.
[Bartolomei 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28447186/)

Post-workout fatigue mechanisms are all directly or indirectly caused by calcium ions building up in muscle fibers → [[Calcium ion-related fatigue]]:
- **Local fatigue:** calcium ions directly damage the muscle fiber in several places. If too much calcium enters, it can activate calpains that break down proteins, phospholipase A2 that weakens membranes, and reactive oxygen species that cause lipid peroxidation, which can worsen the damage in a self-reinforcing loop. [Gissel 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16533926/)
- **Central nervous system fatigue:** comes from the inflammatory response to that damage. In mice, raising IL-1β in the brain worsened running performance after uphill running, and blocking it improved performance after muscle-damaging downhill running. [Carmichael 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16778069/)

Calcium ions build up in line with how long muscle fibers spend activated. Sets with lighter loads last longer, so they cause more calcium accumulation, and so more post-workout damage and every other post-workout fatigue mechanism.

Low-frequency fatigue, a calcium ion-related mechanism, was greater after low-force voluntary contractions than high-force ones, and tracked how long the task lasted.
[Griffin 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18283442/)

## Predicting the fatigue from a drop set

A typical drop set might start with a 10RM (about 80% of 1RM), drop to a 15RM (about 70%), then drop again to a 20RM (about 60%). Depending on our muscular endurance, how quickly we recover from metabolite-related fatigue, and how long we take between drops, we might do 10 reps on the first set and 5–10 reps on each of the next two.

Reps at a given percentage of 1RM vary a lot. Across 269 studies (7,289 people), about 15 reps could be done at 70% of 1RM, and the spread between people grew at lighter loads, with a standard deviation of 2.5 reps at 80% and 4.4 reps at 60%.
[Nuzzo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37792272/)

The post-workout fatigue from this sequence should be similar to one set of a 20RM, for two reasons:
1. The total reps across the three sets are similar to the reps in one 20RM set, only slightly higher.
2. Total fatigue at failure in the final drop has to be the same as total fatigue at failure in a single 20RM set.

Only the mix of fatigue mechanisms can differ. Drop sets start with heavier loads, so they may involve more metabolite-related fatigue and less calcium ion-related fatigue, making the sequence slightly less fatiguing than one 20RM set. But if total reps are higher, the drop set would be slightly more fatiguing. As a general rule, a drop set causes about as much post-workout fatigue as one set to failure with the final load in the sequence.

## Measured recovery after drop sets

In 15 trained men, squat drop sets (6 reps at 85% of 1RM plus 3 drops) reduced jump performance from 30 minutes to 48 hours afterward, like 4 sets of 6 at 85% did. Creatine kinase peaked at 24 hours with no difference between protocols, and lactate was similar between drop sets (8.5 mmol/L) and traditional sets (7.7 mmol/L).
[Raeder 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26349042/)

## Conclusion

Drop sets will likely cause much more muscle damage and soreness than straight sets with moderate loads, because they usually take at least one drop to failure with a light load (16–30RM), and light loads cause far more post-workout fatigue than moderate (6–15RM) or heavy (1–5RM) loads → [[Recovery between workouts]].
