> [!summary] Bottom line
> Beginners (under 6 months of training) get lots of stretch-mediated hypertrophy, can grow while training further from failure and with more fatigue, rarely plateau, and suffer less damage from the same workout. Intermediates (6 months to 2 years) get little stretch-mediated growth, need to train closer to failure while avoiding unnecessary fatigue, need a sound progression model, and recruit more fast fibers, so they're more prone to damage. As beginners become intermediates, they should do fewer stretched-position exercises, train closer to failure, progress gradually and use somewhat less volume with heavier loads.

## 1. Stretch-mediated hypertrophy

Beginners can easily get a lot of [[Stretch-mediated hypertrophy|stretch-mediated hypertrophy]] from [[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]], while intermediates are unlikely to get much. Sarcomerogenesis stops once there are so many sarcomeres in series that each no longer stretches far enough to generate the passive tension that drives it. Exactly when isn't clear, but it's very likely within 6 months. So beginners benefit greatly from stretched-position exercises, while intermediates can put those on maintenance and focus more on shortened-position or mid-range exercises → [[Partial vs full range of motion]].

A computer model of stretch-driven growth predicted that after a limb is lengthened, sarcomeres first stretch from 3.09 to 3.51 µm, then return to their original length within two weeks as new sarcomeres are added.
[Zöllner 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3462200/)

During limb lengthening by distraction, sarcomeres are added in a negative feedback loop, though whether satellite cells are needed for this longitudinal growth isn't known.
[Caiozzo 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12394462/)

In 20 men doing 12 weeks of eccentric knee extensor training, rectus femoris and vastus lateralis fascicles lengthened 17–19%, with most of the change in the first 4 weeks.
[Baroni 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23852989/)

In 21 people doing 10 weeks of slow concentric or eccentric knee extensions, vastus lateralis fascicle length rose about 5% by week 5, with no further increase by week 10.
[Blazevich 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17717119/)

## 2. Which fibers can still grow

Beginners can likely grow the fibers of the lowest high-threshold motor units, while intermediates probably can't. Going up the motor unit pool, fibers become less oxidative, and by the size principle of striated muscle (not Henneman's size principle), slower fibers reach their maximum size at smaller diameters, so they stop growing earlier in a lifter's career → [[Individual differences in muscle growth]].

So beginners can grow while training further from failure, with more reps in reserve, and still get decent results despite a lot of fatigue during and after workouts, since most fatigue mechanisms only affect the fibers at the top of the motor unit pool. Intermediates need to train closer to failure and avoid central nervous system and calcium ion-related fatigue → [[Stimulating reps and failure]].

## 3. Plateaus and progression

Beginners gain strength from workout to workout through many adaptations, so they rarely plateau. Intermediates plateau more often because they can't make the same big gains in coordination, motor unit recruitment and lateral force transmission. So beginners can use almost any progression model, while intermediates need one that matches the rate of progress to the rate of the underlying adaptations → [[Progressive overload]].

Coordination improves very quickly at first. In 15 men doing maximal isometric dorsiflexions, force and muscle activity stabilized within the first 10 attempts.
[Green 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24552367/)

## 4. Damage and the repeated bout effect

A complete beginner hasn't yet developed the [[Repeated bout effect|repeated bout effect]], but by 6 months a beginner has maximal protection from the adaptations behind it. As they become intermediate, though, they can recruit higher-threshold motor units and so access more fast twitch fibers, which makes them more prone to post-workout damage and fatigue than as a beginner.

Well-trained men had higher voluntary activation than untrained men, though they still can't recruit every fiber in large muscles.
[Balshaw 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30387185/)

So intermediates may need more care with the five variables that drive muscle damage: proximity to failure, volume, rep range, lowering phase duration and resistance profile. They can't tolerate training as close to failure, with as much volume, as high reps, as slow eccentrics or as many stretched-position exercises → [[Training volume]].
