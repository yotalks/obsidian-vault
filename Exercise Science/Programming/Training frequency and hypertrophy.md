> [!summary] Bottom line
> Research on frequency for hypertrophy is thin: most comparisons find no difference, with only a weak trend favoring 3× over 1× per week. In theory, frequency should matter: each extra set in a workout adds less than the one before, and the post-workout growth window lasts about 48 hours, so spreading sets across more sessions should give more growth. The limit is recovery, since training a muscle that's still damaged gives a weaker stimulus, and the best frequency varies between people and muscles.

There are very few training studies when assessing training frequency for hypertrophy. The only trend is for 3x per week being better than 1x per week.

Study on trained adults found no differences between 1 and 2 times frequency.
[Brigatto 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29528962/)

Study on untrained adults comparing 1 and 2 times frequency found no differences.
[Gentil 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martim-Bottaro-2/publication/261735493_Effects_of_equal-volume_resistance_training_performed_one_or_two_times_a_week_in_upper_body_muscle_size_and_strength_of_untrained_young_men/links/54da487b0cf261ce15cc9045/Effects-of-equal-volume-resistance-training-performed-one-or-two-times-a-week-in-upper-body-muscle-size-and-strength-of-untrained-young-men.pdf)

Study comparing 1x and 2x frequency on trained adults found no differences.
[Tavares 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28316261/)

Study comparing 1 and 3 times frequency in trained adults found no differences but 3x was numerically better.
[Ochi 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30013480/)

Study on trained adults comparing 1 and 3 times frequency found 3x is better than 1x.
[Schoenfeld 2015b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25932981/)

Study comparing 1 and 3 times frequency on trained adults found 3x is better than 1x
[Neves 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36228016/)

Study on untrained adults comparing 2 and 3 times frequency found no differences.
[Barcelos 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29852092/)

Study on untrained elderly comparing 2 and 3 times frequency found no differences
[Grzyb 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32028459/)

Study on trained adults comparing 2 and 3 times frequency found no differences.
[Lasevicius 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31531139/)

## Why frequency could matter

### Diminishing returns per set

Each extra set in a workout adds less stimulus than the one before ([[Training volume]]). In a model where the first set is worth 1 arbitrary unit (AU), 3 sets in one workout total 1.61 AU, while 1 set in each of three workouts totals 3.0 AU. With the same weekly sets, spreading them out should give more stimulus.

### The post-workout window

After resistance exercise, [[Glossary#MyoPS|myofibrillar protein synthesis]] stays raised for up to about 48 hours. In 20 young men and women, the protein synthesis over those 48 hours correlated with muscle growth after 8 weeks of training, though not in the untrained state.
[Abou Sawan 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35081094/)

Outside that window, muscle is slowly lost, which is what maintenance studies measure. After 16 weeks of training 3 days a week, young adults kept all their muscle growth over the next 32 weeks on either one third of the dose (one day a week) or one ninth of it. The one-third dose even added more fiber growth. Older adults lost fiber size on both maintenance doses.
[Bickel 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/)

A model built on this assumes 3 sets in one weekly workout is exactly enough to maintain muscle. Then the 1.61 AU from that workout is lost over the 5 days outside the 48-hour window, about 0.32 AU a day. On that model, 3 sets once a week gives no net gain, while 1 set three times a week gives 3.0 AU and loses only about 0.32 AU. → [[Detraining adaptations speed]]

## The limit: recovering from damage

Training a muscle that hasn't recovered from the last workout gives a weaker stimulus, for two reasons:
- **Less tension per fiber.** Most of the early strength loss after damaging exercise comes from excitation-contraction coupling failure, and a slow loss of contractile protein over the following days drags out recovery. [Warren 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11337828/)
- **Less recruitment.** In mice, raising IL-1β in the brain worsened running performance after uphill running, and blocking it improved performance after muscle-damaging downhill running. So inflammation from damage may cause central fatigue. [Carmichael 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16778069/)
→ [[Recovery between workouts]]

Recovery varies between people. After 50 maximal elbow flexor eccentrics, 21 people lost 49% of their strength on average, 22 lost 23%, and 3 lost none. The bigger losses came from the muscle itself, with no difference in central measures 5 minutes after exercise.
[Hubal 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17473772/)

It also varies between muscles. When sedentary men did eccentrics for 9 muscle groups, strength 1 day later had dropped by 16–57%, with more damage in the arm muscles than the leg muscles. A second session 2 weeks later caused much less damage in every muscle ([[Repeated bout effect]]).
[Chen 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30663816/)

## Why each exercise needs training twice a week

There are three physiological reasons each exercise, not just each muscle, should be done at least twice a week in a hypertrophy program.

### Sarcomerogenesis

The stimulus for adding sarcomeres in series is entirely different from the stimulus for adding myofibrils in parallel. Doing a stretched-position exercise for a muscle once a week (like a leg press) and a contracted-position exercise once a week on another day (like a knee extension) stimulates myofibril addition twice but sarcomerogenesis only once. So stretched-position exercises need doing twice a week to keep sarcomeres from being lost between workouts → [[Detraining sarcomerogenesis]].

During limb lengthening by distraction, sarcomeres are added in a negative feedback loop that responds to stretch.
[Caiozzo 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12394462/)

### Two-joint muscles

Two-joint muscles switch off in multi-joint exercises where they'd work as agonists at one joint and antagonists at the other. Doing a multi-joint exercise once a week (like a leg press) and a single-joint exercise once a week on another day (like a knee extension) trains the single-joint quadriceps twice but the two-joint rectus femoris only once. So single-joint exercises need doing twice a week to keep the rectus femoris from losing its growth between sessions → [[Quadriceps training]].

Combining hip and knee extension cut rectus femoris activity to 67% of its level in knee extension alone.
[Yamashita 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3220066/)

### Neuromechanical matching

Exercises with very different biomechanics, like different planes of motion or resistance profiles, recruit somewhat different motor units within a muscle → [[Neuromechanical matching]]. A frontal plane exercise (like a wide-grip pull-down) and a sagittal plane exercise (like a narrow-grip row) both train some latissimus dorsi motor units, but some in the middle and lower regions are only trained in the frontal plane and some in the upper region only in the sagittal plane → [[Latissimus dorsi training]]. So each biomechanically different exercise needs doing twice a week to keep the growth in the motor units only it trains.
[Hudson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985474/)

Across 19 segments of the pecs, delts and lats, each segment's activation depended on its moment arm for the intended movement.
[Brown 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16458022/)

During rapid shoulder adduction, segments across the pecs, lats and deltoid showed different EMG burst patterns depending on whether each segment acted as a prime mover, synergist or antagonist.
[Wickham 2004](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0218957704001272)

## Training a muscle on consecutive days

Frequency can be analyzed through three things: the stimulus period after a workout, the atrophy period whenever we're not in a stimulus period, and how long post-workout fatigue lasts. Training again during a stimulus period gains less, because protein synthesis can only be raised so far ([[Training volume]]). Leaving a muscle in the atrophy period too long lets it shrink and undo the last workout. And training while still fatigued means lower fiber tension and recruitment.

Muscle protein synthesis stays raised for about 48 hours after a conventional workout, more in the first 24 hours than the second. In trained lifters the rise is shorter and peaks earlier than in untrained people, so the overall response is smaller.
[Damas 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739559/)

Recovery from 3 sets of moderate loads to failure takes about 48 hours, so 3 sets 3 times a week works well: each workout raises protein synthesis from baseline and is done fully recovered.

Many strength athletes, including Olympic weightlifters, train the same muscles on consecutive days. Some of those workouts give a less efficient hypertrophy stimulus, because protein synthesis is still partly raised from the day before (how much is debatable). They still add some stimulus, but the ratio of stimulus to work done is lower. To keep post-workout fatigue down, strength athletes use heavy loads, several reps in reserve and minimal eccentric phases (Olympic lifters often use derivatives with no eccentric phase at all), so no muscle damage or CNS fatigue is left by the next day:
- In 12 trained men, 8 sets of 10 reps impaired isometric strength for 72 hours, while 8 sets of 3 with heavier loads didn't, though both raised markers of muscle damage. [Bartolomei 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28447186/)
- In 15 trained men, bench presses and half squats to failure (4 × 10 at 75% of 1RM) cut the next day's 1RM by 7% and 11%, while stopping short of failure (5 × 8) didn't. [Ramos-Campo 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7996385/)
- In 11 highly trained men, bench presses with a heavier eccentric (120% of 1RM down, 80% up) still impaired bench throw power 48 hours later, while regular 80% loading didn't. [Bartolomei 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31531133/)

It's hard to say whether training a muscle daily gives more or less total stimulus over a week than 3 times a week. It depends on how raised protein synthesis still is from the last workout, and how many stimulating reps can be done each day without too much post-workout fatigue. More fatigue-resistant lifters might benefit more. Either way, strength athletes train this way to maximize other adaptations that contribute to strength, not to optimize hypertrophy → [[Progressive overload]]. It's doubtful this approach ever makes sense when training only for bodybuilding.

## Finding your own frequency

When 20 people trained one leg 5 times a week and the other 2–3 times a week for 8 weeks, average growth and strength gains were the same. Individually, about a third grew more with the higher frequency, about a third with the lower one, and the rest responded similarly to both.
[Damas 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30289872/)

So the best frequency is the highest one a muscle can recover from.
