> [!summary] Bottom line
> More sets generally produce more muscle growth, with diminishing returns. Later sets add less for three reasons: fatigue builds up and lowers recruitment and fiber tension, damage rises and diverts protein synthesis to repair, and protein synthesis probably has a ceiling that a few sets can reach. The model discussed below predicts that rising muscle damage may eventually offset the extra stimulus, with the useful volume differing between people and muscles. My practical takeaway is to prioritize high-quality sets over simply adding more.

## Diminishing returns

In a meta-analysis of 8 studies, hypertrophy effect sizes rose with sets per exercise: 0.24 for 1 set, 0.34 for 2–3 sets and 0.44 for 4–6 sets. Multiple sets gave about 40% more growth than one set, and 2–3 vs 4–6 sets didn't differ significantly.
[Krieger 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20300012/)

Counting weekly sets per muscle across 15 studies, each extra weekly set added about 0.37 percentage points of growth, and higher-volume groups gained 3.9 percentage points more than lower-volume groups within the same studies.
[Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/)

A later meta-regression of 67 studies also found growth keeps rising with weekly volume, with diminishing returns ([[Swelling in hypertrophy studies]]).

Higher weekly volumes do stimulate more growth, but each later set in a workout adds much less than the earlier ones. There seem to be three reasons: fatigue builds up, muscle damage rises, and protein synthesis has a ceiling.

## The value of each set

Strength and Conditioning Research models this with a curve where the first set gives 1 arbitrary unit (AU) of stimulus and 6 sets give 2 AU:

| Sets per workout | Total stimulus (AU) | Added by that set |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 2 | 1.39 | 0.39 |
| 3 | 1.61 | 0.23 |
| 4 | 1.77 | 0.16 |
| 5 | 1.90 | 0.13 |
| 6 | 2.00 | 0.10 |
| 9 | 2.23 | about 0.07 each |

So the second set is worth about 39% of the first, and the fifth about 13%. That argues for making each set count rather than adding more: raising motor unit recruitment with more stable exercises, lower coordination demands and less working muscle mass ([[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]).

## Fatigue builds up over a workout

Fatigue accumulates over a workout and is greatest in the later sets. [[Calcium ion-related fatigue|Calcium ion-related fatigue]] lowers single fiber tension, and central nervous system fatigue lowers how many fibers are activated ([[Supraspinal CNS fatigue]]), so later sets give a smaller hypertrophy stimulus.

In isolated rat muscle, eccentric contractions caused more low-frequency fatigue, the kind linked to calcium, than isometric or concentric contractions, and fast glycolytic fibers were more affected than fast oxidative ones.
[Rijkelijkhuizen 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14508688/)

Central fatigue builds up over repeated maximal efforts:
- Over 100 maximal plantar flexions, voluntary activation fell from 96% to 70%. [Kawakami 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10846007/)
- Over 9 bouts of 10 maximal plantar flexions, voluntary activation fell 12.6%. [Nordlund 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12972439/)
- During 3 minutes of maximal elbow extension, voluntary activation fell more when the contraction was continuous than with short rests. [Bilodeau 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16691603/)

In 12 trained men doing 6 sets of squats with a 10RM load, sets to failure caused more peripheral fatigue and muscle damage than sets of 5 reps, but central fatigue was similar and stayed depressed for up to 48 hours either way.
[González-Hernández 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33306589/)

## Damage rises with every set

In rat muscle, structural damage rose steadily with the number of forced lengthening contractions.
[Hesselink 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8814552/)

In rats, 70 and 150 stretch-shortening contractions reduced force and increased degenerating fibers and inflammation 48 hours later, while 30 didn't, and 150 caused more fiber degeneration than 70.
[Baker 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762360/)

Later sets start with more calcium already built up inside the fibers, so each one may cause more damage than the last ([[Calcium ion-related fatigue]]). Damage also uses up new protein. In 10 young men, muscle protein synthesis was highest after the first workouts of a program, when damage was greatest, but it wasn't related to growth then. Only after 3 and 10 weeks, once damage had faded, did protein synthesis track hypertrophy (r ≈ 0.9).
[Damas 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5023708/)

Damage competes with growth for the rise in myofibrillar protein synthesis after a workout. When fibers need repair, they use that rise for repair instead of hypertrophy or [[Sarcomerogenesis|sarcomerogenesis]], so rising damage cuts the net growth from later sets. A review argues the early rises in protein synthesis go mostly to repairing damage, and only contribute to growth once damage has faded.
[Damas 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29282529/)

If damage climbs faster with each set while the growth stimulus levels off, the net stimulus of a workout should peak at some number of sets and then fall. In the same model, a more easily damaged muscle peaks around 6 sets per workout, and a less easily damaged one around 8. The real numbers will differ for every person and muscle.

## Protein synthesis has a ceiling

After a workout, myofibrillar protein synthesis rises temporarily because ribosomes translate protein faster (greater translational efficiency). There's a limit to how far translational efficiency can rise, so there's probably a limit to how high protein synthesis can go in a fiber after a hypertrophy stimulus. Once a few sets reach that limit, extra sets can't raise it further. Longer-term growth also depends on making more ribosomes (translational capacity).
[Figueiredo 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31508978/)
