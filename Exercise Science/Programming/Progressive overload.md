> [!summary] Bottom line
> Progressive overload means lifting more weight for the same reps, or more reps with the same weight. Both come from either more maximum strength or more relative muscular endurance. Maximum strength comes from six adaptations that happen on different timelines, from coordination in the first days to fiber growth and lower antagonist coactivation over years. Endurance gains are limited to the first few weeks and likely come from better buffering of metabolites. Training for maximum strength means managing fatigue more carefully than training for size, because fatigue blocks the neural adaptations.

## Repetition strength

Progressive overload is an increase in either the load lifted for the same reps or the reps done with the same load. Both reflect more repetition strength, which comes from either:
- **More maximum strength:** can keep rising for years, and rises more with lower rep ranges.
- **More relative muscular endurance:** limited to the first few weeks of a program, and rises more with higher rep ranges.

In 26 men training leg extensions to failure for 6 weeks, 80% and 30% of 1RM grew muscle thickness similarly, but 80% increased 1RM and isometric strength more, along with voluntary activation and muscle activity.
[Jenkins 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5447067/)

## Six adaptations behind maximum strength

1. **Coordination:** the joints producing the movement work together more efficiently → [[Improvements in coordination traits]].
2. **Less antagonist coactivation:** the opposing muscle at each joint brakes less.
3. **Recruiting high-threshold motor units:** more agonist fibers activated in maximal efforts → [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]].
4. **Thicker and longer fibers:** more actin-myosin cross bridges per fiber → [[Sarcomerogenesis]].
5. **Lateral force transmission:** force from each sarcomere's cross bridges reaches the tendon more efficiently.
6. **Tendon stiffness:** a stiffer tendon stretches less, so fibers shorten more slowly in concentric contractions and, by the force-velocity relationship, form more crossbridges → [[Tendon adaptation]].

## When each happens

Coordination improves first, in the first days and weeks. In 15 men doing maximal isometric dorsiflexions, force and muscle activity stabilized within the first 10 attempts, and force was 11% higher 3 days later.
[Green 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24552367/)
→ [[Improvements in coordination traits]]

The ability to recruit high-threshold motor units also improves quickly, but only once coordination is good enough. After 4 weeks of isometric knee extension training, maximal force rose 20% in the trained leg and 8% in the untrained leg. Agonist muscle activity rose 26% in the trained leg, while antagonist coactivation fell in both legs.
[Tillin 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21319165/)

Tendon stiffness, lateral force transmission and fiber length come next and likely plateau within a couple of months. After 9 weeks of heavy leg extensions in 42 young men, the quadriceps' strength per unit of muscle (specific tension) rose 17%, but single fiber tension didn't change and fiber type shifts didn't explain it, which points to better lateral force transmission.
[Erskine 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20889606/)
→ [[Tendon adaptation]]

In isolated muscle fiber bundles doing stretch-shorten cycles, more compliant artificial tendons increased power and efficiency by letting the fibers shorten at better speeds.
[Lichtwark 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20154185/)

Fiber diameter and lower antagonist coactivation develop most slowly, likely taking years to max out. In 39 untrained men doing 15 weeks of lower body training, muscle growth correlated very strongly with strength gains within each person (r = 0.92 for isometric strength, 0.89 for 1RM), while changes in muscle activity correlated moderately (r ≈ 0.57).
[Marques 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40680785/)

## Training for maximum strength

The biggest difference between training only for hypertrophy and training for the other strength adaptations is how fatigue during and after the workout is handled. Neural adaptations are a big part of strength gains, and fatigue stops them from being stimulated. Fatigue hurts hypertrophy too, but much less.

Coordination, and likely antagonist coactivation too, can't improve when a movement is practiced while fatigued. Across several experiments, practicing a skill while the muscle was fatigued impaired learning, and the deficit carried over to later practice days when the person was no longer fatigued. The effect was partly central and could be reduced by changing motor cortex function.
[Branscheidt 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6443347/)

Motor unit recruitment also improves much less when failure is reached in a fatigued state with light loads than in an unfatigued state with heavy loads. In 26 men training leg extensions to failure for 6 weeks, 80% of 1RM raised voluntary activation and muscle activity during maximal contractions more than 30% did.
[Jenkins 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5447067/)

Strength adaptations are also specific to how the training is done. In 30 women, 3 sessions of slow isokinetic leg extensions raised peak torque at slow and fast speeds, while fast training raised it only at fast speeds, with no change in surface muscle activity.
[Coburn 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194247/)

In 25 active men, heavy, slow hamstring training raised knee flexor strength 16–21%, while light, fast elastic band training raised high-speed torque 31% and improved sprinting. Hamstring coactivation fell in all groups.
[Janusevicius 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5465986/)

So to maximize strength we want as many unfatigued reps as possible, with the best-coordinated motor programs (for coordination and lower antagonist coactivation) and the highest motor unit recruitment (to build recruitment capacity).

In practice, 3 sets of 4–6 reps with 1 rep in reserve (RIR), 3 times a week, might suit an exercise when training only for size, but not for maximizing its 1RM. A simple alternative is 5 sets of 2–3 reps with 2–3 RIR. The bar weight and number of stimulating reps would be about the same, but fatigue during and after the workout would be much lower because each set stops further from failure, making neural adaptations more likely. Since fatigue wipes out the stimulus for neural adaptations, it's best to err well on the side of caution.

## Relative muscular endurance

The adaptations behind gains in relative muscular endurance are less clear. Better buffering capacity, which resists metabolite-related fatigue, is likely the main reason more reps can be done, especially with lighter loads → [[Fatigue resistance]].

More capillaries around each fiber should help in theory, but capillarization doesn't rise in proportion to endurance. Instead it rises with more oxidative fiber types and bigger fiber diameters, which suggests it's a reactive adaptation that keeps up with the oxygen and nutrient demands those changes create.

Endurance-trained men had more capillaries per fiber than untrained men (2.49 vs 1.77) with the same fiber diameter. Capillaries per fiber rose with fiber diameter, and fibers with more mitochondria had more capillaries around them.
[Brodal 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/879309/)

After 12 weeks of resistance training, biceps fibers grew and capillaries per fiber rose for both type I (4.9 to 5.5) and type II fibers (5.1 to 6.2).
[McCall 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941522/)

Four weeks of intense knee extensor training raised the capillary-to-fiber ratio from 1.7 to 2.4, though here capillarization wasn't related to fiber type.
[Jensen 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1665084/)
