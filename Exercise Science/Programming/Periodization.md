> [!summary] Bottom line
> There's little rationale for overhauling a training program every few weeks. Most training variables don't change where muscle grows, swapping exercises risks losing size in regions the new exercises don't cover, and changing a workout only changes how much fatigue it causes, not what kind. Periodization is useful for rotating biomechanically similar exercises to spare the joints, and for novelty that keeps motivation, and so effort, high.

## Hypertrophy

During a program, muscles and muscle regions only grow if they get high [[Increasing motor unit recruitment|motor unit recruitment]]. Where recruitment goes isn't changed by most training variables, including rest periods, proximity to failure, rep range and [[Training volume|volume]], so periodizing them doesn't change where growth happens. And if a variable did increase growth, it would make more sense to train that way all the time.

Exercise selection, including range of motion and resistance profile, does change where hypertrophy happens. So exercise selection could be periodized, carefully, to bring up regions that are lagging → [[Neuromechanical matching]].

## Atrophy

Muscles and regions that never get high recruitment at any point in a program shrink. So periodizing exercise selection can cause muscle loss if the new exercises don't cover the same regions as the old ones. Replacing all knee extensions with squat variations would shrink the rectus femoris, because this two-joint muscle can't contribute while the hip extensors are working → [[Quadriceps training]]. So new exercises need to cover the same muscles and regions as the old ones, unless losing size in lower-priority areas is acceptable.

## Post-workout fatigue

Changing training variables can't change which fatigue mechanisms a workout causes, only how big they are. Changing a workout only changes which muscles and regions are fatigued and the total amount of post-workout fatigue. If one workout causes more post-workout fatigue than another, it causes more of every individual fatigue mechanism. So there's no rationale for alternating blocks that cause different amounts of fatigue. It's better to find the most effective training you can fully recover from.

Heavy squats and deadlifts don't cause more central nervous system fatigue after a workout than other kinds of strength training. In 10 male athletes, heavy squats (10 × 5 at 80% of 1RM), jump squats and sprints all caused fatigue that took up to 72 hours to resolve, mostly from reduced muscle function rather than the CNS. Voluntary activation was reduced for 48 hours after the squats and 24 hours after the jumps and sprints, with no other changes in CNS function.
[Thomas 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30067591/)

## Where periodization helps

There's a rationale for rotating exercises within a range of biomechanically similar ones. All squat variations cause the same hypertrophy and can be rotated to avoid repeating exactly the same movement, which many people find bothersome for their joints. Lat pull-down variations similarly have nearly identical effects.

Motivation also matters physiologically. The maximum motor unit recruitment in a set is limited by the maximum perception of effort we can tolerate, because the central motor command copies itself to the sensory parts of the brain, creating a sense of effort. When that reaches its limit, central motor command, and so recruitment, can't go higher. Motivation raises the effort we can tolerate, so if a program change is motivating, it will increase hypertrophy.
[Marcora 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18618133/)
→ [[Psychobiological model of fatigue]]

## Conclusion

Periodization can add value by providing motivating novelty, which helps reach high motor unit recruitment, and by varying biomechanically similar exercises to avoid repeating the same movement pattern constantly. But there's no inherent rationale for alternating blocks of light and heavy loads, different volumes or frequencies, or different proximities to failure.
