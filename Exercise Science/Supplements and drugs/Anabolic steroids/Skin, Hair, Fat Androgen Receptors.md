> [!summary] Bottom line
> Exogenous AAS stimulate androgen receptor activity to produce unique effects in the skin, hair, and adipose tissue. Androgens exert very strong effects on skin androgen receptors, leading to increased sebum production and other changes that cause blocked hair follicles, which provides the ideal environment for bacterial overgrowth. This then provokes an immune response that includes inflammation, which we see as red, inflamed acne. A physician is the best place to start as there are different diagnoses (acne vulgaris and malassezia folliculitis). After that, there are solutions that can help. Androgens also exert strong effects on hair follicle androgen receptors, causing a reduction in hair size and shortening of hair cycle duration, which looks like hair loss. This can't be completely eliminated and only slowed. Also the exact AAS used affects what can be used as a treatment for slowing hair loss. And exogenous AAS can cause fat loss, but this can affect facial appearance and body shape.

AAS stimulate androgen receptor activity to produce very different effects in the skin, hair, and adipose tissue.

### Skin changes

#### AAS stimulate androgen receptors to cause skin changes

People like to blame diet and bad hygiene for acne and other skin issues but the TRT literature has shown that even relatively low doses of exogenous AAS can cause skin outbreaks in 5-10% of users ([Abou Chawareb et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41182303/)) and that injectable compounds cause more skin reactions than topical or oral, which has been attributed to high peaks in AAS dosage that has been associated with injectables ([Pastuszak et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34510812/)). Similarly, the transgender literature has shown that supraphysiological AAS caused skin issues in 100% of subjects ([Korpaisarn et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8511909/)). There are very clear mechanistic reasons for why AAS produce skin problems.

Androgens arrive at the skin from the bloodstream: Endogenous androgens (DHEA-S, androstenedione, DHT, and testosterone) and exogenous AAS circulate in the blood and arrive at the skin. While exogenous AAS might have been through ester cleavage and therefore lost their carboxylic acid tails, many might still have other features that make them different to testosterone.

The skin converts weak hormones into potent ones: The skin converts DHEA-S into DHEA with the enzyme steroid sulfatase and then converts DHEA and androstenedione with members of the 3β-HSD enzyme family and then androstenedione into testosterone with the members of 17β-HSD enzyme family. Finally, it converts testosterone into DHT by using 5a-reductase. 5a-reductase converts any exogenous AAS possessing the appropriate A-ring features on its own dihydro metabolite (DHT is simply the dihydro metabolite of testosterone). Most dihydro metabolites behave a lot like DHT, as do exogenous AAS that chemists create in the lab directly from DHT rather than from testosterone. The capacity for conversion inside the skin is why blood hormone levels will likely not show any skin-related problems from androgens. Blood hormone levels can be totally normal but the pharmaceutical machine inside the skin is converting tons of weak androgens into entirely excessive amounts of harsher ones ([Lookingbill et al. 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3157707/)).

The skin also downgrades potent hormones into weak ones: The skin uses enzymes from the 17β-HSD type 2 and 3a-HSD families to downgrade excessive amounts of potent hormones into weaker versions. Those AAS that can't be downgraded through these pathways can cause more skin problems than you might expect based just based on their androgenic potential. Obviously, 17β-HSD type 2 needs an unobstructed 17β-hydroxyl group. Oral, 17a-alkylated AAS have 17a-methyl group and obstruct that access to that location, which protects them from going through this process and causes them to punch above their weight in terms of causing acne if they're inherently androgenic (Anavar isn't, so being oral is less problematic). Similarly, 3a-HSD needs an accessible 3-keto group. Stanozolol (Winstrol) doesn't have a 3-keto group so the 3a-HSD enzyme has nothing to act on. Since it's also a 17a-alkylated AAS, stanozolol is therefore very hard to remove from the skin once it's there, which often leads to serious skin problems.

Aromatization is skin is protective: It's often claimed that aromatization is what makes skin oily and cause acne, but estrogen in the skin is protective. Obviously, endogenous and exogenous testosterone can be aromatized in the skin(as can several other exogenous AAS), producing estradiol (or sometimes estrone). Estrogen binds with estrogen receptors and suppresses sebum production. This is why women have less oily skin than men and why estrogen-based female contraception pills are sometimes prescribed for female acne. For this reason, using an aromatase inhibitor to avoid estrogenic side effects could cause an AAS user acne.

Androgens bind to sebocyte androgen receptors: Genes inside the sebaceous gland cells get switched on when the androgens bind with their androgen receptors and they make the sebocytes proliferate in certain parts of the body, like the face. This leads to an increase in the size of the glands and their capacity to make sebum ([Akamatsu et al. 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1402009/)). This increases the amount of oil on the surface of the skin. One annoying deviation from what would otherwise be an entirely androgen-mediated sequence of events is that sebocyte differentiation occurs instead through peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors, which are actually regulators of lipid metabolism (Rosenfield et al. [1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9557223/); [1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9989800/); [2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11595816/)). This is because sebocytes are actually modified fat cells that differentiate by accumulating neutral lipid droplets.

Sebum changes in quality and quantity: An increase in the production of sebum causes comparatively less linoleic acid and relatively more oleic acid ([Perisho et al. 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2964492/); [Stewart et al. 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2947544/)). The excess of oleic acid disrupts the epidermal barrier ([Li et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28567492/)), which testosterone dose independently as well ([Kao et al. 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11231319/)). This combination of excessive sebum, more oleic acid, and impaired cell walls leads to plugged hair follicles which is the ideal habitat for acne-causing bacterium that already live on the skin. The AAS is just making the habitat inside the hair follicles much more attractive and leading to bacterial overgrowth. The keratinocytes lining the follicle then recognise those bacteria through toll-like receptors and respond by releasing β-defensin-2 and interleukin-8, which is the inflammation that turns a blocked follicle into visible, red acne ([Nagy et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15854033/)).

Also WRT 5a-reductase, there are two isoforms. Ubiquitous sites like the sebaceous gland (and the brain) express type 1, while developmental sites like the dermal papilla of the hair follicle and the prostate express type 2. Thus, pharmaceuticals that inhibit only 5a-reductase type 2 (like finasteride) will have effects only on hair loss. In contrast, those pharmaceuticals that inhibit both 5a-reductase types 1 and 2 (like dutasteride) will have effects on both skin problems and hair loss.

### Managing skin changes

There are multiple dermatological remedies that are likely effective to limit the negative AAS effects on skin. Though note that the root cause is the excessive amounts of androgens and not anything else. Aromatase inhibitors aren't doing anything since the entire problem runs on androgen excess and not estrogen. Corticosteroids aren't good either because they target inflammation which is downstream of the clogged hair follicle, which is then downstream from the excessive androgens. You just turn off the natural defense system of the body. Similarly, harsh skin hygiene routines and long courses of antibiotics are misplaced logic (especially since excess scrubbing can strip off more of the damaged skin barrier). The problem is androgen load, not the existence of bacteria on the skin. Remember to get a proper diagnosis of the type of skin problem from a qualified doctor before you start as treatments differ.

For acne vulgaris:

Topical androgen receptor blockade: Clascoterone (cortexolone 17a-proprionate) is a steroid that can be applied as a topical cream on the skin. It competes with DHT and testosterone for binding to skin cell androgen receptors to the extent that it wins the battle against the arriving androgens from the exogenous AAS, it stops sebum production at the base level ([Rosette et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31141847/); [Mazzetti et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31251549/); [Del Rosso & Kircik, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38192024/)). This will not help existing acne caused by blocked hair follicles. But, if taken before starting a cycle, it should help dramatically reduce the onset of new flare-ups. But note that HPA-axis suppression has been reported in some users, which may not be well on top of AAS ([Mazzetti et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31251549/)).

Topical retinoids (adapalene, tretinoin): These are vitamin A derivatives that are applied on the skin locally. Exogenous AAS enhance the production rate of keratinocytes that line the inside of hair follicles such that they build up and cause blockages. They also cause dead keratinocytes to remain stuck together, which exacerbates the clogging effect. Retinoids bind to receptors inside keratinocytes and accelerate them through their maturation process so they leave more quickly. They also help unbind the dead cells so they don't clog the inside of the hair follicle are easily. While this treatment does help already affected skin, it has been reported to take several weeks and the initial reaction can be seem negative due to the purging of the backing of swollen hair follicles.

Systemic retinoid (isotretinoin): This is a powerful Vitamin A derivative with intended effects that are uniquely suited to AAS users. It shrinks the sebaceous gland so that it can't respond to that androgen load. There are 3 main risks though that indicate you should have medical supervision. It's hepatotoxic, so stacking with AAS that are heavily processed through the liver isn't smart. It raises triglycerides and lowers HDL, so stacking with non-aromatizing AAS that do the same can be dangerous. And starting right with high doses can cause acne fulminans which you do not want. So you need a physician to prescribe the proper dose for your exact situation. 

For malassezia folliculitis:

Anti-fungal wash: Malassezia folliculitis is an itchy yeast infection that feeds on the excessive lipids in sebum and commonly forms on the torso (upper back, chest, shoulders). It looks like acne vulgaris but is more uniform in nature and in distribution. As a yeast infection, it responds pretty quickly to anti-fungal topical washes. If you mistake it for acne, you could waste a lot of time of treatment time with ineffective interventions which is why it's good to get a diagnosis from a physician.

### Hair loss

#### AAS stimulate androgen receptors to cause hair loss

Androgens cause head hair follicles at the front and middle of the scalp to produce progressively thinner, shorter, and less pigmented hairs with each successive cycle of their normal lifespan. This creates the male pattern baldness effect. The back and sides of the scalp aren't similarly affected. AAS are going to do this exact thing, but faster. The physiology is very well-documented.

Androgens arrive at the scalp from the bloodstream: Endogenous androgens (DHEA-S, androstenedione, DHT, and testosterone) and exogenous AAS circulate in the blood and arrive at the skin of the scalp. While exogenous AAS have been through ester cleavage and have lost their carboxylic acid tails, many might still have features that differ them from testosterone.

The dermal papilla cells convert weak hormones to strong ones: Arriving endogenous or exogenous testosterone is converted into DHT automatically by dermal papilla cells by using 5a-reductase. Arriving DHT doesn't need conversion. 5a-reductase also converts any exogenous AAS with the proper A-ring features to its own dihydro metabolite (DHT is just the dihydro metabolite of testosterone). For example, boldenone (Equipoise) gets reduced into dihydroboldenone and methandrostenolone (Dianabol) gets reduced into 5a-dihydromethandrostenolone. Most dihydro metabolites are functionally equivalent to DHT as far as the scalp and androgen receptors go but some aren't. The best example of an exogenous AAS that goes through 5a-reduction but creates a dihydro metabolite that interacts more weakly with the androgen receptor than DHT is nandrolone (Deca), which is converted into DHN, which has low affinity with the scalp androgen receptors.

Androgens bind to dermal papilla androgen receptors: Endogenous DHT and the dihydro metabolites of exogenous AAS that were converted by 5a-reductase bind to the androgen receptor of the dermal papilla cells. Also, any arriving exogenous AAS that were constructed by chemists in the lab from DHT rather than testosterone will bind right to the androgen receptor of the dermal papilla cells without first undergoing changes in the body. They don't need to be converted and most, but not all, will have similar effects to endogenous DHT, but this depends on the specific chemical modifications. Good examples of hair-unfriendly DHT-based exogenous AAS are drostanolonoe (Masteron), mesterolone (Proviron), and oxymetholone (Anadrol). The best example of a DHT-based exogenous AAS that interacts more weakly than DHT itself at the androgen receptors is oxandrolone (Anavar). After the arriving androgens bind with their androgen receptors, the dermal papilla cells then change their instructions to their hair follicles.

Dermal papilla cells tell the hair follicle to grow back smaller: Normally, hair follicles alternate between phases of active growth (years) with regression phases in which the follicle shrinks (weeks), then by resting phasese where old hair is shed and a new one grows back in its place (months). If the new hair were the same size as the old hair, then you wouldn't really see any detrimental effect of "hair loss" because it would just be "hair loss and replacement happening. But after getting the signal from DHT, the dermal papilla cells tell the hair follicles to reduce proliferation (which reduces the size of the hairs) and to end the current growth cycle early (which causes the hairs to fall out and grow back, but smaller this time).

Androgens induce apoptosis of dermal papilla cells: Not only all that, but androgens also remove the brake on apoptosis of dermal papilla cells (Wrobel et al. 2000), which takes them completely out of the picture. Any hair follicle that loses its dermal papilla cells won't grow back at all, which is the genuine phenomenon we describe as hair loss.

### Managing hair loss

There are several ways to slow down hair loss from AAS but none are completely effective. You could probably manage skin acne with proper planning, with the right treatments from a trusted physician, and not doing dumb things, you can't really do the same with hair loss. You can only lose it more slowly.

Topical androgen receptor antagonist: RU58841 is often referenced as its a non-steroidal topical anti-androgen that was never made commercially available but does have some research behind it on monkeys with male pattern balding (Uno et al. 1996; [Pan et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9798729/)). There are alternatives to RU58841 in development (including clascoterone, which has already been tested for reducing acne) ([Sun & Sebaratnam, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32421862/)). Just as the androgen receptor antagonist worked for the skin, it works for hair. By taking up the receptor in the dermal papilla cells, the androgens can't produce their own effects, this will work the same regardless of the AAS, because they all must interact with the dermal papilla cells at the same spot. But this is still a competition between the AAS and the androgen receptor antagonist, so it's far from perfect.

5a-reductase inhibitors (finasteride and dutasteride): These stop the conversion of testosterone into the more powerfully active DHT. They also stop the conversion of exogenous AAS that can be 5a-reduced into their dihydro metabolite equivalents of DHT.
But other androgens can continue binding to the androgen receptor of the dermal papilla cells to produce the androgenic hair loss, so the best pairing is with testosterone-based AAS. Also, while these compounds do work to reduce male pattern baldness in men with physiological testosterone levels ([Kaufman et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9777765/)), supraphysiological levels of AAS are going to show that quantity has a quality all of its own. And if you're going to use any AAS derived from DHT (like masteron, proviron, or oxandrolone), then you're completely bypassing the protective system in place.

Topical minoxidil: By acting as a vasodilator, the compound pushes the hair follicle cycle towards a longer life, which counteracts the androgens tendency to push it towards a shorter life (and therefore faster size reductions over multiple cycles). Very much just slowing the process that is going along rather than stopping it, but there really isn't a perfect solution here.

### Fat loss

#### AAS stimulate androgen receptors to cause fat loss

The TRT literature has made it clear that even relatively low doses of exogenous AAS is able to reduce body fat percentages and proxy measures like waist circumference, especially in men with worse body composition at baseline ([Tchernof et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30215860/)), although visceral adipose tissue can trend in the opposite direction when AAS load is higher ([Rasmussen et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28500659/)). A mechanistic analysis shows that fat loss is caused by AAS through 3 pathways that are androgen receptor-dependent.

Inhibition of adipogenesis: We know that AAS block preadipocyte differentiation into mature, lipid storing adipocytes through a pathway that involves the androgen receptor because androgen receptor antagonists (like flutamide and bicalutamide) are able to impede the effect, at least partially (Singh et al. [2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12960001/); [2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4417624/)). The effects are dose-dependent, such that greater doses of AAS create a larger blocking effect ([Gupta et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18801408/); [Blouin et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19500113/)). Also, they act one step before preadipocyte differentiation by impairing the commitment of subcutaneous adipose stem cells to preadipocytes ([Chazenbalk et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3951890/)).

Stimulation of lipolysis: AAS routinely increase lipolysis in a variety of animal and human studies ([Xu et al. 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1846106/); [Rebuffé-Scrive et al. 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1778664/); [Elbers et al. 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10582544/); [Karbowska et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22951290/)). Decreased lipolytic activity in androgen-receptor null mice indicates that this mechanism is similarly androgen-dependent ([Fan et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15793238/)). Also, circulating androgen levels are associated with lipolytic activity (Belanger et al. 2006), which indicates that this aspect of the AAS effect is also dose-responsive.

Reduction of lipid storage: AAS decrease lipoprotein lipase activity and triglyceride uptake in adipose tissue compartments ([Rebuffé-Scrive et al. 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1778664/); [Mårin et al. 1992](https://europepmc.org/article/med/1335979)). The reduction of lipoprotein lipase activity is also dose-responsive, with greater levels of androgens producing a more pronounced effect ([Blouin et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19500113/)). Lipoprotein lipase facilitates the hydrolysis of triglycerides with free fatty acids and glycerol for storage purposes. Thus, a dose responsive reduction in this enzyme indicates a dose-responsive effect of AAS on lipid storage. While there's no clear connection with androgen receptors for this mechanism, the dose-responsiveness does indicate that the same mechanistic basis applies.

AAS are stopping the formation of new adipocytes and preventing the storage of lipids in adipocytes while also upregulating the lipolytic process through which stored triglycerides are hydrolyzed into glycerol and free fatty acids. This means that AAS are going to produce fat loss naturally in a similar way they produce hypertrophy naturally. But this can change your facial appearance as well as your body shape. 

### Androgen receptor markers

Androgen receptor markers are in bloodwork if you know where to look

Medical testing practices don't include androgen receptor markers deliberately ([Gibbons et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10962511/)). The focus tends to be on the endocrine system, rather than the more important intracrine system. But everyone does measure hematocrit at least, and this is a good marker for androgen receptor activity. especially after taking blood volume changes into account. And an advanced AAS user can take a PSA panel which is another solid indicator.

### Practical implications

Think about skin issues prior to a cycle: Some of the ways acne can be avoided require the start of treatment before you start AAS, so planning ahead can be the best investment for skin health

If you already have acne, get diagnosed: While all acne looks bad, they're distinct and need different treatments depending on what you have. Get a physician to diagnose the problem and this can save you a lot of time and effort. Some treatments that are genuinely effective are also very dangerous when stacked with AAS that tax the liver and elevate cardiovascular risk factors. Getting a physician to give personalized, correct info WRT is essential

Take the AAS into account with hair loss: Many people panic when they notice hair falling out after starting a cycle, so it's smart to plan ahead, especially WRT matching the hair treatment with the AAS type. Note that testosterone-based compounds will cause hair loss when the dermal papilla cells convert it into DHT by 5a-reductase. And inhibitors of that (finasteride and dutasteride) can be helpful for reducing its effects (but testosterone tiself still binds with androgen receptors in the dermal papilla cells to produce negative effects). Also DHT-based compounds (like masteron, proviron, or oxandrolone) are completely bypassing these inhibitors so it's useless to combine.
