> [!summary] Bottom line
> Exogenous AAS elevate free testosterone relative to existing endogenous baseline levels. The hypothalamus and anterior pituitary gland read the elevation as excess above their set-point and decrease production of LH to reduce the output of endogenous testosterone in the testes. This reduces testicular volume and sperm count. The hypothalamus and anterior pituitary gland can only reduce LH to zero, it can't go negative. Since maintenance of the prior set-point is the objective, LH is reduced to near zero whenever the amount of androgens arriving exogenously matches the rate that the testes previously produced it. So, even at TRT doses of AAS, it suppresses LH to minimal levels and subsequently reduces testicular volume and sperm count to the same extent as higher doses of AAS.

AAS disrupt the HPG axis which is the main example of endocrine system dysfunction caused by AAS. HPG axis dysregulation has substantial downstream effects on the testes, leading to reduced testicular volume and subsequently reduced sperm count.

### AAS suppress the HPG axis

The HPG axis is an important neuroendocrine system comprised of the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and gonads (testes and ovaries). At the top level of the HPG axis sits gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). GnRH is a neuropeptide produced by neurons in the hypothalamus. Its pulsatile secretion stimulates the anterior pituitary gland to release FSH and LH. FSH acts on Sertoli cells (which have FSH receptors). Sertoli cells are very specialized cells located in the seminiferous tubules of the testes that are needed for spermatogenesis (sperm production). Similarly, LH acts on Leydig cells (which have LH receptors). Leydig cells respond to stimulation by LH by increasing testosterone production inside the testes

Exogenous AAS and their aromatized estrogens hit the HPG axis in multiple places and lead to suppression of both GnRH and LH secretion:

Direct feedback at the hypothalamus: AAS binds to the androgen receptors in the hypothalamus. The body reads this as high levels of endogenous testosterone and inhibits GnRH pulses. The neurons that produce GnRH pulses don't have any androgen receptors, which necessitates communication within the hypothalamus between neurons.

Direct feedback at pituitary: AAS binds to androgen receptors in the gonadotroph cells of the anterior pituitary. The body reads this as high levels of endogenous testosterone and inhibits LH release directly.

Amplification by estrogen: Certain AAS compounds can be converted to estradiol by aromatase. Like testosterone, estradiol has receptors in neurons of the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus and in anterior pituitary gland gonadotrophs (so adding to both of the above mechanisms). Aromatase activity is increased dose-responsively in relation to AAS dose and fat mass, so this mechanism is greater in bodybuilders who are using more AAS and have worse body composition.

The primary end result of this GnRH/LH secretion suppression is the reduction of LH and FSH secretion, which has been recorded multiple times in the literature in response to exogenous AAS use ([Christou et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28258581/)).

### Dose and duration of suppression

We might assume that the suppression of the HPG axis by AAS is dose-responsive, but it's not that simple.

It's worth noting that in the literature looking at the relationship between AAS and LH/FSH suppression, you need to note the AAS that was used and whether endogenous testosterone was also suppressed and how. Like mesterolone is odd and doesn't suppress LH/FSH at any dose ([Aakvaag & Strømme, 1974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4479415/); [Jackaman et al. 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/872444/)). And while [Bhasin et al. 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11701431/) did test multiple doses, they used a GnRH-agonist to suppress endogenous testosterone, which resulted in all groups having similar levels of LH/FSH suppression which we don't know if it had to do with AAS.

But anyways, there are some pretty reliable conclusions.

There is no dose response: While good dose-response analyses do exist, we can look at the suppression produced in different studies that have tested weekly doses. Studies testing single doses have shows large LH/FSH suppression at both TRT doses ([Small et al. 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6430603/); [Remes et al. 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/145651/); [Meriggiola et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9647551/); [McLachlan et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14715841/)) and higher doses ([Ruokonen et al. 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3160892/); [Alen et al. 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3661817/); [Daly et al. 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12573299/)). So any exogenous AAS elevates free testosterone compared to existing endogenous baseline levels, which the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary see as excess relative to their set-point and then start decreasing production. This means the maximum suppression is the point when exogenous AAS is equal to endogenous production, which is going to be below TRT levels (because TRT levels are prescribed to raise testosterone levels above existing endogenous production).

There is duration-dependence: It's worth noting that the time spent exposed to AAS makes a much larger difference to the HPG axis than the dose, even an extremely high dose for a short duration ([Daly et al. 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12573299/))  couldn't completely suppress LH. While TRT doses for longer times completely dropped LH and FSH way below what's normally the minimum detectable level without ultrasensitive research-grade assays ([Meriggiola et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9647551/); [Zhang et al. 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10523008/); [Gu et al. 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12574181/); [McLachlan et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14715841/)). Most people assume increasing side effects that occur with increasing AAS doses are due to increasing negative feedback from the endocrine system. But they're almost certainly caused by all of the other mechanisms that were described (mainly oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and androgen receptor over-activation).

### Effects on the testes

Testicular volume reduces in response to AAS use. The reductions largely track the gonadotropin signals from the HPG axis, which are completely dropped above around 50mg/week. So, the lack of dose-responsiveness that we see for LH and FSH is reflected in the testicular volume changes. So, all practical AAS doses seem to have the same shrinking effects on the testes.

The changes take time to happen though. Typically shorter duration protocols have reported smaller reductions in testicular volume of 10-20% ([Palacios et al. 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6788960/); [Zhang et al. 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10523008/)), while longer durations have shown larger changes of 20-25% ([Wu et al. 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8774299/)). The trajectory isn't linar, though, and shrinkage tends to be faster at the outset and slower later on. Also, while the number 10-25% seem small, only 75% of the testes is comprised of germinal compartment, so it's like 1/3 of the shrinkable mass that goes away.

With the drop in testicular volume, AAS users also go through either oligospermia (low sperm in the semen) or azoospermia (the complete absence of sperm). This has routinely been seen starting from the high-end of TRT dosage levels (200mg/week) in multiple studies ([Palacios et al. 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6788960/); [Wu et al. 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8774299/); [Meriggiola et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9647551/); [Zhang et al. 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10523008/); [Gu et al. 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12574181/)). Oligospermia or azoospermia appear a lot faster than the actual loss of testicular mass. As gonadotropin and intratesticular testosterone levels drop, they signal to remove the mature spermatide and late spermatocytes first, while leaving the less mature compartment intact. So by the time the average AAS or TRT user has noticed a small reduction in testes size, they'll already have lost the ability to procreate (temporarily).

The extent that testicular volume and spermatogenesis can recover and how long it takes has been studied ([Shankara-Narayana et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32030409/)) but likely varies between AAS users and TRT users according to the amount of damage that was experienced and also varies based on the duration spent using AAS.

#### Medications

Your physician can talk to you about hCG (Human Chorionic Gonadotropin) in combination with purified FSH to artificially support your LH and FSH levels and stop the majority of the decline in testicular volume and sperm count ([Stocks et al. 2024](https://www.fertstert.org/article/S0015-0282%2824%2901305-0/pdf)). Since everyone will have this problem that takes TRT and it's the same for AAS users, it shouldn't be hard to find a qualified person to help you with as it's a common occurrence.

### Endocrine markers

#### Endocrine markers make up the majority of bloodwork info

Medical testing practices frequently have many hormone markers ([Gibbons et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10962511/)). They probably have more endocrine system info than they need.

#### Practical implications

Think about your procreative goals before using AAS: Get a baseline semen analysis before using AAS and consider banking sperm if parenthood is a definite goal of yours. If you are planning on having kids while you're still using TRT or AAS, then consider hCG and FSH therapy under medical supervision, while monitoring sperm count every 6 months.
