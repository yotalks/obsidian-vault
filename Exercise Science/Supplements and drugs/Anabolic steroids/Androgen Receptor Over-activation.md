AAS stimulate androgen receptor activity to cause growth and this can be a problem for different organs in your body, especially the heart, kidney, and red blood cells.

### Androgen receptors

AAS cause androgen receptor-dependent cardiac and renal hypertrophy (and skeletal muscle hypertrophy).

The first step happens when the testosterone binds to the intracellular androgen receptor, which acts as a ligand-dependent transcription factor. When ligand binds, the androgen receptor translocates to the nucleus where it binds to androgen response elements and regulates gene expression (Shen et al. 2014, Duran et al. 2017). We can see the importance of androgen receptors for growth in both the heart and kidney because androgen receptor-deficient mice don't have cardiac hypertrophy after supraphysiological testosterone (Kelly et al. 2026) and the application of an androgen receptor antagonist prevents cardiac and renal hypertrophy (Shen et al. 2015, Duran et al. 2017).

To signal for hypertrophy, androgen receptor activation turns on mTOR anabolic pathway, which increases MPS and increased cardiac muscle mass (Chen et al. 2022). This is the same process that happens after strength training workouts. But there's also an increase in AMPK-driven glucose metabolism that gives ATP (Troncoso et al. 2021), likely to power the MPS, and isn't shared with strength training. There are indications that activity in the mTOR anabolic pathway may decline over time in response to persistent AAS use (Pirompol et al. 2016). This may suggest an inherent negative feedback loop on this stimulus or just indicate that growth saturates with a certain dose at a certain point.

### Pathological heart growth

Mechanistically, the heart is the most well-studied organ from the androgen-receptor driven hypertrophy POV. When we look at studies of long term cardiac growth in humans after AAS use, we see the following.

Cardiac hypertrophy happens fast: In a prospective cohort study, an average dose of 900mg/week of testosterone (or equivalent of other AAS) for 16 weeks led to measurable left ventricular hypertrophy that was accompanied by a reduction in ejection fraction (Smit et al. 2021a).

Cardiac hypertrophy impairs function: Unlike hypertrophy of skeletal muscle, increased cardiac muscle mass with AAS is linked to reduced heart function (Smit et al. 2021a; Fyksen et al. 2022; 2025). It often looks like congenital cardiac pathological hypertrophy conditions that similarly present with reduced function (Grandperrin et al. 2023).

Cardiac hypertrophy is duration-dependent: Meta-analytical review found left ventricular mass index and posterior wall thickness increased with increasing exposure to AAS (Abdullah et al. 2024; Fernandes et al. 2026). Left ventricular hypertrophy also correlates with weekly AAS dose (Smit et al. 2021a). Functional impairments similarly increase over time, ultimately reaching very bad levels after 10 years of consistent use (Abdullah et al. 2024).

Cardiac hypertrophy happens to around half of AAS users: Compared to complete absence of symptoms in strength-trained non-users, around half of AAS users had left ventricular hypertrophy or another measure of cardiac hypertrophy (Fyksen et al. 2022; Hammoud et al. 2023). This contradicts claims that cardiac hypertrophy is just an adaptation to strength training and also indicates most AAS users will likely face this problem eventually given enough time.

Cardiac hypertrophy is reversible (but function might not be): Generally, cardiac hypertrophy reverts to normal after you stop using AAS (Smit et al. 2021a; Fykaan et al. 2025). But cardiac function tends to only revert back to normal if use was short (Smit et al. 2021a; Abdullah et al. 2024; Fyksen et al. 2025).

Cardiac hypertrophy includes fibrosis: It's assumed that the increased cardiac mass is just an increase in cardiomyocyte size, but there's data that points to at least a proportion of it being collagen (Rocha et al. 2007; Di Fazio et al. 2025), which makes sense given that AAS elevate collagen synthesis rates. It also helps explain why increased cardiac mass usually leads to reduced function rather than increased function. It also helps explain why a reversal in cardiac hypertrophy after you stop using it doesn't fix impairments in cardiac function  (Abdullah et al. 2024; Fyksen et al. 2025).

Cardiac hypertrophy = more CVD: After cardiac hypertrophy (and related damage), AAS users have a 2-3x greater risk of cardiovascular morbidity/mortality than non-users (Thiblin et al. 2015; Horwitz et al. 2018) and they tend not to seek help (Henriksen et al. 2024). In line with previous findings, AAS users tends to have more non-ischemic heart disease related to cardiac hypertrophy than ischemic heart disease (related to blocked coronary heart disease from atherosclerosis) (Horwitz et al. 2018; Di Fazio et al. 2025). AAS dose has a dose/duration response relationship to CVD risk (Thibilin et al. 2015) in the same way it does for cardiac hypertrophy and functional impairment (Smit et al. 2021a; Abdullah et al. 2024; Fernandes et al. 2026).

### Pathological growth of kidney

Mechanistically, renal hypertrophy is way less studied than cardiac hypertrophy. But we do have a reasonable picture and what happens in the kidneys is very similar to what happens in the heart, at least when looking at animal studies. The kidney grows through both cellular size increases and fibrosis, and the only difference from the heart is that kidney hypertrophy can become atrophy if fibrosis gets too severe and destroys function.

Renal hypertrophy happens fast: Animal studies show very rapid onset of kidney growth with meaningful mass gains happening in just 4 weeks (Mills & Bardin, 1980, Hoseini et al. 2009; Bento-Silva et al. 2010; Brasil et al. 2015; Tofighi et al. 2018; De Lima et al. 2020). Note that 4 weeks in rodent studies is way longer in humans, so we'd expect meaningful changes in around 1-2 years in AAS users. So probably not as fast as cardiac hypertrophy.

Renal hypertrophy is linked to altered function: The early hypertrophy seen in 4 weeks generally doesn't mess with kidney function. Kidney dysfunction usually takes around 8 weeks (Brasil et al. 2015; Tsitsimpikou et al. 2016; Tofighi et al. 2018; De Lima et al. 2020; Odoom et al. 2024; Alqahtani et al. 2025).

Duration-dependence of renal hypertrophy: The renal hypertrophy and fibrosis develop pretty linearly with time, but the developing dysfunction is more non-linear (Brasil et al. 2015; De Lima et al. 2020). Sometimes, the hypertrophy can turn to atrophy (Tsitsimpikou et al. 2016; Alqahtani et al. 2025),, likely due to damage leading to apoptosis (Riezzo et al. 2014).

Reversibility of renal hypertorphy: Mechanistically, renal hypertrophy should be largely reversible due to the role of androgen receptors in producing the effect (Shen et al. 2015). The exception would be the fibrosis that can't be removed after. Some data does show difficulty in reversing kidney hypertrophy even after short durations (Kahal & Allem, 2018).

Relationship with fibrosis: In line with the non-linear time-course of renal dysfunction, fibrosis doesn't appear extensively in the early phases of kidney hypertrophy (Hoseini et al. 2009; Brasil et al. 2015; De Lima et al. 2020) but grows fast after (Tsitsimpikou et al. 2016; Alqahtani et al. 2025).

### Medications

There are medications that can probably help with the fibrosis that happens after cardiac and renal hypertrophy (but there's likely nothing that can stop androgen receptor-driven hypertrophy of either organ without stopping using AAS). They are common medications given to people with CVD and chronic kidney disease (CKD). 

These medications do have very dangerous interaction effects with NSAIDs and diuretics, though.

There are 3 groups of medications that can help with the fibrosis, though they are prescription only.

ACE inhibitors: (Enalapril, lisinopril) block the production of angiotensin II. Angiotensin II exerts its effects directly through an AT1 receptor and by upregulating aldosterone. Angiotensin II acting on AT1 and aldosterone are each definitely pro-fibrotic. ACE inhibitors are the more general compound that act on two pathways partially. ARBs and MRAs each target one of the two pathways more aggressively.

ARBs: These block the AT1 receptor that angiotensin II acts upon.

MRAs: (Spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone) block the aldosterone receptor. MRAs are sometimes stacked with ACEs or ARBs in heart failure patients because aldosterone can be activated by other pathways independently of angiotensin II.
