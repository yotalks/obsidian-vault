> [!summary] Bottom line
> Exogenous AAS cause parts of the heart and kidney to grow and cause functional impairment. This growth seems to be largely reversible, but the impairment isn't unless the AAS use was relatively short. The growth (and associated damage) converts to greater risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality that is dose-responsive with AAS usage. Research shows that a large portion of AAS users face some degree of pathological cardiac hypertrophy and associated impairment at some point in their careers, and risk only increases with dose and duration. AAS users should invest in a yearly echocardiogram to track the changes in cardiac hypertrophy and function. Greater care over kidney function monitoring is also recommended with a simple blood pressure test and urine ACR or urine PCR being cheap and effective options.

AAS stimulate androgen receptor activity to cause growth and this can be a problem for different organs in your body, especially the heart, kidney, and red blood cells.

### Androgen receptors

AAS cause androgen receptor-dependent cardiac and renal hypertrophy (and skeletal muscle hypertrophy).

The first step happens when the testosterone binds to the intracellular androgen receptor, which acts as a ligand-dependent transcription factor. When ligand binds, the androgen receptor translocates to the nucleus where it binds to androgen response elements and regulates gene expression ([Shen et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25701874/), [Duran et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5601904/)). We can see the importance of androgen receptors for growth in both the heart and kidney because androgen receptor-deficient mice don't have cardiac hypertrophy after supraphysiological testosterone ([Kelly et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13605258/)) and the application of an androgen receptor antagonist prevents cardiac and renal hypertrophy ([Shen et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25701874/), [Duran et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5601904/)).

To signal for hypertrophy, androgen receptor activation turns on mTOR anabolic pathway, which increases MPS and increased cardiac muscle mass ([Chen et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8859925/)). This is the same process that happens after strength training workouts. But there's also an increase in AMPK-driven glucose metabolism that gives ATP ([Troncoso et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7863443/)), likely to power the MPS, and isn't shared with strength training. There are indications that activity in the mTOR anabolic pathway may decline over time in response to persistent AAS use ([Pirompol et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26850730/)). This may suggest an inherent negative feedback loop on this stimulus or just indicate that growth saturates with a certain dose at a certain point.

### Pathological heart growth

Mechanistically, the heart is the most well-studied organ from the androgen-receptor driven hypertrophy POV. When we look at studies of long term cardiac growth in humans after AAS use, we see the following.

Cardiac hypertrophy happens fast: In a prospective cohort study, an average dose of 900mg/week of testosterone (or equivalent of other AAS) for 16 weeks led to measurable left ventricular hypertrophy that was accompanied by a reduction in ejection fraction ([Smit et al. 2021a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580689/)).

Cardiac hypertrophy impairs function: Unlike hypertrophy of skeletal muscle, increased cardiac muscle mass with AAS is linked to reduced heart function ([Smit et al. 2021a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580689/); Fyksen et al. [2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9540672/); [2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12323528/)). It often looks like congenital cardiac pathological hypertrophy conditions that similarly present with reduced function ([Grandperrin et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10362519/)).

Cardiac hypertrophy is duration-dependent: Meta-analytical review found left ventricular mass index and posterior wall thickness increased with increasing exposure to AAS ([Abdullah et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37992194/); [Fernandes et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13528975/)). Left ventricular hypertrophy also correlates with weekly AAS dose ([Smit et al. 2021a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580689/)). Functional impairments similarly increase over time, ultimately reaching very bad levels after 10 years of consistent use ([Abdullah et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37992194/)).

Cardiac hypertrophy happens to around half of AAS users: Compared to complete absence of symptoms in strength-trained non-users, around half of AAS users had left ventricular hypertrophy or another measure of cardiac hypertrophy ([Fyksen et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9540672/); [Hammoud et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37758530/)). This contradicts claims that cardiac hypertrophy is just an adaptation to strength training ([Hassan & Kamal, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3612416/)) and also indicates most AAS users will likely face this problem eventually given enough time.

Cardiac hypertrophy is reversible (but function might not be): Generally, cardiac hypertrophy reverts to normal after you stop using AAS ([Smit et al. 2021a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580689/); [Fyksen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12323528/)). But cardiac function tends to only revert back to normal if use was short ([Smit et al. 2021a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580689/); [Abdullah et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37992194/); [Fyksen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12323528/)).

Cardiac hypertrophy includes fibrosis: It's assumed that the increased cardiac mass is just an increase in cardiomyocyte size, but there's data that points to at least a proportion of it being collagen ([Rocha et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17906098/); [Di Fazio et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12546148/)), which makes sense given that AAS elevate collagen synthesis rates. It also helps explain why increased cardiac mass usually leads to reduced function rather than increased function. It also helps explain why a reversal in cardiac hypertrophy after you stop using it doesn't fix impairments in cardiac function  ([Abdullah et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37992194/); [Fyksen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12323528/)).

Cardiac hypertrophy = more CVD: After cardiac hypertrophy (and related damage), AAS users have a 2-3x greater risk of cardiovascular morbidity/mortality than non-users ([Thiblin et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26005042/); [Horwitz et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30712973/)) and they tend not to seek help ([Henriksen et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38268076/)). In line with previous findings, AAS users tends to have more non-ischemic heart disease related to cardiac hypertrophy than ischemic heart disease (related to blocked coronary heart disease from atherosclerosis) ([Horwitz et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30712973/); [Di Fazio et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12546148/)). AAS dose has a dose/duration response relationship to CVD risk ([Thiblin et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26005042/)) in the same way it does for cardiac hypertrophy and functional impairment ([Smit et al. 2021a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580689/); [Abdullah et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37992194/); [Fernandes et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13528975/)).

### Pathological growth of kidney

Mechanistically, renal hypertrophy is way less studied than cardiac hypertrophy. But we do have a reasonable picture and what happens in the kidneys is very similar to what happens in the heart, at least when looking at animal studies. The kidney grows through both cellular size increases and fibrosis, and the only difference from the heart is that kidney hypertrophy can become atrophy if fibrosis gets too severe and destroys function.

The human evidence is thinner but points the same way. Ultrasound of bodybuilders found greater renal volume, cortical thickness and echogenicity in steroid users than in non-users ([Kantarci et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29148625/)), and biopsies from bodybuilders have shown focal segmental glomerulosclerosis after AAS use ([Herlitz et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2799287/)). Neither design can establish how often this happens or isolate AAS as the cause.

Renal hypertrophy happens fast: Animal studies show very rapid onset of kidney growth with meaningful mass gains happening in just 4 weeks ([Mills & Bardin, 1980](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7358032/), [Hoseini et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18938085/); [Bento-Silva et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1590/s1984-82502010000100009); [Brasil et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26165753/); [Tofighi et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29097219/); [De Lima et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881226/)). Note that 4 weeks in rodent studies is way longer in humans, so we'd expect meaningful changes in around 1-2 years in AAS users. So probably not as fast as cardiac hypertrophy.

Renal hypertrophy is linked to altered function: The early hypertrophy seen in 4 weeks generally doesn't mess with kidney function. Kidney dysfunction usually takes around 8 weeks ([Brasil et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26165753/); [Tsitsimpikou et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27346656/); [Tofighi et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29097219/); [De Lima et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881226/); Odoom et al. 2024; [Alqahtani et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41172450/)).

Duration-dependence of renal hypertrophy: The renal hypertrophy and fibrosis develop pretty linearly with time, but the developing dysfunction is more non-linear ([Brasil et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26165753/); [De Lima et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881226/)). Sometimes, the hypertrophy can turn to atrophy ([Tsitsimpikou et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27346656/); [Alqahtani et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41172450/)), likely due to damage leading to apoptosis ([Riezzo et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25065671/)) and to disrupted redox homeostasis in the kidney ([Frankenfeld et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4165578/)).

Reversibility of renal hypertrophy: Mechanistically, renal hypertrophy should be largely reversible due to the role of androgen receptors in producing the effect ([Shen et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25701874/)). The exception would be the fibrosis that can't be removed after. Some data does show difficulty in reversing kidney hypertrophy even after short durations ([Kahal & Allem, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30119263/)).

Relationship with fibrosis: In line with the non-linear time-course of renal dysfunction, fibrosis doesn't appear extensively in the early phases of kidney hypertrophy ([Hoseini et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18938085/); [Brasil et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26165753/); [De Lima et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881226/)) but grows fast after ([Tsitsimpikou et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27346656/); [Alqahtani et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41172450/)).

### Medications

There are medications that can probably help with the fibrosis that happens after cardiac and renal hypertrophy (but there's likely nothing that can stop androgen receptor-driven hypertrophy of either organ without stopping using AAS). They are common medications given to people with CVD and chronic kidney disease (CKD). 

These medications do have very dangerous interaction effects with NSAIDs and diuretics, though.

There are 3 groups of medications that can help with the fibrosis, though they are prescription only.

ACE inhibitors: (Enalapril, lisinopril) block the production of angiotensin II. Angiotensin II exerts its effects directly through an AT1 receptor and by upregulating aldosterone. Angiotensin II acting on AT1 and aldosterone are each definitely pro-fibrotic. ACE inhibitors are the more general compound that act on two pathways partially. ARBs and MRAs each target one of the two pathways more aggressively.

ARBs: These block the AT1 receptor that angiotensin II acts upon.

MRAs: (Spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone) block the aldosterone receptor. MRAs are sometimes stacked with ACEs or ARBs in heart failure patients because aldosterone can be activated by other pathways independently of angiotensin II.

### Red Blood Cells

AAS cause pathological growth of red blood cells. They act on the androgen receptors to stimulate the formation of red blood cells (erythrocytes) ([Shahani et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19494706/)). This leads to an AAS user showing higher than normal concentration of red blood cells, which can be detected as either hematocrit or hemoglobin. The downside of this condition is that the blood becomes thicker and isn't as able to travel through the blood vessels which has negative health consequences.

Erythrocytes, kidney cells, and the liver all have androgen receptors ([Schooley, 1966](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5980545/); [Medlinsky et al. 1969](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5789576/); [Mulder et al. 1972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4259003/); [Paulo et al. 1974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4809096/); [Valladares & Minguell, 1975](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/163042/); [McManus et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32311143/)). Testosterone or DHT arriving signals kidney androgen receptors to increase the rate of erythropoietin (EPO) synthesis and secretion ([Paulo et al. 1974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4809096/)). EPO is the hormone that stimulates bone marrow to make red blood cells. Androgens also signal the liver to reduce output of hepcidin. Hepcidin is a hormone that controls systemic iron levels through its interactions with ferroportin ([Bachman et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3050108/); Guo et al. [2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3602280/); [2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31001931/); [McManus et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32311143/)). Iron is a key component of red blood cells, while ferroportin carries iron from the digestive system and also scavenges it from macrophages that are degrading old and damaged red blood cells. When hepcidin binds to ferroportin, it impedes both iron absorption from the diet and iron release from storage into the bloodstream. Bone marrow itself mainly doesn't respond directly to testosterone/DHT signaling but instead responds to the elevated EPO from the kidney ([McManus et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32311143/)). The end result of this sequence of events is an elevation in red blood cell count among AAS users (including TRT users) that behaves dose-responsively ([Coviello et al 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2266950/)), which is exactly what we'd expect from an androgen-receptor driven process. This condition is called erythrocytosis or polycythemia ([Keohane et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24246666/); [Ohlander et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5690890/); [Ory et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9881804/)).

The primary risks associated with elevated red blood cell count are cardiovascular including deep vein thrombosis ([Braekkan et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2817030/)), arterial thrombosis ([Warny et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31309714/)), and transient ischemic attacks ([Krauss et al. 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1942342/)). The elevated risk is experienced even at TRT doses ([Ory et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9881804/)) but given the effect is driven by androgen receptors, we'd expect risk to scale proportionally with AAS dose. The most common treatment for individuals experiencing AAS-induced erythrocytosis (and those who won't reduce doses) is regular blood donation, though this isn't without risks ([Bond et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11466264/)). Monitoring of the critical measurements (hematocrit, hemoglobin, and red blood cell count) is the most obvious starting point and starts with understanding each.

Hematocrit: The percentage of blood volume that is red blood cells, which is usually between 38-50% in men and levels above the upper bound are usually investigated, though different organizations have different thresholds, with some choosing 50% and some 54% ([Bond et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11466264/)). This is the measurement that's most commonly used and has been connected to the health risks (by being measured in most observational studies).

Hemoglobin: The concentration of the oxygen-carrying protein in the blood.

Red blood cell count: The number of RBCs per volume of blood, which is the strict definition of the underlying problem (erythrocytosis) but very few people use this in  observational research into risks or in clinical practice due to the technical challenges which makes it less useful in practice ([Bond et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11466264/)).

### EPO and Hemoglobin set point
#### AAS affect the EPO/hemoglobin set point

Androgens are not the only thing that determine the rate of EPO synthesis and therefore RBC counts. To understand how EPO synthesis is governed, we need to see how the body makes it, and it comes down to the oxygen requirements of the body.

Oxygen delivery rate is determined by the amount of hemoglobin in the blood, the saturation of that hemoglobin with oxygen, and how quickly the blood flows around the body (which is determined by cardiac output). Increasing the number of RBCs is the primary adaptation for increasing oxygen delivery rate. When renal peritubular fibroblasts in the kidney experience hypoxia because of low hemoglobin in the blood, the signal for EPO synthesis ([Haase, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23334390/)). The EPO then travels to the bone marrow and tells it to start building new RBCs. When renal peritubular fibroblasts in the kidney experience normoxia again, they shut off EPO synthesis. This creates both positive and negative feedback that keeps RBC count within a certain limited range for a given habitual level of physical activity. This limited range for the RBC count has been termed "set point" for EPO/hemoglobin ([Bachman et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4022090/)).

Yet, androgens stimulate androgen receptors to produce an increase in EPO synthesis (and therefore in RBC count) that is unrelated to this system that is driven by positive feedback (from hypoxia) and negative feedback (from normoxia). This increases the volume of RBCs in circulation as long as the androgens are present. Once the androgens leave, the RBC count retraces back to the original set point that was determined by renal hypoxia (for whatever habitual physical activity level is maintained). This explains why hematocrit/hemoglobin levels are elevated on-cycle and often retrace gradually back to physiological levels off-cycle.

People have suggested that this implies more frequent AAS dosing could help keep hematocrit/hemoglobin levels under control on cycle. But weekly ([Coviello et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2266950/)) and daily ([Bachman et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4022090/)) dosing schedules both elevate hematocrit/hemoglobin levels. There is no evidence to point to a more frequent dosing schedule being helpful. And androgen receptor driven effects are almost exclusively dose-dependent and don't depend much on frequency of dosage application. For example, muscle growth with AAS use is androgen receptor-driven and doesn't depend much on the schedule of the doses. If it did, then pro bodybuilders would pin multiple times a day rather than once a week. The muscle growth response to AAS depends on the integrated area under the curve of the AAS dose across the week. And we'd expect RBC production to follow exactly like this.

### Androgen receptor markers

Androgen receptor markers are in blood panels if you know where to look.

Medical test practices don't include specific androgen receptor markers ([Gibbons et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10962511/)). The focus of both groups tends to be more upon the endocrine system rather than on the more important intracrine system. But everyone measure hematocrit, and this is a good marker for androgen receptor activity, especially after taking blood volume changes into account (such as can occur from water retention, diuretics, and blood donation). Also, when advanced AAS users take a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) panel, they're getting a good indicator of androgen receptor activity.

#### Practical implications

Bloodwork doesn't say everything: AAS-induced androgen receptor activation-driven cardiac and renal hypertrophy aren't picked up by any blood panel but this pathological hypertrophy can happen quickly and only grows more over time. Most users are getting no warning of this problem.

Protect your heart: You should invest in an echocardiogram yearly for signs of cardiac pathological hypertrophy progressing over time. An ECG isn't a substitute for it. Across six standard ECG criteria, about two thirds of AAS users with hypertrophy on echocardiography went undetected ([Abdullah et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12830151/)). Getting a cardiac MRI would provide more information, especially WRT fibrosis. but the cost is a lot higher. But if you plan on using AAS for a long time, it's worth it.

Consider anti-fibrotics with a doctor: IF you're using AAS, you should have a doctor you trust and can talk to about cardiac risks at heart. Ideally, one you can show your echocardiogram tests and you can discuss anti-fibrotics with them (like ACE inhibitors). But do bring up the side effects list for each pharmaceutical.

Protect your kidneys: AAS induced androgen receptor activation-driven renal hypertrophy and fibrosis leading to chronic kidney disease is not ideal. Ensure you measure blood pressure regularly at home and track on spreadsheet with weekly or daily numbers, ideally in the same spot, position, and state. If the numbers start staying high even off-cycle, that might be an early warning of kidney damage. And order a cheap urine test for PCR or ACR with your normal bloodwork. There are more advanced monitoring techniques but this is a good start.
