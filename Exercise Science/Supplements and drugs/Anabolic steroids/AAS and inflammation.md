> [!summary] Bottom line
> AAS stimulate the production of excessive inflammation that doesn't get picked up by most blood panels and causes negative sides on major organs, like the heart, liver, kidney, and brain. The specific failure is a phase mismatch, because AAS amplify the pro-inflammatory phases without raising the resolution phase that normally shuts inflammation down, so it persists. That is also why most anti-inflammatory drugs don't fit the problem: NSAIDs suppress the effector and resolution phases together, and corticosteroids are catabolic. Omega-3s are the safest option that acts on resolution.

### AAS cause inflammation

AAS have marked effects on inflammation that are dose responsive and duration-dependent. AAS users who use high doses for longer are going to be more strongly affected by inflammation induced sides. There are two separate mechanisms by which AAS provoke inflammatory responses.
[Riezzo 2014 - kidney cytokines, oxidative stress and apoptosis in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25065671/)
[Kahal and Allem 2018 - tissue histology after nandrolone and withdrawal in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30119263/)

Downstream effect of oxidative stress-induced damage: Studies show that AAS elevate ROS primarily by effects on mitochondria but also by provoking increased NADPH oxidase activity, and by upregulating cytochrome P450 monooxygenases. AAS also suppress the antioxidant defenses that normally keep ROS under control. In response to the AAS induced increases in oxidative stress, there are pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades. As with oxidative stress itself, these inflammatory processes have been linked to downstream apoptosis and downstream fibrosis and may be related to elevated SNS activity as indicated by increased blood pressure and heart rate.
[Frankenfeld 2014 - NADPH-dependent peroxide and antioxidant enzymes in rats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4165578/)
[Montalvo 2012 - androgen signaling and fibrosis in a mouse pressure-overload model](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3338422/)
[Dornelles 2017 - liver and kidney redox markers in rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27896593/)
[Pey 2003 - oxidative stress and antioxidant enzymes in rat liver](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14698208/)
[Riezzo 2014 - kidney cytokines, oxidative stress and apoptosis in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25065671/)
[Souza 2023 - coronary fat attenuation and circulating cytokines in human AAS users](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37991119/)
[Severo 2013 (online 2012) - inflammatory markers in human AAS users](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22345686/)
[Hassan and Kamal 2013 - cardiac apoptosis and hemodynamics in rats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3612416/)

Direct signaling to immune cells: Recent data has shown that AAS also provoke an inflammatory response by signaling to immune cells even before any tissue damage happens. AAS act through androgen receptor mediated signaling pathways to influence monocytes, which upregulated pro-inflammatory activity in affected tissues before any oxidative stress-induced damage take. The mechanism is fast-acting and sensitive. It occurs at low (even TRT) dosages. This contrasts with the oxidative stress mechanism, which requires a much greater dose to produce an effect. It explains why inflammatory mediators like hs-CRP are more sensitive than might expect when using the older model that treats inflammation as only a downstream effect of oxidative stress induced damaged. It also explains why common immune markers like peripheral blood mononuclear cells rise proportionally with testosterone levels over time.
[Sellau 2020 - testosterone and monocyte signaling in an infection model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32651360/)
[Severo 2013 (online 2012) - inflammatory markers in human AAS users](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22345686/)

### Organs affected

Heart: Direct inflammation markers have been recorded in the heart myocardium. This inflammation appears to be at least partly from oxidative stress, since it can be rescued by antioxidant support. Subsequent to this inflammatory response, signs of cardiac damage and fibrosis have been reported, alongside impaired cardiac function. Inflammation has also been observed in the coronary arteries through elevated pericoronary fat attenuation.
[Amini 2025 - cardiac inflammation and oxidative injury in rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39894191/)
[Montalvo 2012 - androgen signaling and fibrosis in a mouse pressure-overload model](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3338422/)
[Hassan 2023 - cardiac and skeletal muscle pathology in rats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10015925/)
[Hussain 2024 - cardiac oxidative stress and injury in rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529199/)
[Ghowsi 2023 - collagen deposition in rat hearts](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37953582/)
[Di Fazio 2025 - review of cardiovascular autopsy findings](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41143179/)
[Kahal and Allem 2018 - tissue histology after nandrolone and withdrawal in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30119263/)
[Fyksen 2025 - 16-year follow-up of cardiac structure and function in AAS users](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12323528/)
[Souza 2023 - coronary fat attenuation and circulating cytokines in human AAS users](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37991119/)

Brain: Though androgens are regarded as anti-inflammatory in the brain at physiological levels, inflammation markers have been found in the brain after AAS use. Also, they've been linked with alterations in behavior, including depressive symptoms, and they seem to be reversed when the inflammatory actions of the AAS in the brain are inhibited. Also, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is elevated in rodent pre-frontal cortices after AAS use alongside reduced brain volume. GFAP is an indicator that is commonly used to monitor traumatic brain injury, concussion, and dementia, and indicates neurodegeneration that is likely due to neuroinflammation. It's also worth noting some AAS are more damaging to the brain than others, which implies that care over the steroid chosen could be important here.
[Camiletti-Moiron 2015 - brain redox and inflammatory markers in rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26252547/)
[Abed and Al-Azawi 2020 - brain oxidative stress in rabbits](https://plantarchives.org/SPL%20ISSUE%2020-2/488__2940-2947_.pdf)
[El-Shamarka 2020 - nandrolone and cannabis neurotoxicity in adolescent rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31704101/)
[Souza 2020 - kynurenine pathway and depressive-like behavior in mice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.steroids.2020.108727)
[Tabor 2020 - prefrontal GFAP expression in an adolescent rat brain-injury study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7288073/)
[Zelleroth 2021 - neurite growth and viability in rat cortical cell cultures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33677017/)
[Sessa 2020 - postmortem brain microRNA markers in human AAS users](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7467388/)

Kidney: Inflammation after oxidative stress has been found in animal models in the kidney. It increases dose-responsively with AAS use and leads to damage and fibrosis, glomerulosclerosis and necrosis in addition to functional decline. These structural and functional issues seem reversible as long as AAS use is very short but aren't when used longer.
[Riezzo 2014 - kidney cytokines, oxidative stress and apoptosis in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25065671/)
[De Lima 2020 - oxidative stress, collagen deposition and kidney function in female rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881226/)
[Kahal and Allem 2018 - tissue histology after nandrolone and withdrawal in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30119263/)
[Memudu and Dongo 2023 - rat kidney function and oxidative tissue injury](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9996376/)

Liver: inflammation has rarely been reported in the liver, likely due to its strong regenerative capacity. Even then, recent studies have found an increased inflammatory response subsequent to AAS use. Similarly, recent data have also found liver fibrosis after AAS use that is likely to follow inflammation although oxidative stress is evidently the primary upstream determinant.
[Ye 2026 - inflammatory findings in metandienone-related liver injury](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42286859/)
[Mohammadpour-Asl 2026 (online 2025) - liver injury and fibrotic signaling in rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41242401/)

Testes: Studies in animal models have found inflammatory markers in the testes after AAS use that is most likely downstream of oxidative stress and human autopsy studies have found downstream fibrosis in the interstital compartment in which the Leydig cells are located. This points to long-term impairments from oxidative damage would likely be limited to testosterone production rather than spermatogenesis.
[Bueno 2017 (online 2016) - inflammatory and redox markers in rat testes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28166954/)
[Hassan 2026 - testicular cytokines in boldenone-exposed rats](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-55900-3)
[Darke 2014 - testicular fibrosis in an AAS-positive human autopsy series](https://doi.org/10.1111/1556-4029.12424)

### Do anti-inflammatory meds help?

No. Inflammation goes through 4 phases, initiation, amplification, main effector work, and resolution. The resolution phase isn't just the inflammatory response going away, it's actively managed by an anti-inflammatory response. AAS increase the activity of the pro-inflammatory phases but fail to increase the activity of the corresponding resolution phase. That leads to a mismatch between four phases that leads to persistent inflammation in different tissues. The perfect anti-inflammatory medication would target one of the earlier 3 phases while leaving the 4th in place or even elevate the 4th phase while leaving the other phases the same.
[Sellau 2020 - testosterone and monocyte signaling in an infection model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32651360/)

Most commonly used anti-inflammatory medications do none of this. NSAIDs like ibuprofen and COX-2 selective inhibitors suppress phases 3 and 4 together which would at best lead to a neutral effect before we consider the inherent side effects of the medication themselves. Corticosteroids do target the earlier three phases quite well, but they have catabolic effects that counteract the very thing AAS are trying to achieve.

As we saw for antioxidants, there are some medications that do either uniquely suppress the amplification phase of the pro-inflammatory response (colchicine, ACE inhibitors, MRAs, and ARBs) or provide some support to the anti-inflammatory resolution phase (SPMs and probably also omega 3 supplements). ACE inhibitors, MRAs, and ARBs are prescription-only though and can have very dangerous interaction effects with NSAIDs and diuretics, especially in those with kidney problems (which most long-term AAS users will have). It is useful to be aware of them, though.

Colchicine: At very low doses, colchicine has been suggested as a potential anti-inflammatory that might limit the excessive pro-inflammatory response produced by AAS, because it does target the machinery that AAS elevates. However, it has toxic effects at higher doses that could easily add on top with AAS and muscle-damaging exercise to create liver problem, so may not be the best idea for bodybuilders.

Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonist (MRAs), Angiotensin Receptor Blocker (ARBs): These are related groups of medications that target the amplification phase of the pro-inflammatory response produced by AAS and produce anti-fibrotic effects. They also have effects on androgen receptors.

Specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs): These are derived from PUFAs like omega-3s, they elevate the anti-inflammatory phase that normally comes after the pro-inflammatory phase in most tissues. Omega 3 supplementation is likely the safest choice here.

### Inflammation testing

#### Inflammation is weakly present in blood panels

Medical testing practices don't often include inflammation markers. Though advanced AAS users include a variety of tests (hs-CRP, full blood count with neutrophil to lymphocyte and platelet to lymphocyte ratios, and ferritin). We might add fibrinogen (providing similar info to CRP but with greater emphasis on thrombogenicity which is important), and myeloperoxidase (MPO) to the extent it can be obtained (MPO is the oxidative stress marker created by neutrophils).
[Gibbons 2024 - recommended primary-care blood tests](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10962511/)

#### Practical implications

Protect your mitochondria: Most inflammation from AAS is from the oxidative stress from damaged mitochondria. So ensure daily cardio and good sleep.

Don't just take anti-inflammatories: Most anti-inflammatory meds are pretty useless, the only one that really makes sense is an omega 3 supplement at least without talking to a doctor.

Don't rely on blood panels: AAS-induced inflammation isn't picked up well in bloodwork, so you're getting no warning of inflammation-induced damage to important organs.

Protect your heart: AAS-induced inflammation damages the heart but none of the damage is picked up in bloodwork. You should regularly monitor your heart like a yearly echocardiogram to see pathological progression over time and you can add a cardiac MRI every few years to detect fibrosis.

Protect your brain: AAS-induced inflammation damages the brain and you can't tell from bloodwork and there are no ways of monitoring it. You can expand your blood panel to include blood markers often used for tracking recovery after traumatic brain injury or concussion and get some useful longitudinal data if done yearly.

Protect your kidneys: AAS-induced inflammation damages the kidneys and doesn't get picked up on bloodwork. Make sure you regularly check blood pressure and track on a spreadsheet daily or weekly. (Ideally in the same location, position, and state). If numbers go up too high (especially off-cycle), that might be an early sign of kidney damage. Order a cheap urine test for either protein to creatinine ratio or albumin to creatinine ratio. along with regular blood work. These pick up early signs of glomerular permeability which is downstream damage by oxidative stress to kidney.

Protect your testicles: AAS-induced inflammation damages the testes, causing fibrosis. The oxidative stress and inflammation produces fibrosis in the interstitial compartment is quite bad as it causes irreversible damage. This doesn't really impact fertility as it's governed by a separate part of the testes, it will impede normal endogenous testosterone production from returning to baseline levels.

All in all, AAS produce a strong pro-inflammatory response that can damage various organs, like the kidney, liver, heart, and brain. This is mainly through the inflammatory response to excessive oxidative stress and the suppression of antioxidant defenses. Unlike well-known changes to endocrine function, this isn't detected in bloodwork except maybe in the liver. Anti-inflammatory meds are mostly useless, but there are some exceptions like omega 3 supplementation. Supporting the mitochondria directly is likely helpful and can be done with cardio and good sleep and proper supplementation.

### Mitigating inflammation

There are several compounds that may help mitigate these effects though it's important to distinguish suppressing inflammation and helping the body regulate and resolve it because inflammation is still an important body process for tissue repair and training adaptations.

#### Downstream effects of oxidative stress-induced damage

AAS increase oxidative stress by damaging mitochondria, increasing ROS production and suppressing our natural antioxidant defenses. This then activates inflammatory signaling pathways and increases production of inflammatory cytokines and leads to apoptosis and fibrosis.

So doing the oxidative stress protocols would help reduce inflammation through that.

#### Direct signaling to immune cells

AAS can directly influence immune cells through androgen receptor-mediated signaling. Sellau et al. 2020 has shown androgen signaling increased the production of inflammatory mediators which can potentially amplify the inflammatory response.
[Sellau 2020 - androgen signaling and monocyte responses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32651360/)

PEA (palmitoylethanolamide): This is a naturally occurring lipid involved in regulating inflammation, especially through PPAR-a signaling and modulation of mast cells and microglia, shown to improve inflammatory conditions and reduce inflammatory biomarkers. While conventional anti-inflammatories like NSAIDs can interfere with training adaptations, PEA has not been demonstrated to do that.
[Bortoletto 2025 - systematic review of PEA trials in patient populations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11745966/)
[Huschtscha 2024 - PEA and resistance-training adaptations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38844675/)

#### Supporting inflammation resolution

Excessive inflammation can happen when pro-inflammatory activity isn't matched with a proper resolution response

Omega 3 fatty acids and/or SPMs: SPMs are signaling molecules involved in the resolution of inflammation, including resolvins, protectins, and maresins, with many produced from omega 3 fatty acids. Omega 3 supplementation has been shown to increase lipid mediators associated with inflammation resolution, while SPM-enriched marine oil supplementation has been shown to increase circulating SPM concentrations, improve immune-cell phagocytic activity and reduce markers of immune-cell activation.
[Oakes 2024 - marine omega-3 and circulating lipid mediators](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518540/)
[Souza 2020 - enriched marine oil, circulating SPMs and immune-cell responses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31829100/)
[Gracia Aznar 2024 - SPM-enriched marine oil in post-COVID syndrome](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9059/12/10/2221)

#### Basic Protocol Example

1. PEA (micronised or ultra micronised) — 300–600mg daily

2. Omega-3 fish oil — 2g combined EPA/DHA daily
