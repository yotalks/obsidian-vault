> [!summary] Bottom line
> Strength coaches often call workouts "stressors", but stress researchers define a stressor as an adverse experience the brain perceives as unpredictable and uncontrollable. A planned, voluntary workout is neither. This matters because stressors add up toward illness, so if exercise were one, you'd avoid it in stressful times. Instead, exercise tends to relieve stress.

## Two meanings of "stressor"

Strength and conditioning professionals use "stressor" to mean any disturbance to homeostasis. A workout raises sympathetic nervous system activity, lowers parasympathetic activity and triggers fatigue mechanisms, so by that definition it's a stressor.

Stress researchers use a narrower definition: an adverse experience that the brain perceives as both unpredictable and uncontrollable, with a physical reaction to that perception. A planned, voluntary workout doesn't qualify. The physiological "stress" response to rewarding experiences can be as large as to negative ones, and during exercise it reflects the body's metabolic demands. So stress should be restricted to demands that exceed the body's ability to regulate itself, especially ones that are unpredictable or uncontrollable.
[Koolhaas 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21316391/)

## Why the difference matters

Stressors can be analyzed with the general adaptation syndrome, because they're interchangeable and cumulative.
[Selye 1950](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2038328/)

Everyday stressors trigger a "fight or flight" response that looks superficially like exercise's effects on heart rate and metabolism, but the brain interprets the two differently. Several stressors at once add up like one larger stressor, and if the total stays above a threshold for too long, health problems follow.

If exercise were a stressor, we'd be wise to avoid it during stressful times so it didn't add to the total. But many people find exercise relieves stress and improves health. Exercise acts on the brain's reward and motivation circuits, improving mood and reducing psychological distress, and it's a core part of effective stress reduction.
[Esch 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3282525/)

So calling exercise a stressor isn't just wrong but counterproductive: there's no reason to skip workouts during stressful periods for fear of adding to your stress load.
