> [!summary] Bottom line
> The stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) effect is the boost in concentric strength, speed and power when an eccentric phase comes right before it. It's a measurable outcome, not a single physiological process, and five mechanisms contribute: the stretch reflex, pre-activation, residual force enhancement in titin, elastic energy stored in tendons, and muscle-tendon interactions that let fibers shorten more slowly.

## What it is

The SSC effect is the observation that concentric strength, speed and power output are higher when an eccentric phase is done immediately before. Like strength gains over a program or fatigue during and after a workout, it's a measurable outcome rather than a physiological process, and it has several underlying mechanisms.

## Five mechanisms

### 1. Stretch reflex

When muscle spindles stretch quickly during an eccentric contraction, they send a signal up Ia afferent nerves to the spinal cord, which sends a signal back down the motor nerves to raise motor unit recruitment.

When the tibialis anterior was stretched 2° during contractions, the reflex muscle response had three peaks (at about 59, 86 and 120 ms). The reflex's mechanical contribution rose up to about 40% of maximal contraction, then declined to zero at 80%.
[Toft 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2924837/)

### 2. Pre-activation

Fibers that are inactive at the start of a concentric phase take time to fully activate, and force is suboptimal meanwhile. When the preceding eccentric phase has already activated them, force at the start of the concentric phase is much higher.

In 40 trained men, work in the first 300 ms of a concentric squat was 130 J with no preload, 148 J after an isometric preload and 155 J after a stretch-shortening cycle, with no difference in muscle activity. The boost came mostly from reaching a higher active state before the concentric phase started.
[Walshe 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9451623/)

### 3. Residual force enhancement

When titin is stretched inside active fibers during an eccentric contraction, it produces passive tension and stores elastic energy, then returns it in the following concentric phase → [[Stretch-mediated hypertrophy]].

After an active lengthening contraction, isometric force stays higher than in a purely isometric contraction at the same final length. In voluntary human contractions this residual force enhancement isn't purely mechanical but also involves more efficient activation.
[Seiberl 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25956547/)

### 4. Tendon elastic energy

Tendons stretched in an eccentric contraction produce passive tension and store elastic energy, then return it in the concentric phase → [[Tendon adaptation]].

In 24 people from walking to sprinting, shorter Achilles tendon moment arms (shorter heels) were linked to greater tendon stress and elastic energy storage.
[Foster 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8087768/)

### 5. Muscle-tendon interactions

When tendons lengthen in the eccentric phase and shorten in the concentric phase, the muscle itself lengthens and shortens less. So the fibers shorten more slowly for the same joint angular velocity and, by the force-velocity relationship, produce more force.

In 21 men, 12 weeks of ankle-only depth jumps stiffened the Achilles tendon and improved depth jump performance, with the tendon shortening faster and the gastrocnemius fascicles shortening more slowly during push-off.
[Hirayama 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263151/)
