> [!summary] Bottom line
> Coordination improves fast but is highly specific to the movement, velocity and contraction mode practiced, unlike gains in motor unit recruitment, which transfer broadly. Coordination improves most when you practice at task-like loads and speeds, without fatigue, with an external focus of attention, and with some autonomy. An external focus improves performance by raising coordination and lowering antagonist activation, but an internal focus on the muscle seems better for growing it.

## Adaptation traits

Improvements in coordination are an integral adaptation that contributes to strength and speed gains. Improvements in coordination show unique features that differ from many other adaptations, including other neural adaptations like increasing ability to recruit motor units.

Increases in coordination are very fast. They can happen after just one or two practice sessions. Compared to other strength gain mechanisms that are much slower.
[Green 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24552367/)

The strength gains from improvements in coordination are very specific to the movement pattern that is used for the practice session. But there can be transfers to other movements as long as the trained and untrained movements are super similar.
[McGuire 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4255822/)

Comparatively, there are no improvements in strength as a result of coordination increases when testing strength in a very different movement, like looking at difference of leg extension after a short time of training squats.
[[Ansdell 2020]]

In this respect, increases in coordination are completely different from increases in the ability to recruit motor units. Increases in voluntary activation are not even close to specific to the movement pattern or even joint used when training.
[[Ema 2018]]

Strength gains that come from improvements in coordination are specific to the velocity that is used when practicing. Strength gains are greater after fast movement practice when they're tested at fast speeds (rather than slow speeds) ,while strength gains are greater after slow movement practice when they're tested at slow speeds (compared to fast speeds).
[[Coburn 2006]]

Again increases in coordination are completely different than improvements in motor unit recruitment. Increases in voluntary activation   is no way near specific to movement speed that is used when practicing (although increases in motor unit firing rate is much more specific).
[[Behrens 2016]]

Improvements in coordination are also contraction mode specific used when practicing. The coordination of eccentric contractions improves much more after eccentric-only training compared to normal strength training.
[Mueller 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19543908/)

Again completely different to increases in ability to recruit motor units, eccentric overload training increases voluntary activation during isometric contractions, even though no isometric contractions were done when training.
[Walker 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27199764/)

Improvements in coordination seem to happen a lot slower after fast movement training involving low forces than slow movement  training with high forces. Why is unclear, but it tells us that greater time may be required practicing fast movements to get a certain level of coordination.
[Oliveira 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20706733/)

There are also signs that improvements in coordination happen more slowly after eccentric training than concentric or normal strength training. This is most likely because eccentric contractions involve greater force and activation variability at baseline before training, and they seem to be more difficult for the [[Glossary#CNS|CNS]] to control.
[Purtsi 2012](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/japa/20/3/article-p345.pdf)

Data shows that eccentric contractions create higher level of effort for same amount of force output, telling us that they involve a cognitive load associated with the difficulty of coordinating the movement pattern itself.
[[Kan 2019]]

## Training techniques

Increasing coordination is needed for improving in all sports. These improvements can be enhanced with different techniques 

Rapid improvements in strength (which are almost certainly from increases in coordination) are load specific. Increases in coordination that enhance high-velocity strength (as expressed during throwing, jumping, and sprinting) are greater after training with light loads (and max efforts). Improvements in coordination that increase low-velocity strength (as is expressed during powerlifting) are greater after training with heavy loads. So when we want to improve coordination, you should use loads similar to those needed during the task itself.
[[Coburn 2006]]

Increases in coordination are impaired by fatigue. This likely happens because fatigue alters the movement pattern of the activity practiced, so the optimal movement pattern is not practiced (and rather a suboptimal movement pattern is). So when fatigue occurs during  one training session, this impairs the ability to achieve metalbearing in the next training session, not just in the one in which fatigue occurred.
[Branscheidt 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30832766/)

Using an external focus of attention enhances performance during a workout, and increases gains in performance after training. Both of these immediate and long-term effects occur at least in part due to improvements in coordination. An external focus of attention has been shown to beneficially alter movement patterns during performance tests such as horizontal jumps.
[Ducharme 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26691415/)

And we have a lot of data showing that practicing a movement with an external focus of attention can greatly increase the rate of motor learning in physical tasks.
[Diekfuss 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29792580/)

### External vs internal focus

Attention can go toward the muscle, to create a mind-muscle connection (an internal focus), or toward the environment (an external focus). Looking at a spot on the ceiling and picturing the bar moving toward it during a bench press, or looking at a point on the floor several meters ahead during a horizontal jump, are external focuses. An external focus has three effects:
1. **Better coordination.** It improves take-off angle in horizontal jumps, a key determinant of how far we jump. In 21 people, long jumps with an external focus had a projection angle closer to the optimal 45° than with an internal focus. [Ducharme 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26691415/)
2. **Less antagonist activation.** Antagonists oppose the prime movers, like the biceps in the bench press as the elbow extends, so less antagonist activation raises the net turning force at the joint. In 11 men doing maximal elbow flexions, an external focus produced more force (311 vs 282 N) and less coactivation (21.5% vs 26.3%) than an internal focus. [Wiseman 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7016702/)
3. **Slightly less agonist activation,** because the brain's signal is spread across the body instead of sent to one muscle, which weakens the stimulus for both agonist activation gains and hypertrophy in that muscle.

Better coordination and less antagonist activation together improve performance whenever we use an external focus: we run faster, jump higher, lift heavier and do more reps to failure with a given weight. But that comes from better efficiency, not more agonist force. In 30 men doing 10RM squats and deadlifts, an external focus produced more force and more reps with less muscle activity than an internal focus. [Nadzalan 2020](https://doi.org/10.1088/1742-6596/1529/2/022008)

So an external focus isn't ideal for increasing agonist activation or hypertrophy. In 30 untrained men training for 8 weeks, focusing on contracting the target muscle grew the elbow flexors more than focusing on the lift's outcome (12.4% vs 6.9%), though quadriceps growth was similar.
[Schoenfeld 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29533715/)

### Autonomy

Improvements in motor skills are greater when autonomy is present during a task that is practiced, compared to when there is no autonomy. So while most training progratms are prescribed by coaches, this may not always be optimal for the athlete, if there is no place for the athlete to make choices, and therefore experience some autonomy.
[Wulf 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25589021/)
