> [!summary] Bottom line
> For team sports athletes, strength training should never leave fatigue that interferes with skill sessions, so keep it heavy, low volume, with reps in reserve and about twice a week. It should build the qualities that win matches, found through a needs analysis, like short sprints in soccer. It can also cut strain and tendon injuries, though maximal eccentrics cause a lot of fatigue and heavy training stiffens tendons, which may trade off against stretch-shortening performance.

## 1. Don't interfere with skill sessions

Strength workouts aren't the most important part of a team sports athlete's routine, so their post-workout fatigue should never interfere with skill sessions. That means minimizing post-workout fatigue with heavier loads, lower volumes and more reps in reserve, and not training too often, ideally twice a week.

In 11 highly trained men, bench presses with a heavier eccentric (120% of 1RM down, 80% up) still impaired bench throw power 48 hours later, while regular 80% loading didn't, so eccentrically loaded sessions need at least 48 hours of recovery.
[Bartolomei 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31531133/)

## 2. Build the qualities that win matches

Strength training should improve the physical qualities that matter for the sport, usually identified with a needs analysis. The most rigorous way is to study large datasets of matches to see what athletes do at critical moments. In soccer, this usually shows the importance of short-distance sprinting speed. Biomechanical analysis can then identify what physically contributes to that action, and strength training can be built around it → [[Sprint speed]].

## 3. Reduce strains and tendon injuries

Team sports have many muscle strains and tendon injuries. Maximal-effort eccentric-only training can reduce strains, and heavy strength training can reduce tendon injuries, but both have downsides → [[Muscle strain injuries]].

Across 4 trials, eccentric hamstring training didn't significantly reduce hamstring injuries overall (RR 0.59, 0.24–1.44), but in athletes who stuck with it, injuries fell 65% (RR 0.35, 0.23–0.55).
[Goode 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25227125/)

Maximal-effort eccentrics cause a lot of post-workout fatigue that can interfere with skill training, so they should be done for very few sets and reps.

Heavy strength training stiffens tendons, so they stretch less, which may reduce stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) function and hurt sprinting and jumping → [[Tendon adaptation]]. Whether an athlete accepts worse SSC function for better injury protection, or the reverse, is probably an individual decision.

Across 61 studies, mechanical loading increased lower limb tendon stiffness (SMD 0.74), mainly by making the tendon material stiffer rather than thicker. Resistance training increased tendon modulus more than other training, and protocols that strain the tendon more increased stiffness the most.
[Lazarczuk 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474511/)

In 21 men, 12 weeks of ankle-only depth jumps increased Achilles tendon stiffness without changing maximal strength, and depth jump performance improved, with the tendon shortening faster and the gastrocnemius fascicles shortening more slowly during push-off.
[Hirayama 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263151/)
