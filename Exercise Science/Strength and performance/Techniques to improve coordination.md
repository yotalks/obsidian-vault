> [!summary] Bottom line
> Coordination improves most when you practice at task-like loads and speeds, without fatigue, with an external focus of attention, and with some autonomy. An external focus improves performance by raising coordination and lowering antagonist activation, but an internal focus on the muscle seems better for growing it.

Increasing coordination is needed for improving in all sprots. These improvements can be enhanced with different techniques 

Rapid improvements in strength (which are almost certainly from increases in coordination) are load specific. Increases in coordination that enhance high-velocity strength (as expressed during throwing, jumping, and sprinting) are greater after training with light loads (and max efforts). Improvements in coordination that increase low-velocity strength (as is expressed during powerlifting) are greater after training with heavy loads. So when we want to improve coordination, you should use loads similar to those needed during the task itself.
[[Coburn 2006]]

Increases in coordination are impaired by fatigue. This likely happens because fatigue alters the movement pattern of the activity practiced, so the optimal movement pattern is not practiced (and rather a suboptimal movement pattern is). So when fatigue occurs during  one training session, this impairs the ability to achieve metalbearing in the next training session, not just in the one in which fatigue occurred.
[Branscheidt 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30832766/)

Using an external focus of attention enhances performance during a workout, and increases gains in performance after training. Both of these immediate and long-term effects occur at least in part due to improvements in coordination. An external focus of attention has been shown to beneficially alter movement patterns during performance tests such as horizontal jumps.
[Ducharme 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26691415/)

And we have a lot of data showing that practicing a movement with an external focus of attention can greatly increase the rate of motor learning in physical tasks.
[Diekfuss 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29792580/)

## External vs internal focus

Attention can go toward the muscle, to create a mind-muscle connection (an internal focus), or toward the environment (an external focus). Looking at a spot on the ceiling and picturing the bar moving toward it during a bench press, or looking at a point on the floor several meters ahead during a horizontal jump, are external focuses. An external focus has three effects:
1. **Better coordination.** It improves take-off angle in horizontal jumps, a key determinant of how far we jump. In 21 people, long jumps with an external focus had a projection angle closer to the optimal 45° than with an internal focus. [Ducharme 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26691415/)
2. **Less antagonist activation.** Antagonists oppose the prime movers, like the biceps in the bench press as the elbow extends, so less antagonist activation raises the net turning force at the joint. In 11 men doing maximal elbow flexions, an external focus produced more force (311 vs 282 N) and less coactivation (21.5% vs 26.3%) than an internal focus. [Wiseman 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7016702/)
3. **Slightly less agonist activation,** because the brain's signal is spread across the body instead of sent to one muscle, which weakens the stimulus for both agonist activation gains and hypertrophy in that muscle.

Better coordination and less antagonist activation together improve performance whenever we use an external focus: we run faster, jump higher, lift heavier and do more reps to failure with a given weight. But that comes from better efficiency, not more agonist force. In 30 men doing 10RM squats and deadlifts, an external focus produced more force and more reps with less muscle activity than an internal focus. [Nadzalan 2020](https://doi.org/10.1088/1742-6596/1529/2/022008)

So an external focus isn't ideal for increasing agonist activation or hypertrophy. In 30 untrained men training for 8 weeks, focusing on contracting the target muscle grew the elbow flexors more than focusing on the lift's outcome (12.4% vs 6.9%), though quadriceps growth was similar.
[Schoenfeld 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29533715/)

## Autonomy

Improvements in motor skills are greater when autonomy is present during a task that is practiced, compared to when there is no autonomy. So while most training progratms are prescribed by coaches, this may not always be optimal for the athlete, if there is no place for the athlete to make choices, and therefore experience some autonomy.
[Wulf 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25589021/)
