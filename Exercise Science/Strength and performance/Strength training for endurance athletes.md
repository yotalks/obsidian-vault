> [!summary] Bottom line
> For endurance athletes, strength training shouldn't leave fatigue that interferes with endurance sessions, so keep it heavy, low volume, with reps in reserve, concentric-only and about twice a week. Heavy and moderate loads stiffen tendons, which may reduce the elastic energy they store in the stretch-shortening cycle but also likely protects them from injury. The program should match the sport's needs, often favoring strength over size since extra bodyweight costs efficiency.

## 1. Don't interfere with endurance sessions

Strength workouts aren't the most important part of an endurance athlete's routine, so their post-workout fatigue should never interfere with endurance sessions. That means minimizing post-workout fatigue with heavier loads, lower volumes, more reps in reserve and concentric-only reps, and not training too often, ideally twice a week → [[Cardio and hypertrophy]].

## 2. Tendon stiffness and the stretch-shortening cycle

Strength training with heavy or moderate loads makes tendons stiffer, which reduces how much they can stretch and store elastic energy for the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC). The SSC saves energy, making athletes more efficient, so stiffer tendons may hurt endurance performance by shrinking the SSC effect → [[Running Economy]]. On the plus side, stiffer tendons very likely explain how strength training lowers tendon injury risk, since collagen tissues like tendons are damaged by stretching → [[Tendon adaptation]].
→ [[Stretch-shortening cycle]]

Across 61 studies, mechanical loading increased lower limb tendon stiffness (SMD 0.74), mainly by making the tendon material stiffer. Resistance training and higher-strain protocols increased it the most.
[Lazarczuk 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474511/)

In 21 men, 12 weeks of ankle-only depth jumps increased Achilles tendon stiffness and improved depth jump performance, with the tendon shortsening faster and the gastrocnemius fascicles shortening more slowly during push-off.
[Hirayama 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263151/)

## 3. Match the sport's needs

Some whole-body development probably helps every athlete, but some need more focus on certain areas. Every athlete also needs a different balance of strength and size. Many endurance athletes do better with a higher strength-to-size ratio, since extra bodyweight makes movement less efficient. So the best program comes from analyzing the specific endurance activity and what it needs → [[Strength training for team sports]].
