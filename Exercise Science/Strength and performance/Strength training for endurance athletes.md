> [!summary] Bottom line
> For endurance athletes, strength training shouldn't leave fatigue that interferes with endurance sessions, so keep it heavy, low volume, with reps in reserve, concentric-only and about twice a week. Heavy and moderate loads stiffen tendons, which may reduce the elastic energy they store in the stretch-shortening cycle but also likely protects them from injury. The program should match the sport's needs, often favoring strength over size since extra bodyweight costs efficiency. Strength training can improve running economy by raising vertical stiffness through eccentric strength, and through hypertrophy lengthening internal moment arms so less activation is needed.

## 1. Don't interfere with endurance sessions

Strength workouts aren't the most important part of an endurance athlete's routine, so their post-workout fatigue should never interfere with endurance sessions. That means minimizing post-workout fatigue with heavier loads, lower volumes, more reps in reserve and concentric-only reps, and not training too often, ideally twice a week → [[Cardio and hypertrophy]].

## 2. Tendon stiffness and the stretch-shortening cycle

Strength training with heavy or moderate loads makes tendons stiffer, which reduces how much they can stretch and store elastic energy for the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC). The SSC saves energy, making athletes more efficient, so stiffer tendons may hurt endurance performance by shrinking the SSC effect → [[#Running economy|Running Economy]]. On the plus side, stiffer tendons very likely explain how strength training lowers tendon injury risk, since collagen tissues like tendons are damaged by stretching → [[Tendon adaptation]].
→ [[Stretch-shortening cycle]]

Across 61 studies, mechanical loading increased lower limb tendon stiffness (SMD 0.74), mainly by making the tendon material stiffer. Resistance training and higher-strain protocols increased it the most.
[Lazarczuk 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474511/)

In 21 men, 12 weeks of ankle-only depth jumps increased Achilles tendon stiffness and improved depth jump performance, with the tendon shortsening faster and the gastrocnemius fascicles shortening more slowly during push-off.
[Hirayama 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5263151/)

## 3. Match the sport's needs

Some whole-body development probably helps every athlete, but some need more focus on certain areas. Every athlete also needs a different balance of strength and size. Many endurance athletes do better with a higher strength-to-size ratio, since extra bodyweight makes movement less efficient. So the best program comes from analyzing the specific endurance activity and what it needs → [[Strength training for team sports]].

## Running economy

Factors where strength training can improve running economy:

### Vertical stiffness

When running, you have vertical oscillation, moving up and down of center of mass, greater stiffness means less of this oscillation, making you more efficient and you're able waste less energy for every footfall.

We can improve this vertical stiffness by increasing our eccentric strength in the muscle fibers used in the running activity.

This can be done with strength training that has highish activation on the eccentric phase (very high recruitment not really necessary as not recruiting those fibers on submaximal running). So we can do heavy strength training to train for this.

### Greater internal moment arm

Hypertrophy of a muscle causes the muscle to bulge further from the bone, increasing the [[Internal moment arm|internal moment arm length]] to the joint center. This increased moment arm will give greater joint torque for a given muscle force which will increase the torque you'll be able to generate at lower levels of muscle activation which is beneficial because you're using less metabolic activity for same output. This will also provide benefit for cyclists.
