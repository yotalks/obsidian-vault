> [!summary] Bottom line
> A sticking point is where bar force falls below gravity until bar speed hits zero, driven by long external moment arms or shifting internal ones. Fix it with technique practice, angle-specific isometrics, and building the muscles involved. In the squat, sticking points come from weak quads, a weak adductor magnus, or technique that lengthens external moment arms with the thighs parallel to the ground. In the bench press, they come from weak pecs, anterior delts or triceps, or technique that lengthens external moment arms near the chest. In the deadlift, they come from weak hip extensors or erector spinae, or technique that lengthens the hip extension moment arm off the floor.

Powerlifting is a sport that involves lifting the heaviest load in the squat, bench, and deadlift. One challenge that powerlifters often face is a sticking point in one of these lifts. Sticking points are locations within the exercise ROM where bar speed drops to zero, with the result that the lift can't be completed. Bar speeds only drop to zero after a period of deceleration so this means that the location of a sticking point is at the end of a section on the exercise ROM where the applied force on the barbell was lower than the force exerted on the weight from gravity. Because of this, fixing the sticking point will involve increasing the powerlifter's ability to generate force on the barbell within the section prior to the sticking point. 

## Reasons for sticking regions

External moment arm lengths are the first reason for a sticking region, when the [[Glossary#EMAL|EMAL]] is longest, the joint moments required to produce sufficient force to lift the weight are largest, so the necessary muscle forces are normally also largest.
[Kompf 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5357260/)
[Lockie 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5968970/)
[Aidar 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162553/)

[[Internal moment arm|Internal moment arm lengths]] of the current prime mover are another reason as when the [[Glossary#IMAL|IMALs]] of current prime movers shorten, the muscle forces needed to generate the joint moments to lift the weight increase.

Changes in the relative IMALs of all the muscles at a joint are the last reason as when the IMAL of one muscle group reduces relative to another at the same joint, the contribution of the first muscle group decreases and the contribution of the second increases, this shifts the reliance  of the lift from one muscle to another and requires a shift on where the focus of muscular development to make progress.

## Training methods to fix sticking points

Improvements in technique to reduce EMAL in sticking region can be done by doing deliberate practice with feedback with the absence of fatigue and using an external focus of attention.
→ [[Techniques to improve coordination]]

Increasing joint angle-specific [[Glossary#MUR|MUR]] can also help and can be achieved with maximum effort isometrics at the start of the sticking region
[Garbisu-Hualde 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10203844/)
→ [[Increasing motor unit recruitment]]

Increases in overall MUR and an increase in muscle size and lateral force transmission can also help which can be achieved with any strategy that helps maximize MUR during exercise like motivational techniques, accessory lifts for all included muscles in the lift is also beneficial.
[Gene-Morales 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7675618/)
[Reya 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30908368/)

[Kompf 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4887540/)

## The squat

### Why squats stick

| Reason for sticking region | Location in the squat |
|---|---|
| External moment arm lengths (EMALs) | Longest when the top of the thigh is parallel to the ground, where both the knee and hip extension EMALs peak. |
| Internal moment arm lengths of the prime mover | The quadriceps' internal moment arm is longest between about 40° and 60° of knee flexion, so deeper in the squat the knee extensors make less torque for the same force. |
| Changes in the relative internal moment arms at a joint | As the hip flexes, the gluteus maximus' moment arm shrinks and the adductor magnus' grows, so the adductor magnus contributes relatively more where EMALs are longest. |

The patellar tendon's moment arm, which sets the quadriceps' leverage, peaked at 4–6 cm near 45° of knee flexion in cadaver knees.
[Krevolin 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15047009/)

The gluteus maximus' hip extension moment arm averaged 79 mm with the hip straight and shrank as the hip flexed, while the adductor magnus' (15 mm with the hip straight) grew up to about 75° of hip flexion → [[Gluteus maximus training]].
[Németh 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3988782/)

So squat sticking points come from too little quadriceps force (from small muscles or low motor unit recruitment), too little adductor magnus force (for the same reasons), or technique that lets EMALs get longer with the top of the thigh parallel to the ground.

### Fixing squat sticking points

| Adaptation                    | Training method                                                                                                      | Specific guidance                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Better technique              | Deliberate squat practice with feedback, without fatigue, and with an external focus of attention.                   | Some features of the squat shorten EMALs. A wider stance, for example, reduces the sagittal plane distance between the knee and hip joints and the line of gravity through the bar. |
| Higher motor unit recruitment | Maximal-effort isometrics for the key muscles, and in the squat at the joint angle where the sticking region starts. | Isometrics on knee extension and hip adduction machines raise muscle-specific recruitment, and an isometric squat raises joint angle-specific recruitment.                          |
| Bigger muscles                | Accessory hypertrophy exercises for the quadriceps and adductor magnus.                                              | Supplemental knee extensions for the quadriceps → [[Quadriceps training]], and side-lying straight-leg hip adductions for the adductor magnus.                                      |

### When you can't squat

Sometimes a lifter can't squat because of injury, pain or lack of equipment. Two strategies can maintain or even improve squat strength: accessory exercises for the muscles the squat uses, and isometric contractions in the squat position (where possible) to raise motor unit recruitment, especially at the joint angle of the hardest part of the range of motion.

The squat uses knee extension (the quadriceps) and hip extension (the two hip extensors that only cross the hip, the adductor magnus and gluteus maximus). The external moment arms at the knee and hip are longest with the upper thigh parallel to the ground. The quadriceps are the only knee extensors, so they're automatically the main contributor to knee extension. The adductor magnus has the best leverage with the hip flexed at that point, so it's the main contributor to hip extension in the squat.

In 15 young adults, the posterior and anterior-distal parts of the adductor magnus made up over 80% of its volume and had longer moment arms for hip extension than adduction, so the muscle's torque capacity was over twice as high for hip extension as for adduction.
[Takahashi 2025](https://europepmc.org/article/med/40139264)

| Muscle | Accessory exercises |
|---|---|
| Quadriceps | Knee extensions, leg press, machine pendulum squat or back squat if possible → [[Quadriceps training]] |
| Adductor magnus | Side-lying hip adduction, seated hip adduction (less effective) |
| Gluteus maximus | Hip thrust or glute bridge, horizontal back extension → [[Gluteus maximus training]] |

## The bench press

### Why bench presses stick

| Reason for sticking region | Location in the bench press |
|---|---|
| External moment arm lengths (EMALs) | Probably longest in the first half of the range of motion, closer to the chest. |
| Internal moment arm lengths of the prime mover | With a wide grip, the pectoralis major's moment arm is longest at the start and shrinks after. With a narrow grip, the clavicular pec's is longest from 30° of shoulder flexion on. In every pressing variation, the triceps' moment arm grows through the range of motion. |
| Changes in the relative internal moment arms at a joint | With a wide grip, the pec and anterior deltoid's moment arms change together. With a narrow grip, they have similar leverage at the start, then the pec's relative leverage grows toward lockout. |

In cadaver shoulders, the clavicular pec and anterior deltoid were among the most effective shoulder flexors, while the middle and lower pec acted more as a stabilizer and extensor → [[Pectoralis major training]].
[Ackland 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)

With the arm raised 90°, the pectoralis major had the largest moment arm for horizontal flexion, and the anterior deltoid a larger moment arm for elevation in the forward planes → [[Deltoid training]].
[Kuechle 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9356931/)

So bench press sticking points come from too little force from the pectoralis major, anterior deltoid or triceps (from small muscles or low motor unit recruitment), or technique that lets EMALs get longer early in the range of motion.

### Fixing bench press sticking points

| Adaptation | Training method | Specific guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Better technique | Deliberate bench press practice with feedback, without fatigue, and with an external focus of attention. | Reducing the horizontal distance between the line of gravity through the bar and the shoulder and elbow joints at the start would likely make the lift more efficient. |
| Higher motor unit recruitment | Maximal-effort isometrics for the key muscles, and in the bench press at the joint angle where the sticking region starts. | Isometrics on pec fly, front raise or overhead press, and pushdown machines raise muscle-specific recruitment, and an isometric bench press at the start of the sticking region raises joint angle-specific recruitment. |
| Bigger muscles | Accessory hypertrophy exercises for the pectoralis major, anterior deltoid and triceps. | Heavy pec flies for the pecs, front raises or overhead presses for the anterior delts, and dips or pushdowns for the triceps → [[Triceps training]]. |

## The deadlift

### Why deadlifts stick

| Reason for sticking region | Location in the deadlift |
|---|---|
| External moment arm lengths (EMALs) | Longest at the start, when the hip is furthest from the vertical line of gravity through the bar. |
| Internal moment arm lengths of the prime mover | The adductor magnus' moment arm is also longest at the start. The erector spinae's is longest in spinal flexion, which tends to encourage losing the back arch. |
| Changes in the relative internal moment arms at a joint | As the hip flexes, the gluteus maximus' moment arm shrinks and the adductor magnus' grows, so the adductor magnus contributes relatively more where EMALs are longest. |

So deadlift sticking points come from too little force from the adductor magnus, hamstrings, gluteus maximus or erector spinae (from small muscles or low motor unit recruitment), or technique that lets EMALs get longer early in the lift → [[Hamstring training]].

### Fixing deadlift sticking points

| Adaptation | Training method | Specific guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Better technique | Deliberate practice with feedback, without fatigue, and with an external focus of attention. | Some features of the deadlift reduce EMALs, especially the hip extension EMAL at the start of the lift. |
| Higher motor unit recruitment | Maximal-effort isometrics for the key muscles, and in the deadlift at the joint angle where the sticking region starts. | Isometrics on hip adduction, leg curl, hip thrust and low back extension machines raise muscle-specific recruitment, and an isometric rack pull at the start of the sticking region raises joint angle-specific recruitment. |
| Bigger muscles | Accessory hypertrophy exercises for the adductor magnus, hamstrings, gluteus maximus and erector spinae. | Side-lying straight-leg hip adductions for the adductor magnus, hip thrusts for the glutes, leg curls for the hamstrings and a low back extension machine for the low back. |

The low back responds well even to low training frequencies and isometrics:
- In 114 adults, one set of lumbar extensions to fatigue once every other week, once, twice or 3 times a week, or isometric training once a week, all increased lumbar extension strength similarly over 12 weeks. [Graves 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2144914/)
- In 56 people doing the same training for 12 and 20 weeks, strength rose much more with the spine extended than flexed (123% vs 17% at 20 weeks), with no difference between frequencies. [Carpenter 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1852797/)
- After the initial training, lumbar extension strength was maintained for 12 weeks with training as rarely as once every 4 weeks, while people who stopped lost 55%. [Tucci 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1471008/)

### Deadlift variations

Deadlifts vary in four ways: foot position (conventional or sumo), range of motion (deficit, from the floor, or rack or block pull), bent or straight legs (deadlift or stiff-legged deadlift), and whether there's a stretch-shortening cycle (deadlift or Romanian deadlift).

**Foot position.** A conventional stance, with feet about shoulder width, makes the hips travel further back from the bar, lengthening the bar's external moment arm on the hip, so the lift is more hip-dominant. A sumo stance, with the feet wider, keeps the hips closer to the bar, so it's less hip-dominant and more knee-dominant, more like a squat.

In 13 football players, sumo deadlifts produced more vastus medialis, vastus lateralis and tibialis anterior activity, and conventional deadlifts more medial gastrocnemius activity.
[Escamilla 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11932579/)

In national-level powerlifters, sumo lifters had more horizontal thighs, more upright trunks and a much wider stance, and conventional lifters did 25–40% more mechanical work. Knee and ankle moments differed between styles, but hip moments and moment arms didn't differ significantly.
[Escamilla 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10912892/)

**Range of motion.** A larger range of motion lowers the load that can be lifted and lengthens the bar's moment arm on the hip, so the hip muscles contribute relatively more. It also changes the hip angle where peak effort happens, which changes which hip muscle contributes most. Deficit deadlifts use the lightest loads and the most hip flexion, so they work the adductor magnus most. The hamstrings have their best leverage at about 45° of hip flexion, but a rack pull from that height would allow very heavy loads that make other muscles more limiting, so a lower rack or block pull is likely better for the hamstrings. Higher rack pulls are likely limited by the back rather than the hip extensors.

The adductor magnus' hip extension moment arm grew up to about 75° of hip flexion, while the hamstrings' grew up to about 35° and then shrank.
[Németh 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3988782/)

**Bent or straight legs.** With bent legs the knee has to extend, which triggers the two-joint muscle problem and switches the hamstrings off → [[Neuromechanical matching]]. So stiff-legged deadlifts always involve more hamstrings, and bent-leg deadlifts more adductor magnus.

Combining hip and knee extension cut semimembranosus activity to 42% of its level in hip extension alone.
[Yamashita 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3220066/)

**Stretch-shortening cycle.** A stretch-shortening cycle doesn't meaningfully change which muscles contribute or the hypertrophy stimulus, since it barely changes fiber tension or the number of activated fibers, but it does allow heavier loads.

So for the adductor magnus, a conventional, bent-leg deadlift from a highly flexed hip is ideal, and for the hamstrings, a conventional stiff-legged deadlift from a less flexed hip is better → [[Hamstring training]].

## The overhead press

### When you can't overhead press

Injury, pain or lack of equipment can stop a lifter from overhead pressing. The same two strategies apply as for the squat: accessory exercises for the muscles involved, and isometrics in the overhead press position (where possible) to raise motor unit recruitment, especially at the joint angle of the hardest part of the range of motion.

The overhead press can be done in the sagittal plane (shoulder flexion), the frontal plane (shoulder abduction) or the scapular plane (scapular-plane elevation), and all three use elbow extension by the triceps. In each, the external moment arm on the shoulder is longest with the upper arm parallel to the ground, so whichever muscle has the best leverage there will likely be the main shoulder mover. In the sagittal plane that's likely the clavicular pectoralis major, in the frontal plane the middle and anterior deltoids, and the scapular plane probably uses all three.

In cadaver shoulders, the clavicular pec and anterior deltoid were among the most effective shoulder flexors, and the middle and anterior deltoid the most effective abductors.
[Ackland 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)

A systematic review found the anterior and middle deltoid had the largest elevating moment arms of all shoulder muscles in frontal- and scapular-plane abduction and in flexion, with all three deltoid regions acting as substantial elevators throughout the range of motion.
[Hik 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6284439/)
→ [[Deltoid training]]

| Muscle | Accessory exercises |
|---|---|
| Clavicular pectoralis major | Narrow-grip bench press, stretched-position partial machine or dumbbell front raise |
| Anterior deltoid | Steep incline press, contracted-position partial machine or dumbbell front raise |
| Middle deltoid | Machine or dumbbell lateral raise |
| Triceps | Machine or free weight dips, overhead dumbbell triceps extension, standing cable pushdown, machine triceps extension |
