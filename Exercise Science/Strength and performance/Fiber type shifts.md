> [!summary] Bottom line
> Fiber type shifts are driven by how long calcium ions stay inside a fiber. Long, sustained activation, as in aerobic exercise, shifts fibers toward slower, more oxidative types such as IIX to IIA, and they shift back when that exercise stops. Strength training mainly causes shifts when sets go to or near failure, which is why athletes who need fast fibers often stop sets short.

## How muscles produce force

There are five steps:
1. The brain sends electrical signals down nerves to the muscles.
2. The signals travel along the outside of the muscle fibers (this is what EMG detects).
3. The electrical signals are converted into a chemical signal, calcium ions. Electrical signals can travel along the outside of fibers but not inside them, while calcium ions work as internal signals.
4. Calcium ions are detected by the myofibrils inside the fibers, which are made of actin and myosin.
5. When actin detects calcium ions, it attracts myosin, and the two bind into cross bridges that produce force.

## Calcium drives the shift

The same calcium ions also cause fiber type shifts. When fibers have calcium inside them for long periods, they become slower and more oxidative, shifting from type IIX to IIA for example. When they go without calcium for a while, they shift back to faster, more glycolytic types → [[Calcium ion-related fatigue]].

In engineered muscle stimulated for the same total active time, longer individual contractions (60 and 600 seconds) lowered fast IIX myosin and raised IIA, while shorter ones (0.6 and 6 seconds) didn't.
[Khodabukus 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25857846/)

In rats given identical electrical stimulation, training shifted fibers from type 2B to 2X regardless of load, so the shift followed electrical activity rather than mechanical load.
[Eftestøl 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27488660/)

Aerobic treadmill training in rats raised activity of calcineurin and NFATc1, a calcium-dependent signaling pathway, and shifted myosin isoforms in the soleus.
[Liu 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4234965/)

## Aerobic exercise vs strength training

How long calcium is present in a fiber is the key stimulus, which is why aerobic exercise shifts fibers toward slower, more oxidative types. In strength training, calcium builds up non-linearly, and only the last couple of reps of a set with moderate loads (8–12RM) involve enough calcium to cause shifts. So many athletes stop sets before failure to keep their fastest fibers for movements like throwing and sprinting → [[Increasing speed]].

In 22 young men doing 8 weeks of squats, stopping sets at 40% velocity loss (closer to failure) reduced the share of type IIX myosin, while stopping at 20% preserved it. The 20% group also improved jumping more (9.5% vs 3.5%), with similar strength gains.
[Pareja-Blanco 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27038416/)

After 10 weeks, both resistance training and endurance cycling raised the share of type IIA fibers and lowered type IIX, but only resistance training increased fiber size and rate of force development.
[Farup 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24530017/)

After 6 weeks of aerobic interval training in untrained women, satellite cells increased around hybrid fibers (expressing both type I and II myosin), suggesting they help remodel fibers even without hypertrophy.
[Joanisse 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3804745/)
