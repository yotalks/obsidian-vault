> [!summary] Bottom line
> If animals have a right not to be hunted, killing predators to protect prey can be justified, and claims that removing predators would collapse ecosystems (like the Yellowstone wolves story) are overstated.

## The argument

> [!note] My take
> I think humans have the right to not be exposed to situations where they're hunted and killed for food, and I think it's fine to use lethal force to save the lives of the hunted (serial killers for example), all else equal, I see animal rights as a logical extension of human rights, so I believe they have this right too.
> → [[Animal Rights]]

Say xenomorphs evolved to prey on humans in a natural predator-prey cycle by eating them alive, say you see the xenomorph and you know it will go on to kill more humans and you have the ability to kill it, I would be in favor of doing so.

To extend to reasons of self-defense, say it wasn't going to kill you specifically, but other humans that you don't know or care about necessarily.

## Ecological objections

The magnitude of population control that predators have on prey populations is contentious

The argument does not seem to empirically be substantiated for ecological destruction.

Even if it were, it's not clear why the same case can't be made towards humans.

To extend to ecological concerns, say shooting the xenomorph would lead to humans overpopulating and there's ecological harm because of that, I mean humans are the species that cause the most environmental harm, maybe it'll lead to less resources and humans will suffer more, do we let the xenomorph eat the human alive or do we shoot it?

Even if it couldn't, it wouldn't result in no predator killing, at best only predator killing up until some threshold such that there won't be a trophic collapse. Like let's not forget we are comparing to the perpetual torture system we see in nature.

Killing predators would not entail the ecosystem being destroyed, despite many people asserting it, it's just a faith based belief. Sure, things like overpopulation can be an issue, but that doesn't mean mass destruction.

The idea that predators control herbivore populations  to a substantial degree is just pseudoscience. Right now, with predators, the major things that control herbivore populations are disease, starvation, and the elements. 

With Yellowstone, the claims of negative effects on trophic cascade and herbivores are highly exaggerated to the point the original authors had to denounce them. The fact that people are still citing Yellowstone wolves as evidence of the ecological importance of predators is wild, very little of it was true.
