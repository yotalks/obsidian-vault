> In 2016 Medicare chose not to set a national policy on gender reassignment surgery because it judged the evidence inconclusive for the Medicare population. That isn't a ban: regional contractors decide coverage case by case. The memo is from 2016, and more robust data has been published since then.

## The decision

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) decided not to issue a national coverage determination on gender reassignment surgery for people with gender dysphoria, because the clinical evidence was inconclusive for the Medicare population. CMS stressed that this is not national non-coverage. Without a national policy, the regional Medicare Administrative Contractors (MACs) decide whether surgery is reasonable and necessary for each person based on their circumstances, and Medicare Advantage plans make the first call for their members.

Medicare had banned coverage of "transsexual surgery" since 1989, until a 2014 appeals board ruling struck that ban down. The 2016 analysis covered surgery only, not counseling or hormones.
[CMS 2016](https://www.cms.gov/medicare-coverage-database/details/nca-decision-memo.aspx?NCAId=282)

## The evidence CMS reviewed

Of 33 eligible studies, results were mixed and overall quality was low: most were observational with no comparison group, small, used subjective endpoints, and lost many patients to follow-up. It was also hard to separate the effect of surgery from therapy and hormones given before it.
- The four best-designed studies of quality of life before and after surgery found no clinically significant change.
- In Swedish national data, about 2–3% of patients requested reversal or a change of legal status.
- A Swedish cohort of people who had surgery found higher mortality (mostly from suicide) and 2.8 times the rate of psychiatric hospitalization compared with matched controls. But the study wasn't designed to test the effect of surgery itself, since it had no comparison group of people with gender dysphoria who didn't have surgery.
