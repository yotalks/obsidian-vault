> [!summary] Bottom line
> The strongest case for reparations isn't about skin color. It's that someone unjustly interrupted what would have been passed down, so the descendants are owed what they would otherwise have had. There are three main ways to reject this: inheritance gives no real claim, being brought to the US offset the harm, or the claim expired. The first and last come down to values. The second is partly empirical, and comparing Black American income with African GDP doesn't settle it.

## The inheritance argument

If the government unjustly bulldozed your parents' house, which you would have inherited, it would seem to owe you its value. That holds even if you did nothing to earn the house, and even if you're already wealthy. A tornado wouldn't create the same claim, because nobody did anything unjust. So the claim has two parts:

1. **Rightful ownership:** the property would have rightly passed to you.
2. **Unjust interruption:** someone wrongfully stopped that, leaving you worse off.

This is a claim on whoever did the interrupting, not a claim on the world. So it isn't based on an immutable trait. Inheritance runs through relationships and wills, not skin color, and genetics is only a default stand-in for those relationships. Applied to slavery, the claim belongs to descendants of enslaved people, not to everyone who shares their race.

It also turns on a counterfactual. If most descendants would have been better off, a few who wouldn't have been don't change the policy, since policy works on averages.

## Objection: inheritance gives no claim

Two people can live the same lives, and one dies poor while the other inherits a fortune. That seems unfair if you only look at those two.

The reply is that there's a third person, who worked for the money and wanted to pass it on. Stopping that wrongs the giver, not just the heir. Someone might say there's no reason to honor the wishes of the dead, since they'll never know. But people can value something being true, not just believing it's true, the same way someone wants a faithful spouse and not just the belief that they have one.

This doesn't have to be absolute. A threshold view can respect inheritance and still override it for a large enough harm, like leaving money to a terrorist, or for extreme inequality. Giving property only to one race would be gross, but this view says banning it is worse. Markets can be treated differently, because discrimination there may do much more harm.

## Objection: being brought to the US offset the harm

The argument is that descendants are better off in the US than they would have been in Africa, so there's no net harm. This has two parts: whether it's true, and whether it would cancel the debt.

### Whether it's true

The usual evidence is that Black Americans earn more than people in almost every African country. Median Black household income in 2021 was about $48,000, while the highest GDP per capita (PPP) in Africa, in the Seychelles, is roughly $30,000–$36,000 depending on the year and source.
[EPI 2022](https://www.epi.org/blog/the-labor-market-recovery-and-pandemic-relief-measures-lifted-black-and-brown-workers-and-families-in-2021/)

That comparison has several problems:

- **Different measures:** household or personal income isn't comparable to GDP per capita.
- **Different people:** not all Black Americans descend from people enslaved in the US. In a 2021 survey, 41% said their ancestors were enslaved in the US, 11% in both the US and elsewhere, and 5% only elsewhere. Another 34% weren't sure, and 8% said they weren't enslaved. US-born Black adults were more likely to say so (55%) than immigrants (21%). Family memory is also imperfect in both directions.
[Pew 2022](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-ethnicity/2022/04/14/black-americans-family-history-slavery-and-knowledge-of-black-history/)
- **Different counterfactual:** modern Africa isn't what Africa would have been without the slave trade. Countries that exported more enslaved people are poorer today, and this link holds up in instrumental variable tests. So the comparison is partly measuring the damage of the trade itself.
[Nunn 2008](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13367)
- **The right comparison:** the question is what the descendants would have had without the wrong, including wages for their ancestors' labor, and not only what Africans have now.

### Whether it would cancel the debt

If the government destroyed your house and shackled you, then moved you somewhere wealthier, that wouldn't obviously cancel what it owes you. Ending up better off isn't the same as being paid.

What a transfer is for matters. A gift to your landlord doesn't count against your rent. And if a general welfare program happened to give the same amount, it wouldn't pay the debt, because it wasn't paid as repayment. The other side replies that the move and the harm can't be separated, since Africans were only brought to the US because of slavery. So they say the benefit belongs in the calculation of whether any debt exists at all.

## Pain and suffering

Suffering doesn't pass down generations, but compensation can. A person wrongly harmed would have been owed money for the harm and for unpaid labor, and that money could have been inherited. So the claim is for money that should have been paid and passed down, not for inherited pain. The other view is that compensation for suffering was owed to the people who suffered, and it ended with them.

This changes the size of the debt. If only lost wealth counts, a descendant better off overall might be owed nothing. If unpaid wages and compensation count, they could be owed something either way.

## Objection: the claim expired

If descendants can claim from slavery, they could seem to claim from every old atrocity, like the Crusades. The reply is that limits exist even without an exact line. Claims weaken as generations pass and as the wrongdoer stops being the same entity, though whether those limits are principled or just practical is an open question. Slavery is recent by this standard, and the US government still exists.

So the claim against the government as a continuing entity differs from blaming people alive today. Any payment would still come from current taxpayers, and whether that's fair is a separate question.

## Who gets it

Reparations can mean several things. One prominent proposal limits eligibility to people who can trace their ancestry to people enslaved in the US, rather than going by race.
[Darity 2022](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jecper:v:36:y:2022:i:2:p:99-122)

## Values and facts

Much of this may come down to values. On an anti-realist view, "descendants ought to be paid" reports a preference, so two people can agree on the facts and still differ. Debate can then only show whether someone's own values lead to the conclusion. → [[Metaethics]]

Three separate intuitions can each reject reparations: that inheritance gives no real claim, that the move to the US counts against the debt, or that the claim has expired. Someone who holds none of them, and who thinks descendants are worse off on average, will likely think something is owed. The empirical question also doesn't settle who has the burden of proof.
