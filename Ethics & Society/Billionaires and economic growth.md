> [!summary] Bottom line
> Countries with more billionaires look better on almost every outcome, but the association disappears once GDP per capita is controlled for, because billionaire counts largely track how rich a country already is. Where billionaire wealth does predict growth, what matters is how it was acquired. Wealth held by politically connected billionaires is linked to slower growth, by about a third of the average growth rate per standard deviation, while politically unconnected wealth, income inequality and poverty show no significant effect. A separate literature finds the same split between inherited and self-made wealth. The direction of causation remains weakly identified throughout.

## Billionaire lists as a wealth measure

Theories about inequality are usually about the distribution of wealth, but almost all empirical work uses income instead, because wealth distribution data doesn't exist for enough countries. Forbes' annual billionaire list is one workaround: total the wealth of a country's billionaires and divide by GDP. The measure is positively correlated with the wealth Gini and with the share of wealth held by the top decile, whereas land inequality, the older proxy, correlates with neither.

Coverage grows over time. 23 countries appeared on the first list in 1987, and 41 by 2002.
[Bagchi 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jce.2015.04.002) · [working paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp7733.pdf)

## Politically connected versus unconnected wealth

Using four five-year panels from 1987 to 2007, with country fixed effects and lagged explanatory variables:

| Variable | Effect on growth |
|---|---|
| **Total billionaire wealth / GDP** | Negative, significant |
| **Politically connected wealth / GDP** | Negative, significant |
| **Politically unconnected wealth / GDP** | Not significant |
| **Income inequality (Gini)** | Insignificant or borderline |
| **Initial poverty (share under $2/day)** | Not significant in any specification |

A one standard deviation rise in total wealth inequality (3.72%) corresponds to 0.49 percentage points lower annual growth in real GDP per capita. Splitting by source, a one standard deviation rise in politically connected wealth, holding unconnected wealth constant, corresponds to a 0.60 point slowdown. Mean growth over the period was 1.9%, so that slowdown is nearly a third of the average growth rate.

So in a head-to-head comparison, it's wealth inequality rather than income inequality or poverty that predicts growth, and within wealth inequality it's the politically connected component doing the work.
[Bagchi 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jce.2015.04.002)

### How "politically connected" was coded

The classification is deliberately conservative, covering only individuals who clearly would not have become billionaires without political support. It separates altering the playing field for particular people from a generally pro-business environment: businessmen in Hong Kong or Singapore benefit from a pro-business administration and aren't counted, whereas Suharto-era Indonesia, where knowing the president determined who got a lucrative import licence, is. Russia had no billionaires until 1996, when the number jumped with the privatisation of state assets around Yeltsin's re-election. Politically connected billionaires hold between 4% and 13% of total billionaire wealth depending on the year.

The authors describe the categorisation as "somewhat subjective," in the same vein as perception-based corruption indices.

### Checks on the classification

- **Corruption indices:** politically connected wealth per GDP correlates strongly with ICRG corruption scores. Unconnected wealth doesn't, and if anything runs the other way, with less corrupt countries having more unconnected billionaire wealth.
- **Country rankings:** the five countries with the most politically connected wealth (Malaysia, Colombia, Indonesia, Thailand, Mexico) had a median Transparency International rank of 32 out of 41 in 1995 and 94 out of 174 in 2012. The six with billionaires every year but none politically connected (Hong Kong, Netherlands, Singapore, Sweden, Switzerland, UK) ranked 9 out of 41 and 8 out of 174.
- **Historical instrument:** land suitability for wheat versus sugarcane, an instrument for structural inequality built on colonial factor endowments, predicts politically connected wealth but not total or unconnected wealth.

[Bagchi 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jce.2015.04.002) · [Easterly 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2006.11.002)

## Inherited versus self-made wealth

An earlier literature splits billionaires a different way and finds a parallel result. Per capita GDP grows faster where self-made billionaire wealth is a larger fraction of GDP, and slower where inherited billionaire wealth is. The proposed mechanism is entrenchment: concentrated inherited control biases capital allocation, retards capital market development and obstructs entry by outside entrepreneurs.

In Canadian firm data, heir-controlled firms show lower industry-adjusted financial performance, labour-capital ratios and R&D spending than other firms of the same age and size.
[Morck 2000](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6814)

The summary of this view is that "economists need to think less about concentration of wealth per se and more about concentration of wealth in whose hands."
[Morck 2005](https://doi.org/10.1257/002205105774431252)

## The cross-country correlations

Billionaires per capita correlate with higher living standards, longer lives, lower crime, less corruption and higher life satisfaction. But billionaire counts track national wealth closely, correlating with GDP per capita at ρ = 0.70, with GDP per capita explaining roughly half the variance in billionaire numbers. Virtually every improved outcome goes null once GDP per capita is controlled for.

Beyond GDP, R&D spending and patenting predict billionaire numbers. In panel data the order runs one way: rises in R&D predict more billionaires later, while billionaire numbers don't predict later rises in R&D.

Using booms and busts in billionaire-heavy industries as an instrument for growth in billionaire wealth gives three results that hold up: more R&D spending, more researchers, and fewer new companies. Most other outcomes are directionally positive but neither statistically significant nor large. Splitting by market-made versus state-made wealth, market-made billionaires are associated with more trusting populations and state-made with less, state-made numbers correlate with lower life satisfaction while market-made numbers don't, and R&D predicts only market-made billionaires.
[Cremieux 2026](https://www.cremieux.xyz/p/more-billionaires-more-good-stuff)

## What isn't established

- **Direction of causation.** Cross-sectional correlations can't establish it. Panel estimates with lagged variables are closer but still not causal, and valid instruments are hard to find here. The two instruments in Bagchi 2015 pass the formal tests but have conceptual weaknesses.
- **Where the split lies.** Both the politically connected and market-made classifications rest on hand-coding judgements about individual billionaires rather than an objective criterion.
- **Whether the two splits are the same split.** Inherited-versus-self-made and connected-versus-unconnected are different cuts that happen to point the same way, and nothing establishes that they identify the same underlying mechanism.
