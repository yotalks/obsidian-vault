> [!summary] Bottom line
> New York City law requires Rikers Island to close by August 31, 2027, but the four borough jails replacing it won't all open until 2032 and hold about 4,100 people, against about 6,600 detained now. Mayor Mamdani's November 2026 plan pushes the deadline to the end of 2032 and closes the gap by cutting the population by about 2,500. About 1,200 of those beds would come from releasing people held pretrial into diversion, supervised release and alternative-to-incarceration programs, including about 685 lower-risk people charged with class B felonies like attempted murder and first-degree robbery, rape, burglary and assault. Bail reform in New York lowered re-arrest for misdemeanor and nonviolent felony charges, but over 2 years 44% were still re-arrested.

## Background

A 2019 city law requires the end of incarceration on Rikers Island by August 31, 2027, with the jail population moved to four smaller borough jails. During the 2025 mayoral race, Mamdani's campaign said he would work to keep to the 2027 deadline.
[Spectrum News 2025](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/all-boroughs/politics/2025/10/02/rikers-island-challenge-next-mayor)

On November 20, 2026, Mamdani released a plan moving the deadline to December 31, 2032, which needs City Council approval.
[AP 2026](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/mamdani-sets-plan-to-close-nycs-notorious-rikers-island-jail-by-2032)

## How the 2027 deadline became unworkable

The borough jails fell years behind schedule, mostly because construction stopped during the pandemic and never resumed at pace under Mayor Adams. The projected cost roughly doubled, from about $8 billion to about $16 billion. By late 2025 Rikers held about 7,100 detainees, against the 4,160 the four borough jails will hold. More than 100 people have died in city jails over the past decade, and in 2025 a federal judge moved toward handing elements of control over the jails to a third-party receiver.

Closing Rikers is the legal default. The 2019 law sets the deadline, so it binds unless the City Council amends it, whether or not replacement capacity exists. That inverts the usual shape of a contested closure: here it takes action to keep the jail open, not to shut it.

At the final mayoral debate on October 22, 2025, the candidates split three ways:

| Candidate | Position on the 2027 deadline |
| --------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Mamdani** | Keep it. He would "do everything in my power to try and meet that deadline," because Rikers "is a stain on the history of this city" |
| **Cuomo** | Unmeetable. Scrap the borough jails and rebuild on the island: "you can't, unless you intend to release all the people on Rikers Island" |
| **Sliwa** | Keep Rikers open, fight the closure law in court, and convert the borough jails into affordable housing |

The Independent Rikers Commission, which wrote the closure plan, held that the deadline is out of reach but that the city shouldn't change the law until the mayor and City Council produce a detailed plan to shutter the jails. Its chair, former Chief Judge Jonathan Lippman, said "Rikers must close. No delays. No detours."
[Queens Daily Eagle 2025](https://queenseagle.com/all/2025/10/24/mayoral-candidates-spar-over-future-of-rikers-island)

The jail population was 6,542 in late August 2026, down from a peak of 7,650 in July 2025. The plan aims for no more than 5,500 by the end of 2029 and no more than 4,100 by the end of 2032.
[Close Rikers Strategic Plan 2026](https://closerikers.nyc.gov/the-strategic-plan/)

## Who is detained

- **Pretrial:** about 85% (5,579 people in August 2026) haven't been convicted, and about 60% of them are held on bail they can't afford.
- **Charges:** of an average 5,520 people detained pretrial in January to June 2026, 4,070 were held on a violent felony charge, 1,240 on a nonviolent felony and 210 on a misdemeanor.
- **Murder charges:** over 840 people, detained for an average of 690 days.
- **Length of stay:** an average of 102 days in 2025, up from 63 in 2017, against 43 days nationally for large jurisdictions. 1,470 people had been held pretrial for over a year.
- **Mental illness:** 22% have a serious mental illness.

[Close Rikers Strategic Plan 2026](https://closerikers.nyc.gov/the-strategic-plan/)

## How the plan cuts the population

| Strategy           | Beds | Who it targets |
| ------------------ | ---- | -------------- |
| Diversion          | ~500 | About 1,000 lowest- and low-risk people charged with class C felonies or lower, mainly released at arraignment, including through supervised release |
| Targeted reduction | ~700 | 300 lowest-risk and 385 low-risk people charged with class B felonies, through alternative-to-incarceration programs, electronic monitoring and making bail easier to pay |
| Case delay         | ~800 | Speeding up cases, especially the longest pretrial detentions |
| Recidivism         | ~500 | Supportive housing, mental health and reentry services |

The plan's examples of class B felonies are "attempted murder and first-degree: robbery, rape, burglary, and assault." It sorts people by risk of failing to appear and pretrial re-arrest, not by whether the charge is violent.

Funding is $30 million right away and $260 million through fiscal year 2030 for these four strategies, separate from about $16 billion for the borough jails.
[Close Rikers Strategic Plan 2026](https://closerikers.nyc.gov/the-strategic-plan/)

## Release and re-arrest

Diversion and targeted reduction mean people charged with crimes, who would otherwise be held pretrial, go back to the community under supervision or in a program. The only way to avoid this with the planned beds would be more capacity, such as more or larger jails.

Most people at Rikers are legally innocent and awaiting trial, and long pretrial stays can cost them housing and jobs. On the other hand, people released pretrial are often re-arrested:

- **Misdemeanors and nonviolent felonies:** New York's 2020 bail reform eliminated bail for most of these charges, and it reduced recidivism. Over two years, 44% of people released under reform were re-arrested, versus 50% of similar people who would have faced bail, and 24% versus 27% for a felony re-arrest.
- **Violent felonies:** for people still eligible for bail, most of them charged with violent felonies, using less bail through measures like expanded supervised release didn't change recidivism either way.

[Ropac 2023](https://datacollaborativeforjustice.org/work/bail-reform/does-new-yorks-bail-reform-law-impact-recidivism-a-quasi-experimental-test-in-new-york-city/)

So releasing people into programs doesn't seem to raise re-arrest compared with holding them, but the absolute rate stays high. How much risk is acceptable depends on the charge, and the plan's targeted reduction group is charged with more serious felonies than most of the people bail reform released.
