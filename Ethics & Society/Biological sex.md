> [!summary] Bottom line
> Biologically, sex is defined by gamete size. In species with two gamete sizes, females are the phenotype that produces the larger gametes and males the phenotype that produces the smaller ones. There are only two sexes, but sex isn't binary in the scientific sense of a dichotomous variable, where each case takes exactly one of two values, because some organisms are both male and female. Hermaphrodites are common in plants and invertebrates like earthworms, and rare humans have produced both eggs and sperm.

## Defining male and female

[[Glossary#Anisogamy|Anisogamy]] is when an organism's gametes differ in size. In organisms with two sexes, these different-sized gametes define the sex: males have small, often flagellated sperm, and females have large eggs.
[Cotner 2011](https://books.google.com/books/about/Arguing_for_Evolution.html?id=p0_t5koK0XAC)

The biological definitions follow from that:
- **Female:** the adult phenotype that produces the larger gametes in anisogamous systems.
- **Male:** the adult phenotype that produces the smaller gametes in anisogamous systems.
- **Hermaphroditism** (in animals) or **monoecy** (in plants): systems where the male and female sexes exist in the same individual, so a single individual produces both small and large gametes.

So male and female only exist in species with anisogamy. Our ancestors were isogamous, producing only one gamete size, and asking how males and females evolved is the same as asking why gamete sizes diverged. The two main classes of models, gamete competition and gamete limitation, aren't alternatives but two aspects of one process. Once anisogamy evolved it was very stable, and the split in gamete size went on to drive many of the other differences between the sexes.
[Lehtonen 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25323972/)

## Is sex binary?

The argument that sex isn't binary is a modus tollens:
- **P1:** If male/female sex with respect to organisms is binary, then it's not the case that there can be organisms who are male and female (P→¬Q).
- **P2:** There can be organisms who are male and female (Q).
- **C:** Therefore, it's not the case that male/female sex with respect to organisms is binary (∴¬P).

### What binary means (P1)

In science, "binary" variables are dichotomous, and binary variables are usually coded as 0 or 1. A dichotomous variable takes one of only two possible values when observed or measured, so the disjunction is exclusive: each data point can only take one of the two values. Sex recorded as male or female is a dichotomous [[Levels of measurement|nominal variable]].
[SAGE: Dichotomous variables](https://methods.sagepub.com/reference/the-sage-encyclopedia-of-social-science-research-methods/n239.xml)
[SAGE: Logit and probit models](https://methods.sagepub.com/foundations/logit-and-probit-binary-and-multinomial-choice-models)

Examples of binary variables:
- the outcome of an experiment ("success" or "failure")
- the response to a yes-no question ("yes" or "no")
- presence or absence of some feature ("is present" or "is not present")
- the truth or falsehood of a proposition ("true" or "false")

None of these can take both values at the same time.

Golf balls are an analogy. If golf balls only come in red and yellow, there are only two colors, but a ball that's half red and half yellow means ball color isn't binary. Sex works the same way: there are only two gamete types, so two sexes, but an individual that makes both means sex isn't a dichotomous variable.

### Organisms that are both (P2)

A hermaphrodite is an individual that is both male and female. Simultaneous hermaphrodites are male and female at the same time, and sequential hermaphrodites are male first and then female, or the reverse. Salps, a group of ocean-drifting tunicates, are one example.
[Gershwin 2013](https://publications.csiro.au/rpr/download?pid=csiro:EP1312315&dsid=DS2)

A hermaphrodite organism is both male and female and has both types of sex organs. The earthworm is one: its male and female sex organs sit close together near the front of its body. Two earthworms pair up and exchange sperm, and each later sheds a cocoon that collects its eggs and the received sperm, where fertilization happens.
[BIOL 205 Lecture 29 (2010)](http://s3.amazonaws.com/docuum/attachments/4935/BIOL%20205%20Lecture%2029.pdf?1271436874)

Biologists studying these animals treat each individual as both sexes. Measuring sexual selection is less clear-cut in simultaneous hermaphrodites, because each individual is both male and female at the same time. In the pond snail *Lymnaea stagnalis*, an individual's mating success in the female role seemed to reduce its own reproductive success in the male role, an effect that only showed up several weeks into the experiment.
[Hoffer 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5511009/)

The same applies to sexual conflict. Separate-sexed animals can partly resolve conflicts between the sexes, but simultaneous hermaphrodites can't resolve them as easily, because each individual is both male and female at the same time. In the hermaphroditic flatworm genus *Macrostomum*, a complex sperm design with stiff bristles was associated with reciprocal mating and lost when sperm injection through the skin evolved.
[Schärer 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21220334/)

### Humans

In humans, true hermaphroditism (now called ovotesticular disorder of sex development) means having both ovarian and testicular tissue. Cases exist with both egg and sperm production:
- A 1973 case report described true hermaphroditism with both oogenesis and spermatogenesis.
[Shannon 1973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4532534/)
- A 32-year-old phenotypically male true hermaphrodite had fathered a child, and histology of his removed ovary suggested ovulation had occurred at some point. It was believed to be the first cytogenetically proved true hermaphrodite who was fertile as a male.
[Parvin 1982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7200380/)
