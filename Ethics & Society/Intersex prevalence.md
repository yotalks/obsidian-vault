> [!summary] Bottom line
> How common intersex conditions are depends on which conditions count. The often-quoted 1.7% includes late-onset [[Glossary#CAH|congenital adrenal hyperplasia]], which alone is about 1.5%. Without it, the total is about 0.2%, and a narrower definition limited to sex chromosomes that don't match the body, or bodies that can't be classed as male or female, gives about 0.018%. Even at the low end, rarity doesn't stop something from being a real category. Red hair in Germany is about as rare as the 0.2% figure, and heterochromia is rare too, yet ID forms list both.

## Estimates

A review of the medical literature from 1955 onward estimated that as many as 2% of live births deviate from the ideal male or female in sex chromosomes, gonads, hormone levels, internal genital ducts or external genitals. About 1 or 2 per 1,000 live births (0.1–0.2%) get "corrective" genital surgery.
[Blackless 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11534012/)

The Intersex Society of North America's frequency table, drawn from that review, gives late-onset adrenal hyperplasia as 1 in 66 individuals (about 1.5%). The other conditions are much rarer:
- Not XX and not XY: 1 in 1,666 births
- Klinefelter syndrome (XXY): 1 in 1,000 births
- Vaginal agenesis: 1 in 6,000 births
- Androgen insensitivity syndrome: 1 in 13,000 births
- Classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia: 1 in 13,000 births
- Ovotestes: 1 in 83,000 births
- Idiopathic (no discernible medical cause): 1 in 110,000 births
- Partial androgen insensitivity syndrome: 1 in 130,000 births
- Complete gonadal dysgenesis: 1 in 150,000 births
- Iatrogenic cases, 5-alpha reductase deficiency and mixed gonadal dysgenesis: no estimate
[ISNA: How common is intersex?](https://isna.org/faq/frequency)

Adding these up gives 1.71% in all, or about 0.20% without late-onset adrenal hyperplasia.
![[Intersex condition frequencies.png]]

The 1.71% figure has been criticized for including late-onset adrenal hyperplasia, Klinefelter syndrome and Turner syndrome, which most clinicians don't consider intersex. Restricting intersex to conditions where chromosomal sex doesn't match phenotypic sex, or where the phenotype can't be classified as male or female, gives a prevalence of about 0.018%, almost 100 times lower.
[Sax 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12476264/)

## Rarity and categories

A common objection is that intersex people exist, but they're abnormalities rather than the norm, so they shouldn't count as a valid category.

Even excluding late-onset adrenal hyperplasia, the 0.2% estimate is on par with the prevalence of red hair in Germany, estimated at 0.20%.
![[Hair color prevalence by country.png]]
[Katsara 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsigen.2019.01.001)

Even in a hypothetical world where redheads were 0.05% of the population, in line with lower estimates for intersex conditions, we wouldn't deny that red is a real point on the spectrum of hair colors. "Red" would still be an option on licenses and demographic forms, and nobody would insist that someone with red hair pick black, brown or blond because red hair isn't the norm. The same goes for intersex people. They are rare and not the norm, but that doesn't matter to whether their sex is a real point among the possible sexes, any more than rarity matters for red hair.

Heterochromia iridis, where the two eyes are different colors, is rare, affecting fewer than 200,000 people in the US.
[Ur Rehman 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2518194/)

Despite being roughly as rare as intersex conditions, or rarer, it has its own option on official forms:
- Virginia's eye color codes include MUL for multicolored. [Virginia State Police: Eye, hair and race codes](https://web.archive.org/web/20210423081119/https://www.vsp.virginia.gov/afis/livescan/files/VAEye&HairColor&RaceCodes.pdf)
- Pennsylvania's out-of-state driver's license application lists dichromatic as an eye color. [PennDOT: Form DL-180R](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/penndot/documents/public/dvspubsforms/bdl/bdl-form/dl-180r.pdf)
