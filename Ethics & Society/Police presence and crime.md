> [!summary] Bottom line
> Police reduce crime, but mostly where and while they are present. When London closed most of its police stations without cutting front-line officers, violent crime rose and fewer crimes were solved near the closed stations, and the city as a whole ended up with slightly more violent crime. Fishing patrols, a police strike and federal grants for new officers point the same way: deterrence fades quickly once police leave, and adding officers cuts crime. Tokyo spreads its officers across hundreds of small neighborhood posts, so police are rarely far away.

## London station closures

After the 2010 Spending Review, police funding in England fell 20% in real terms, and London's Metropolitan Police lost 29% of its budget. To save money, it closed most of its police stations. There were 144 in 2013, 82 in 2014 and 45 by 2017. Front-line officers fell only 1% from 2010 to 2016, while 60% of civilian staff and community support officers were let go. Officers were moved to the surviving stations, so the average station went from about 100 officers to more than 180.

| Measure                                 | Before        | After       |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------- | ----------- |
| **Average distance to nearest station** | 1.3 km (2011) | 3 km (2016) |
| **Median emergency response time**      | 13 minutes    | 19 minutes  |
| **People per station**                  | 50,000        | 200,000     |

The study compared census blocks whose nearest station closed with blocks whose didn't, before and after the closures ([[Glossary#Difference-in-differences|difference-in-differences]]), holding officer numbers at the nearest station constant. The two groups had similar trends before the closures.

- **Violent crime:** assaults and murders rose 7–9.4% in blocks whose nearest station closed, about 3 extra a year per block. The rise began right after the closures and lasted at least 3 years. Each 10% increase in distance raised violent crime by about 0.4%.
- **Solving crimes:** the chance a crime was cleared fell by 0.3–0.5 percentage points from a baseline of 17%, a relative drop of 2–3%. The number of cleared crimes fell 6% and convictions 3% ([[Glossary#Clearance rate|clearance rate]]).
- **Distance:** both effects were largest in the blocks closest to the closed stations and became negligible in the farthest fifth.
- **Near surviving stations:** with more officers based there, violent crime fell by about 4.5 offenses a year per block, though the chance of a crime being cleared didn't change.
- **Reporting:** victims became less likely to report low-severity crimes like thefts, though this result wasn't statistically significant. Reporting of violent crime barely changed, so the rise in recorded violence reflects more actual violence.
- **House prices:** prices fell about 2% in blocks whose nearest station closed, mostly in high-crime and deprived areas, and rose slightly near surviving stations.

Combining the losses near closed stations with the gains near surviving ones, a spatial model predicted a 2% rise in violent crime across London. The rise ranged from −0.3% to nearly 14% across police divisions. Closing stations at random would have raised violent crime by about 4.4%. Choosing closures to keep stations as close as possible to people would have lowered it by 1.4%, with average distance rising only to 1.9 km. Counting crime and house prices, each £1 saved cost society about £0.80.
[Facchetti 2026](https://ifs.org.uk/system/files/2026-09/WP202511-Police-infrastructure-police-performance-and-crime-evidence-from-austerity-cuts.pdf)

## Deterrence fades when police leave

### Fisheries patrols in The Gambia

The Gambia reserves waters within 9 nautical miles of shore for small-scale fishers, but industrial vessels often fish there. Satellite radar from 2017 to 2023 tracked vessels around joint patrols by Gambian authorities and Sea Shepherd in 2019, 2020 and 2023. Compared with what a model predicted without enforcement, patrols cut vessel presence by 12.1% across Gambian waters, 37.9% within 9 nautical miles and 5.9% from 9 to 25 nautical miles. About 16% of the drop was vessels moving into neighboring Senegalese waters. Vessel presence returned to within 5% of its predicted level about 15–30 days after the last arrest, and 31–44 days in the nearshore zone.
[Agarwal](https://aishwarya76734.github.io/gambia-enforcement/paper.pdf)

### Police strike in Ceará, Brazil

In February 2020, military police in the Brazilian state of Ceará went on strike for about two weeks. Police associations said about 65% of street officers stopped working, and even with 2,500 soldiers sent in, police presence fell by about half. Ceará recorded 459 homicides that February, against 165 in February 2019. Compared with a forecast from homicide trends since 2015, homicides were 197–252% higher in the first week of the strike and 110–144% higher in the second. Regression models gave a 76–105% rise during the strike and a 25–43% weekly rise in the period after it.
[Aziani 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17440572.2022.2098121)

## More officers

The 1994 Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act created the COPS program, which gave grants to US cities to hire police officers. Using grant size to predict force size, officers added through COPS significantly reduced auto thefts, burglaries, robberies and aggravated assaults.
[Evans 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272706000831)

## Koban in Tokyo

Tokyo has 102 police stations for about 14 million people, about 0.7 per 100,000. Below them are koban, small police boxes staffed around the clock in shifts, and chūzaisho, residential posts where an officer lives on site. There were 826 koban across Tokyo in May 2019. Koban officers respond to incidents and accidents, give directions, handle lost property, give security advice, patrol and visit homes door to door.
[Tokyo Metropolitan Police Department](https://www.keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/multilingual/english/about_us/activity/koban.html)
