> [!summary] Bottom line
> Rent control works at its stated goal: 25 of 31 studies find it significantly lowers rents on the units it covers. The disagreement is about everything else. Housing quality deteriorates in every study but one, mobility falls almost universally, and uncontrolled rents rise. Effects on new construction are the most ambiguous of all — more than half of studies find a decline, several find nothing. The largest review concludes that the net effect on social welfare "is not clear," because the unintended effects counteract the intended one rather than obviously outweighing it.

## The evidence base

The most comprehensive review gathered 172 works on rent control effects, of which about 80% were empirical and 70% published. The analysis focuses on the 92 empirical studies published in refereed journals between 1967 and 2022.

Coverage is narrow. Only 36 countries have been studied. The USA accounts for 32% of studies and North America 38%; Europe accounts for 52.7%. The remaining 9% is spread across Africa, Asia, Oceania and South America.

The literature identifies **19 distinct effects**, which in order of how much attention they receive are: controlled rents, mobility, homeownership, construction, quality, supply, uncontrolled rents, homelessness, misallocation, property value, segregation, vacancy, net welfare, commute times, inequality, marriage, profitability, side payments, and tax base.
[Kholodilin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhe.2024.101983) · [working paper](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/266608/1/1826442545.pdf)

## Direction of the main effects

| Effect | What the studies find |
|---|---|
| **Controlled rents** | 25 of 31 studies find a statistically significant decrease |
| **Housing quality** | Deterioration in every study except one |
| **Residential mobility** | Almost all studies find a decrease |
| **Uncontrolled rents** | As a rule, they rise |
| **Homeownership** | Majority find an increase, but studies point in both directions |
| **New construction** | Most ambiguous of all: more than half negative, several null |

### Why quality falls

Landlords whose revenues are capped have reduced incentive to invest in maintenance and refurbishment, so properties are left to wear out until the real value of the dwelling falls to match the low real rent. The single exception pegged allowed rent increases to improvements the landlord made, which restores the incentive.

### Why mobility falls, and why that cuts both ways

Tenants in controlled dwellings have little reason to leave. This reduces labor-market flexibility — when employment deteriorates in a city, controlled tenants are less likely to move somewhere with better prospects. But the same effect also reflects genuine tenure stability, since rent control usually travels with eviction protections, and longer tenure raises tenant satisfaction.

### Why uncontrolled rents rise

Rent ceilings amplify the housing shortage, lengthening waiting queues. Would-be tenants who can't wait turn to the unregulated segment, raising demand and rents there.

### Why homeownership rises

Two mechanisms, pointing the same way from opposite directions. Landlords sell or convert dwellings into condominiums to escape properties generating insufficient rental revenue. Separately, tenants in controlled dwellings are *less* inclined to buy, given how protected their position already is.

### Why construction is ambiguous

Three confounds. Rent control designs often exempt newly built housing. The available data usually measure total construction rather than rental construction specifically. And where private construction falls, governments may step in with social housing, leaving total completions flat or higher — which can be misread as rent control doing no harm.

## What moderates the size of the effect

- **Strictness.** Strict controls bite harder than soft ones. Fewer exemptions leave less room for an unregulated sector to expand — if new construction isn't exempt, building is more likely to fall.
- **History.** Introducing rent control where there is no precedent produces a surprise effect, since market participants haven't yet developed strategies to respond.
- **Asymmetry.** Deregulation is not the mirror image of regulation. A market regulated for decades doesn't revert on removal: rent control can convert a tenant-dominated market into a homeownership-dominated one, and removing it won't quickly revive the private rental market.
- **Enforcement.** In some countries rent control has little effect because market participants are unaware it exists. Where they are aware, evasion absorbs some of the effect — key money and other side payments, or principal tenants subletting at market rates above the "fair rent" they pay the landlord.
- **Method.** Some studies in this literature use rigorous statistical methods and others use rudimentary descriptive analysis, which can miss omitted variables and flip signs.

## Net welfare

Rent control is effective at its main goal, but produces undesired effects — lower mobility and construction among them — that counteract the intended one and reduce the net benefit. The review's conclusion is that the overall impact on social welfare **is not clear**.

Two reasons the question stays open. Rent control is never adopted in a vacuum: it arrives alongside eviction protection, housing rationing, housing allowances and construction subsidies, and banking, climate and fiscal policy all interact with it. And some effects, such as higher homeownership or changed inequality, can't be scored as positive or negative without a prior normative commitment.

Net welfare is one of the least-studied of the 19 effects, ranking thirteenth by number of studies.
[Kholodilin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhe.2024.101983)
