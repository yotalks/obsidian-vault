> [!summary] Bottom line
> The US closed most of its state psychiatric hospitals after the 1950s, and public beds fell about 97% per person. Both parties helped, and the community care meant to replace them was never fully built. Many people with serious mental illness ended up in nursing homes, jails or on the street, though this explains only a small part of mass incarceration. People with psychosis make up a small share of violent crime, but their risk of homicide is much higher, mostly alongside substance abuse. Whether to rebuild long-stay hospitals depends on who they're for and whether they work, and the best causal evidence so far is on short holds, where it looks bad for judgment-call cases.

## What an asylum is

An insane asylum was a standalone state hospital for severe mental illness, including people committed against their will. A psych ward is usually a unit inside a general hospital. A standalone outpatient clinic isn't an asylum either.

The word carries stigma, and some of these hospitals changed their names for that reason. What matters is what gets built, how many beds it has and who goes there, not what it's called.

## Bed capacity

In 1955, the US had 340 public psychiatric beds per 100,000 people. By 2016, it had 11.7 state and county beds per 100,000.
[Psychiatric News 2020](https://psychnews.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.pn.2020.1a14)

That's only one kind of bed. Counting all inpatient and 24-hour residential psychiatric beds, about 170,000 people were in them at the end of 2014, or 53.6 per 100,000. That's down 64% from 1970, and 77.4% per person. State and county hospitals shrank, while private psychiatric hospitals and general hospital units grew.
[Lutterman 2017](https://nri-inc.org/media/1319/tac-paper-10-psychiatric-inpatient-capacity-final-09-05-2017.pdf)

The state beds that remain are increasingly used by the criminal courts. Forensic and sex offender services are almost half of state psychiatric hospital spending.
[Lutterman 2017](https://nri-inc.org/media/1319/tac-paper-10-psychiatric-inpatient-capacity-final-09-05-2017.pdf)

## Who closed them

Neither party can pin this on the other:

- **1963:** Kennedy's Community Mental Health Act was meant to replace state hospitals with community centers. Only half were built, and none were fully funded to keep running.
[Chicago Policy Review 2021](https://chicagopolicyreview.org/2021/03/12/community-mental-health-care-lessons-from-history/)
- **1965:** Medicaid excluded institutions for mental diseases, facilities of more than 16 beds, for adults aged 21–64. This gives states a reason to keep patients out of large hospitals.
[CRS: IMD exclusion](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF10222.html)
- **1967:** Governor Ronald Reagan signed California's Lanterman–Petris–Short Act, which ended most indefinite involuntary commitment. A 72-hour hold still applies to people who are a danger to themselves or others, or gravely disabled.
[Wikipedia: Lanterman–Petris–Short Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanterman%E2%80%93Petris%E2%80%93Short_Act)
- **1975:** In O'Connor v. Donaldson, the Supreme Court ruled that a state can't confine a nondangerous person who can survive safely in freedom, alone or with help from family or friends.
[O'Connor v. Donaldson](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/422/563)
- **1981:** Carter signed the Mental Health Systems Act in 1980. Reagan's budget law repealed most of it a year later.
[Wikipedia: Mental Health Systems Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_Health_Systems_Act_of_1980)

## Where people went

Moving people from hospitals into other institutions is called transinstitutionalization. A related pattern is "treat and street," where someone is stabilized briefly, released, and comes back through the hospital or jail.

**Jails:** Across five jails, 14.5% of men and 31% of women had a current serious mental illness. That definition includes major depression, so it's broader than psychosis.
[Steadman 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19487344/)

**Prisons:** Closing hospitals explains about 4–7% of the growth in incarceration from 1980 to 2000, and there was no sign of transinstitutionalization from 1950 to 1980. So it's a small part of mass incarceration, but it could still explain a sizable share of mentally ill people behind bars.
[Raphael 2013](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/jls/vol42/iss1/7)

**Nursing homes:** In 2014, 183,534 nursing home patients had a diagnosis of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. That's more than everyone in specialty psychiatric beds.
[Lutterman 2017](https://nri-inc.org/media/1319/tac-paper-10-psychiatric-inpatient-capacity-final-09-05-2017.pdf)

## Violence

Two things are true at once. Severe mental illness explains a small share of violence, and the risk is still higher for those who have it.

In Sweden, people discharged from hospital with schizophrenia or other psychoses committed about 1 in 20 violent crimes.
[Fazel 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16877653/)

Across 20 studies, psychosis without substance abuse raised the odds of violence about twofold (OR 2.1, 1.7–2.7). With substance abuse it was about ninefold (OR 8.9, 5.4–14.7), similar to substance abuse without psychosis. Homicide risk was far higher (OR 19.5, 14.7–25.8), though homicide is rare to begin with.
[Fazel 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19668362/)

So the case for commitment is about people who are dangerous, not everyone with a diagnosis, and not homeless people as such. Most homeless people aren't the target, and someone who's dangerous and housed would be. Serious mental illness has to be paired with danger to mean anything here.

## Does involuntary care work

The best causal evidence covers short holds, not long stays. Physicians differ in how readily they hospitalize, and patients get assigned to them quasi-randomly. In 16,630 evaluations in Allegheny County, for cases where some physicians would hospitalize and others wouldn't, hospitalization raised violent crime charges by 2.6 percentage points from a base of 3.3% over 3 months. Suicide or overdose death rose by 1.0 percentage point from a base of 1.1%. The authors point to lost earnings and housing as possible reasons.
[Emanuel 2025](https://doi.org/10.59576/sr.1158)

This applies only to judgment calls. It doesn't cover people every physician would hospitalize, who are the ones most likely to be dangerous. Holds were also short, at up to 5 days before any extension, so it doesn't test long-stay hospital care. But it does mean that holding more people on the margin isn't obviously safer.

Commitment sets one person's right not to be confined against other people's right not to be attacked, and against the patient's own interest in being treated. → [[Positive and negative rights]]

### The case for long-stay beds

Some people are too ill to be treated in a community center or as outpatients. If that's true, the main cost of having no beds is an opportunity cost: people cycling through jail who might have recovered enough to work. Long-stay care doesn't have to rule out other reforms, and the old hospitals don't have to be copied.

## Housing First

Housing First gives people permanent housing without requiring sobriety. Across four randomized trials, all at high risk of bias, it increased days housed (SMD 1.24) and the chance of being housed at 18–24 months (RR 2.46). It also reduced emergency visits (IRR 0.63) and hospitalizations (IRR 0.76). Effects on most short-term health outcomes were imprecise, with no clear difference in substance use.
[Baxter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30777888/)

So it works for housing, and it isn't really a mental illness treatment. It and hospital beds solve different problems, and someone can support both.

## Recent policy

Sleeping in public is a separate issue from mental illness. In Grants Pass v. Johnson (2024), the Supreme Court ruled that enforcing general camping laws doesn't violate the Eighth Amendment.
[City of Grants Pass v. Johnson](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/23-175)

A 2025 executive order calls for moving homeless people into long-term institutional settings through civil commitment, and for ending federal support for Housing First policies.
[EO 14321](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/07/ending-crime-and-disorder-on-americas-streets/)

## Stigma and single cases

News stories can raise stigma. In a randomized survey of 1,797 adults, 54% of those who read a story about a mass shooting by someone with mental illness rated people with serious mental illness as dangerous, compared with 40% of controls. Adding a policy proposal to the story didn't change that.
[McGinty 2013](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.13010014)

That's about news on mass shootings, not about arguing for more hospital beds. Whether that kind of advocacy raises or lowers stigma is a separate empirical question.

Promoting a policy after a tragedy also isn't an argument against it. The policy stands or falls on its effects, whatever the timing.

A single case doesn't settle this either. In August 2025, Iryna Zarutska, a Ukrainian refugee, was stabbed to death on a Charlotte light rail train. The man charged had 14 prior arrests, his mother says he was diagnosed with schizophrenia, and he's been found incompetent to stand trial. Many things could have prevented it, including a longer sentence, commitment or bail reform. North Carolina responded with Iryna's Law, which ended cashless release for some offenses and requires some mental health evaluations.
[Wikipedia: Killing of Iryna Zarutska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_of_Iryna_Zarutska)
