> [!summary] Bottom line
> The MAHA report blames rising childhood chronic disease on four drivers: ultra-processed food, chemicals, screens and over-medication. Its medication claims don't hold up well. The ADHD trial it leans on showed medication helped for two years. Its own antibiotic source says 29% of childhood prescriptions are unnecessary, not over 35%. And links between early antibiotics and later chronic disease mostly shrink or vanish once you account for the infections being treated.

## The rise in childhood chronic conditions

In 1960, 1.8% of US children had a health condition severe enough to interfere with daily activities. By 2010 it was over 8%. Most of the rise came from four common conditions: asthma, obesity, mental health conditions and neurodevelopmental disorders like ADHD and autism, which rose sharply in the 1980s and 1990s. Obesity and asthma may have levelled off since 2000, while ADHD and autism diagnoses keep rising mainly through better awareness and screening. The authors point to genetic susceptibility interacting with environmental exposures, changes in children's social environments, diet, physical activity and screen time, and wider screening that picks up milder cases. Poverty has become increasingly linked to chronic conditions since the 1990s.
[Perrin 2014](https://doi.org/10.1377/hlthaff.2014.0832)

## The report's four drivers

The report says rising childhood chronic disease is driven by:
- **Food:** nearly 70% of children's calories now come from ultra-processed foods, up from zero 100 years ago.
- **Chemicals:** over 40,000 chemicals are registered for use in the US, and pesticides, microplastics and dioxins are found in children's and pregnant women's blood and urine.
- **Technology:** screen time, too little physical activity and too little sleep, alongside rising sadness, suicidal ideation and loneliness in teens.
- **Medication:** children are "highly medicated – and it's not working".

Only the medication claims are checked below.

## ADHD stimulants

The report says stimulant prescriptions for ADHD rose 250% from 2006 to 2016 "despite evidence they did not improve outcomes long-term".

That evidence is the 3-year follow-up of the MTA trial. Children given the trial's intensive medication management did significantly better on ADHD symptoms than those given behavior therapy or usual care at 14 and 24 months, but the groups no longer differed at 36 months. By then medication use had converged: the share taking medication more than half the time rose from 14% to 45% in the behavior therapy group and fell from 91% to 71% in the medication groups.
[Jensen 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17667478/)

The authors themselves say it would be incorrect to conclude that treatment makes no difference. Intensive medication may only make a lasting difference if it's continued at the same intensity as in the trial's first 14 months, and starting medication late or at too low an intensity can even make it look associated with worse outcomes.

## Antipsychotics

The report says antipsychotic prescriptions for children rose 8-fold from 1995 to 2005, mostly for conditions not FDA-approved for children.

Off-label antipsychotic use did grow, from 4.4 million visits in 1995 to 9.0 million in 2008. But that study counted a use as off-label if it lacked FDA approval as of 2008, even when looking at earlier years.
[Alexander 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21254289/)

In children and adolescents, atypical antipsychotic visits rose from 1993 to 2010 and over 65% of them lacked an FDA-approved diagnosis, with ADHD the most common (24% of visits in 2007–2010). Several pediatric indications (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism-related irritability) were only approved partway through that period.
[Sohn 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27281081/)

## Antibiotics: how many are unnecessary?

The report says "more than 35% (equivalent to more than 15 million prescriptions) of childhood antibiotics are unnecessary".

Its own source gives 29% for all childhood (0–19) antibiotics: 646 prescriptions per 1,000 children each year, against an estimated 458 that are appropriate. None of the source's figures is "just over 35%": the closest are just under it (34% for acute respiratory conditions and for pharyngitis), and the next figure up is 100% (asthma, allergy, bronchitis, flu and viral infections).

[Fleming-Dutra 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27139059/)

## Do early antibiotics cause chronic disease?

The report says infants given antibiotics in their first 2 years are more likely to develop asthma, allergic rhinitis, eczema, celiac disease, obesity and ADHD. Its source found hazard ratios from 1.20 to 2.89 for these conditions.
[Aversa 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33208243/)

The key issue is that it isn't adjusted for why the antibiotics were given. The infections themselves may cause later health problems, and children prone to infections may also be prone to those conditions. Studies that adjust for this find much smaller effects, and they aren't always statistically significant:
- Adjusting for infections, doctor visits and other factors, antibiotics in the first year carried only a small asthma risk (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.08–1.16).
[Marra 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19255032/)
- After adjusting for the number of illness visits to a doctor, early antibiotics showed no relation with asthma at all; the link was an artifact of how often children were sick.
[Su 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20545705/)
- Antibiotics before 3 months were linked to asthma by 15 months (OR 2.32), but after adjusting for chest infections this dropped to 1.58 and was no longer significant.
[Wickens 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18727794/)
