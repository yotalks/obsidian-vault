> [!summary] Bottom line
> Medicare for All usually means a single government plan that replaces most private insurance. CBO's modeling of single-payer systems found total health spending could fall or rise, but federal spending would rise by $1.5–3 trillion a year, and demand for care would outgrow supply, causing more delays and forgone care. Countries with single-payer systems have much longer specialist waits than the US. The Yale estimate of $1 trillion saved and 114,000 lives a year rests on contested assumptions. Two-thirds of Americans favor the idea, but support drops to about a quarter when people hear it could delay tests and treatment.

## What it means

Most Medicare for All proposals, including the bill Bernie Sanders introduced in 2017, would replace most current private health insurance with a single government plan. Universal coverage is a separate idea: it means everyone is covered, however the coverage is provided.
[KFF 2026](https://www.kff.org/public-opinion/current-and-historical-public-opinion-on-medicare-for-all-and-other-national-health-plan-proposals/)

Most Americans already have coverage. In 2025, 7.9% of the population (26.7 million people) went without health insurance at some point in the year, about the same as in 2024.
[Census Bureau 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2026/demo/p60-291.html)

## Spending

CBO modeled five illustrative single-payer systems based on Medicare's fee-for-service program. Compared with current law in 2030:
- Federal subsidies for healthcare would rise by $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion, so the government would finance a much larger share of health spending.
- National health expenditures would change by anywhere from a $0.7 trillion decrease to a $0.3 trillion increase. Lower payment rates to providers and less administrative spending were the biggest factors lowering spending, and more use of care was the biggest factor raising it.

Much of the federal increase is spending that shifts from employers and households to taxes, which is why federal spending rises much more than total spending.
[CBO 2020](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/56811)

In CBO's economic model, output 10 years in would range from 0.3% lower to 1.8% higher before counting how the system is financed. Financed with an income or payroll tax, GDP would be about 1% to 10% lower by 2030, depending on the design and the financing.
[CBO 2022](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/57637)

## Demand, supply and wait times

Under the designs CBO analyzed, coverage would be nearly universal and out-of-pocket costs lower, so demand for care would rise. Supply would rise too, through fewer billing restrictions, less administrative work and providers responding to demand, and people would use more care overall. But demand would rise more than supply, so unmet demand would be greater than under current law. That means more congestion, including delays and forgone care, especially in designs with lower cost sharing and lower payment rates.
[CBO 2020](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/56811)

### Specialist waits in other countries

In the Commonwealth Fund's 2023 survey of ten countries, the share of people who waited this long for a specialist appointment after being referred was:

| Country        | 2 months to under 1 year | 1 year or more |
| -------------- | ------------------------ | -------------- |
| Switzerland    | 12.4%                    | 0.8%           |
| Netherlands    | 14.7%                    | 0.7%           |
| United States  | 14.9%                    | 1.6%           |
| Germany        | 22.1%                    | 1.6%           |
| Australia      | 27.6%                    | 4.6%           |
| Sweden         | 29.1%                    | 5.2%           |
| New Zealand    | 29.9%                    | 3.1%           |
| United Kingdom | 31.7%                    | 11.0%          |
| Canada         | 37.9%                    | 10.0%          |
| France         | 43.3%                    | 2.2%           |

[OECD Health at a Glance 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/11/health-at-a-glance-2025_a894f72e/full-report/waiting-times_3a1021fa.html) ([data](https://stat.link/fcx732))

The survey only asks about the most recent specialist referral, so it understates how likely someone is to hit a long wait over many referrals. If each referral had the same chance of a long wait, it would take about 43 referrals in the US before someone was more likely than not to have waited a year or more at least once, compared with 7 in Canada and 6 in the UK. For a wait of 2 months or more, it would take 4 referrals in the US and 2 in Canada and the UK.

### Tolerance for waiting

In a survey of 196 patients at a US hand surgery practice, most patients in every age group didn't want to wait more than a week to have a new problem evaluated. 71% would rather wait longer for a hand specialist than be seen sooner by a non-specialist.
[Caggiano 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4641108/)

## The Yale estimate

A 2026 preprint led by Alison Galvani of Yale, not peer reviewed, estimated that a single-payer system modeled on the Medicare for All Act would save $1.041 trillion and prevent 114,174 deaths a year.
[Yale School of Public Health 2026](https://ysph.yale.edu/news-article/universal-health-coverage-could-save-one-trillion-dollars-and-114000-lives-every-year/)

It's a one-year accounting model: it starts from 2024 national health spending and subtracts assumed savings. Criticisms of its inputs:
- **Fraud ($285.7 billion):** based on a 2003 study of Taiwan's move to national health insurance. That figure was unexplained spending growth after coverage expanded, not a reduction in fraud.
- **Drug prices ($377.5 billion):** applies a 51% price cut meant for private insurers to a broader spending category, about twice the $184 billion the authors' own source estimated.
- **Administration ($286.3 billion):** assumes overhead across the system falls to Medicare's ratio, citing an analysis that argues that comparison is flawed. The model also assumes fraud recovery rises while administrative spending, which includes auditing, falls.
- **Avoidable hospital use ($100 billion):** assumes better primary care reduces emergency and inpatient visits.
- **Paying all providers Medicare rates ($295.6 billion):** leaves out how providers would respond. CBO's own options paid hospitals and physicians more than Medicare rates.
- **Deaths:** 29,631 come from an assumed mortality risk for the underinsured, for which the authors note no direct estimates exist. 51,311 come from a separate forecast about 2025 policy changes, and the rest from a hazard ratio of 1.40 from a study of adults surveyed in 1986–1994.

[Muse 2026](https://newsletter.amuseonx.com/p/advocacy-in-a-lab-coat-inside-yales)
[Cremieux: Against attribution studies](https://cremieux.xyz/p/against-attribution-studies)

In the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment, where Medicaid was assigned by lottery to about 25,000 people, coverage increased emergency department use by 0.41 visits per person over about 18 months, a 40% increase.
[Taubman 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24385603/)

## Public opinion

In KFF's September 2026 poll, 66% favored a national health plan, sometimes called Medicare for All, in which everyone gets insurance from a single government plan. That was the highest share since KFF began asking in 2017, up from 53% in October 2020. Support included 84% of Democrats, 72% of independents and 34% of Republicans.

Support shifts a lot with what people hear about it. It rose to about three-quarters when people heard it would guarantee insurance as a right or eliminate premiums and reduce out-of-pocket costs. It fell to:
- about a quarter when they heard it could delay medical tests or treatment
- about a third when they heard it could raise taxes
- about four in ten when they heard it could threaten the current Medicare program

61% expected their taxes to get worse under Medicare for All. 57% of privately insured adults thought they could keep their current insurance, which most proposals wouldn't allow.

Wording matters too. In 2019, "Medicare-for-all" and "universal health coverage" each got 63% positive reactions, "single-payer health insurance system" 49%, and "socialized medicine" was split. In a 2017 Gallup poll that let people say they didn't know enough, 61% did.
[KFF 2026](https://www.kff.org/public-opinion/current-and-historical-public-opinion-on-medicare-for-all-and-other-national-health-plan-proposals/)

## Medicare physician payment

Medicare doesn't automatically raise physician payment rates with the cost of running a practice. Adjusted for practice-cost inflation, Medicare physician payment fell 33% from 2001 to 2026.
[AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/medicare-medicaid/medicare-physician-pay-has-plummeted-2001-find-out-why)

The Patients First Act of 2026 (H.R. 9693), introduced in July 2026, would base the yearly update on the Medicare Economic Index, a measure of practice-cost inflation, within a set floor and ceiling. That's less than a full inflation update, but more stable and predictable than current law.
[ACP comments on H.R. 9693](https://www.acponline.org/sites/default/files/acp-policy-library/letters/acp_submits_comments_on_patients_first_act_hr_9693_2026.pdf)
