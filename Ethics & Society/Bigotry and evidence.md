> [!summary] Bottom line
> Whether someone is a bigot is two questions: what bigotry is, and what counts as evidence for it. A definition doesn't set the evidence standard. Almost no single statement proves bigotry by logic alone, but many are strong evidence, so the useful question is which hypothesis best explains everything a person says and does, and whether their standards change with the group. Bigotry also comes in degrees, and it doesn't have to be conscious.

## What bigotry is

Bigotry is prejudice or hostility against a group, or discrimination against it, because of its race, religion or ethnicity. That covers both mental states and behavior. Someone can be prejudiced without acting on it, and someone can discriminate without feeling hatred. A shopkeeper who puts up a "no Black people" sign only because they think white customers will spend more has no opinion about race, but they're still discriminating on the basis of race.

Having a reason doesn't stop something from being prejudice. "I don't like criminals and Black people are criminals" gives a reason, and it's still prejudice. Most bigotry works this way, resting on a story about a group, like crime for Black people or terrorism for Muslims. That's also why bigots don't need to dislike every member of a group. Someone can vote for a Black president, or have Muslim friends they really do love, and still be biased against the group. People can even be biased against their own group.

Most words are learned from examples rather than dictionaries. So disagreements about the dictionary wording usually matter less than which cases the word gets applied to.

### Prejudice plus power

Some activists define racism as prejudice plus institutional power, so only a dominant group can be racist.
[Wikipedia: Prejudice plus power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prejudice_plus_power)

That's a different concept from the ordinary one, not a better version of it. On that definition, someone from a less powerful group who stabs people because they're white isn't racist.

## Definition vs evidence

A definition says what bigotry is, not how much evidence you need to conclude someone is a bigot. Two people can share a definition while one calls everyone a bigot on the slightest hint and the other demands a confession.

Logical possibility is also a different question from probability. If someone lost all memory of learning about the Holocaust and then denied it, they wouldn't be a bigot, so denying it isn't bigotry by definition. That doesn't make it weak evidence. In the real world it's close to conclusive. Extreme hypotheticals test whether a definition is right, and they shouldn't be read as claims about how often the exception happens. Someone who bans dark-skinned customers because of a sincere aversion to the melanin molecule isn't racist by definition, and in real life nobody should believe that's what's going on.

So statements and actions almost never prove bigotry. They raise or lower your credence. Someone saying they want to close the border doesn't show they're racist, but it raises the odds a little and adds to everything else you know. The more someone has had time to look into a topic, as with a professional commentator, the more their claims count.

## Best explanation

The question is which hypothesis best explains the whole pattern.

- **Compare across groups.** If someone hates all bankers, the explanation is bankers. If they only hate Black bankers, race is an effect modifier, and it shouldn't be one. Bias against a subset of a group is still bias.
- **Look for double standards.** Does the person use the same language and the same standard for other groups in the same position? Showing that they also use a slur against another race doesn't excuse it, because that only shows two prejudices.
- **Look for proxies.** A rule can avoid mentioning race or religion and still be aimed at it. If a travel ban's criteria are carefully chosen to capture Muslim countries, that's strong evidence of Islamophobia. Banning ISIS members isn't.
- **Check the ignorance.** "I didn't know" only helps if the ignorance is even. Someone who knows every detail about one religion and repeats myths about another needs an explanation for the difference.
- **Check when defenses appear.** Mentioning that you also dislike another group only counts if you bring it up when nobody's accusing you.

### Stacking excuses

Any single piece of evidence can be explained away. Maybe they were tired, maybe they didn't know, maybe they only apply the rule to bigger groups. But all of those excuses have to be true at once, so each one lowers the odds of the innocent theory. Five independent excuses that are each 70% likely leave only about a 17% chance that all of them hold. This is the real content of the phrase "mental gymnastics."

### Ideas and statistics

Observing a statistic isn't bigotry. Black Americans are overrepresented in crime statistics, and saying so isn't racist. Saying it's because of their genes is. Motive matters as well, since the same fact can be raised to understand a problem or to attack a group. → [[Black White IQ gap]]

Criticizing a religion isn't bigotry either, because beliefs do affect what people do. It starts to look like bigotry when the criticism turns into obsession, double standards, and treating ordinary believers as the worst of them.

## What doesn't count as a defense

Evidence only helps if a rival hypothesis can't explain it just as well.

- **Self-serving statements.** "I love Hispanics" or "I've always fought racism" don't move anything, because a bigot would say the same thing.
- **Friends.** Having a friend from a group doesn't rule out bias against it. It doesn't show the friendship is fake either.
- **Costly statements.** These do count. Condemning an ally for bigotry, when it costs you something, is hard to explain as self-interest.

A walk-back works the same way. It doesn't count if the person only regrets giving opponents ammunition, claims they were taken out of context, or casts themselves as the victim. It counts if they say the statement was wrong, say what they got wrong, and stop.

## In-group standards

People often hold their own group to higher standards. Some of that is bias of closeness: the more bigoted someone is against an out-group, the angrier they get at in-group members who won't join in. That's different from hatred of the group itself, so it should be labeled as what it is.

Saying someone isn't a "real" member of a group can mean two things. It can be a true claim that a label doesn't match someone's knowledge or practice. Or it can mean that their ethnicity obliges them to hold certain politics, which is bigoted, since nobody's ancestry assigns them a political view.

## Degrees

Everyone has some bias, so the bare word "racist" misleads. Used alone, it implies hatred. The goal is to avoid two mistakes: overshooting by treating someone like Hitler, and undershooting by waiting until they reach Hitler's level before saying anything. If the only options are "hates the group" and "not a bigot," a lot of real bigotry goes unchallenged.

Qualifiers fix this. Saying someone is meaningfully racist means they're well past the bias you'd expect from anyone, without claiming they're the worst kind. It also doesn't need to be conscious, since people aren't always aware of their own feelings.

One statement can be enough when it's clear, the person has had time to reflect, and they never adequately take it back. → [[Slurs]]

## Bigotry in law

A law can target effects rather than motives, because intent is hard to prove and the point is preventing harm. So whether something is legal and whether it's bigoted are separate questions.

Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act shows this. In Mobile v. Bolden (1980), a plurality read it to require discriminatory purpose. Congress amended it in 1982 so that a violation could be shown through results, because it saw intent as too hard to prove.
[Hasen 2021](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Hasen%20-%20Testimony1.pdf)

In Louisiana v. Callais (2026), the Supreme Court narrowed that test 6–3. Plaintiffs' illustrative maps can't use race, must meet the state's political goals, and must disentangle race from party. The majority said this doesn't demand a finding of intentional discrimination, but liability now needs a strong inference that the state drew its districts to disadvantage minority voters because of race. The dissent said this brings back the Bolden intent standard.
[Louisiana v. Callais](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/24-109_2026-04-29)

Applied to a gerrymander:
- If lines are drawn by race, even to help a party, that's using race.
- If something else, like income, is used as a proxy for race, it's still using race.
- If a map-drawer really ignores race and only wants partisan gain, the map can hurt Black voters without being racist. It could still be bad for other reasons, and it could still break a results-based law.

The Trump travel ban shows the other direction. Proclamation 9645 didn't mention religion and covered countries like North Korea and Venezuela, and the Supreme Court upheld it 5–4 in Trump v. Hawaii (2018). But it asked whether the policy could be justified on national security grounds under a deferential standard, even after considering his call for a "total and complete shutdown" of Muslim entry. So the ruling doesn't settle whether prejudice motivated the policy, and his statements are still strong evidence on that question.
[Trump v. Hawaii](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/17-965)
