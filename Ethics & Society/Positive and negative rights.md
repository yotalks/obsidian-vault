> [!summary] Bottom line
> Negative rights alone mean there's no obligation to save infinite drowning children even at no cost. Positive rights with no limit reduce everyone to slavery. The position that gets around both is a ratio: how much someone else's suffering falls compared with how much yours rises, with higher ratios moving helping from virtuous to obligatory, and extra weight on the people you're closest to. Veganism doesn't depend on any of this, since it follows from negative rights alone.

## Definitions

A negative right is an entitlement to non-interference, like the right not to be assaulted. A positive right is an entitlement to the provision of some good or service, like welfare assistance.
[SEP: Rights](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rights/)

## Veganism

Veganism follows from negative rights, which nearly everyone already accepts, so whether positive rights exist has no bearing on the argument for it.
→ [[Animal Rights]]

Positive rights can only add to that. Whatever positive right you extend to humans also extends to animals, unless you can name a trait that justifies the difference.
→ [[Wild animal suffering]]

## Why hypotheticals count

Saying a scenario would never happen doesn't get you out of answering it. Hypotheticals test the logical extensions of a moral system, and in classical logic, a contradiction anywhere lets you derive anything, so it can't be fenced off in an unrealistic corner.
[SEP: Paraconsistent logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-paraconsistent/)

You also don't need to know how a hypothetical would work. If someone built a button that would torture millions of people, pressing it would be wrong even if you didn't know the mechanism. All that matters is what follows from each choice, so objections about how a scenario would happen are pragmatics, not answers to the principle.

Arbitrary cut-offs aren't the problem either. For a subjectivist, what matters is where a line leads when it's applied consistently, not whether the line is arbitrary.
→ [[Metaethics]]

## Testing each position

| Position | Reductio |
| --- | --- |
| Negative rights only | Infinite children drown because saving them isn't obligatory |
| Positive rights with no limit | Slavery |
| Positive rights up to a fixed amount of effort | Infinite Holocaust just past your limit |
| Their benefit minus your cost, with a high threshold | No obligation to stop a child being punched, even at no cost |
| Their benefit minus your cost, with a low threshold | Close to slavery again |
| Their benefit divided by your cost | Mainly that family get no special priority, so special relationships have to be added |

### Negative rights only

If a child is drowning and saving them costs you wet shoes, is that an obligation or just a good thing to do? Scale it to a million children, or an infinite number, saved by tapping your shoes together. If saving them still isn't obligatory, that's an infinite drowning you're fine with. Calling it a virtue doesn't help.

The drowning child comes from Peter Singer, who used it to argue that distance doesn't change the obligation to help people far away.
[Wikipedia: Famine, Affluence, and Morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine,_Affluence,_and_Morality)

### Positive rights with no limit

Once there's an obligation to help, what stops it? There are always people suffering, and your money would almost always do more for them than for you. The Against Malaria Foundation provides a net for about $6, at about $5,500 per life saved. So without a limit, you'd have to keep giving until you were as badly off as the people you're helping. This is the standard objection that morality can't be that demanding.
[GiveWell: Top charities](https://www.givewell.org/charities/top-charities)
[SEP: Consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/)

### A fixed limit on effort

You could say you'll help up to a point and no further. But whatever point you pick, the suffering can be scaled up. If trillions of people are being tortured and going one inch past your limit would stop it, you'd have to say you have no obligation to.

### Their benefit minus your cost

Take X as the decrease in their suffering and Y as the increase in yours, with X − Y of 0–100 being virtuous and over 100 being obligatory. This avoids the two problems above, since scaling up X makes helping obligatory and scaling up Y keeps it virtuous.

But set Y to zero, so that helping costs you nothing, and drop X below 100. Now stopping a child being punched in the face isn't obligatory, even though it costs you nothing. Lowering the threshold fixes that, but then your money can always do more than a punch's worth of good for someone else, so you end up a slave with slightly better well-being.

### Their benefit divided by your cost

Using X / Y, with below 1 being bad, 1–100 being virtuous and over 100 being obligatory, gets around all of these:

- **Infinite suffering:** scaling up X takes the ratio over 100, so stopping it is obligatory.
- **Slavery:** if X is high but Y is also high, the ratio can land in the virtuous range, so helping isn't obligatory once it costs you enough.
- **Helping for free:** as Y approaches zero, the ratio approaches infinity, so helping when it costs nothing is always obligatory, without having to touch the threshold.

The numbers themselves are arbitrary. They can be calibrated so ordinary life stays in the virtuous range. The cost is that preventing a microsecond of mild annoyance becomes obligatory if it truly costs you nothing, but that's a small bullet to bite.

Acts that are good but not obligatory are called supererogatory, so this is one way of keeping that category.
[SEP: Supererogation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/supererogation/)

## The pragmatic escape

Another response is that you'd do more good by building yourself up first, then giving more later, so luxuries are really investments. This doesn't work:

- You don't know it's true, since you could lose the investment.
- Even if it's true for you, it wouldn't be for everyone, and you'd still have to say whether those people should live as slaves.
- Nobody actually buys ice cream or a new suit to save more children. It's a justification after the fact.

## Special relationships

A pure ratio says that if two people can be saved and a stranger is very slightly more sentient than your mother, you should save the stranger. To avoid that, add constants:

```
(X × k1) / (Y × k2)
```

k1 is the base value on the people you share your closest relationships with, and k2 is the base value on everyone else. These are obligations owed to some people and not others, which are known as special obligations.
[SEP: Special obligations](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/special-obligations/)

This is still a universal rule. Your mother doesn't have more value than anyone else's. What's valued is the relationship, so if you're choosing between your mother and mine, you should save yours even if mine is slightly more sentient.

Children are the easy case, because bringing a child into the world creates an obligation to care for them.

The relationship can't be genetic. If value rises with how related someone is, you'd have to save a stranger of your own race over a slightly more sentient stranger of another. So the category has to be close relationships, and a closed category rather than a sliding scale of relatedness. This also fits intuition better, since you'd probably save a best friend over an uncle you've never met.

It isn't reciprocation either. If your mother were sealed in a box where she could never interact with you again, you'd still value her over a stranger, so this is closer to loyalty to the relationship.

Relationships can be outscaled once enough suffering is at stake.

## Humans and more sentient animals

If a human and a pig were equally sentient and everything else was equal, nothing would favor saving either. If the pig were slightly more sentient and you had no relationship with either, you'd save the pig. Most people have no problem saying that about a more sentient alien. It only sounds wrong about a pig because pigs aren't actually more sentient, and because in reality killing a child causes far more suffering to the people who know them.

Valuing humans more at a base level isn't a way out, since that's the same logic as the racism reductio above.
