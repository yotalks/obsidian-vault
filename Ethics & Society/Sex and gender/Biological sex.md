> [!summary] Bottom line
> Sex can be defined by chromosomes, by gametes, by the gametes an organism produces, or by secondary sex characteristics, and each gives sex different properties. Biologically, sex is defined by gamete size. In species with two gamete sizes, females are the phenotype that produces the larger gametes and males the phenotype that produces the smaller ones. There are only two sexes, but sex isn't binary in the scientific sense of a dichotomous variable, where each case takes exactly one of two values, because some organisms are both male and female. Hermaphrodites are common in plants and invertebrates like earthworms, and rare humans have produced both eggs and sperm. Whether sex is a spectrum depends on whether there's a wide range of possible sexes, but it can be bimodal even with only a few possible values, as long as two of them are far more common than the rest.

## Defining male and female

[[Glossary#Anisogamy|Anisogamy]] is when an organism's gametes differ in size. In organisms with two sexes, these different-sized gametes define the sex: males have small, often flagellated sperm, and females have large eggs.
[Cotner 2011](https://books.google.com/books/about/Arguing_for_Evolution.html?id=p0_t5koK0XAC)

The biological definitions follow from that:
- **Female:** the adult phenotype that produces the larger gametes in anisogamous systems.
- **Male:** the adult phenotype that produces the smaller gametes in anisogamous systems.
- **Hermaphroditism** (in animals) or **monoecy** (in plants): systems where the male and female sexes exist in the same individual, so a single individual produces both small and large gametes.

So male and female only exist in species with anisogamy. Our ancestors were isogamous, producing only one gamete size, and asking how males and females evolved is the same as asking why gamete sizes diverged. The two main classes of models, gamete competition and gamete limitation, aren't alternatives but two aspects of one process. Once anisogamy evolved it was very stable, and the split in gamete size went on to drive many of the other differences between the sexes.
[Lehtonen 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25323972/)

## Four definitions of sex

Which properties sex has depends on how it's defined:

| Definition | Applies to | Level of measurement | Bimodal | Spectrum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chromosomal | Organisms (here, humans) | Depends on how other karyotypes are classed | — | — |
| Gametic | Cells | Nominal | — | — |
| Gametic entailed | Organisms in anisogamous species | Nominal, ternary | Yes | No |
| Secondary sex characteristics | Organisms (humans) | Ordinal | Yes | Yes |

### Chromosomal

There are many chromosomal definitions of sex across species, but in humans, 46,XX is female and 46,XY is male.

The problem comes when someone who is neither 46,XX nor 46,XY can reproduce and would clearly be considered a given sex. Are they a third sex, or outside the category with no sex at all?

Using this definition needs a rule for how to classify those cases.
→ [[Sex chromosome exceptions]]

### Gametic

Sex can describe cells themselves (sex cells): sperm cells are male and egg cells are female. This definition is about cells, not organisms, and it makes sex [[Levels of measurement|nominal]]. Its other properties depend on whether a third gamete is possible. None has been observed, but it isn't accepted to be logically or physically impossible.

### Gametic entailed

The gametes an organism produces define its sex. Males produce small gametes (sperm in animals), females produce large gametes (eggs), and organisms that produce both are hermaphrodites, which are both male and female, not neither male nor female, outside the category or sexless (see Organisms that are both, below).

This definition is about organisms, not cells, and only applies to anisogamous species. It makes sex a nominal, ternary variable (male, female or both) with a bimodal distribution, but not a spectrum.

### Secondary sex characteristics

Sex can be defined by features that appear during puberty in humans, like enlarged breasts and wider hips in females, and facial hair and Adam's apples in males.

This definition is ordinal, even if not quantitative, because people can be ordered from most masculine to most feminine, with many possible values between the two extremes. It makes sex ordinal, a bimodal distribution and a spectrum.

## Is sex binary?

The argument that sex isn't binary is a modus tollens:
- **P1:** If male/female sex with respect to organisms is binary, then it's not the case that there can be organisms who are male and female (P→¬Q).
- **P2:** There can be organisms who are male and female (Q).
- **C:** Therefore, it's not the case that male/female sex with respect to organisms is binary (∴¬P).

### What binary means (P1)

In science, "binary" variables are dichotomous, and binary variables are usually coded as 0 or 1. A dichotomous variable takes one of only two possible values when observed or measured, so the disjunction is exclusive: each data point can only take one of the two values. Sex recorded as male or female is a dichotomous [[Levels of measurement|nominal variable]].
[SAGE: Dichotomous variables](https://methods.sagepub.com/reference/the-sage-encyclopedia-of-social-science-research-methods/n239.xml)
[SAGE: Logit and probit models](https://methods.sagepub.com/foundations/logit-and-probit-binary-and-multinomial-choice-models)

Examples of binary variables:
- the outcome of an experiment ("success" or "failure")
- the response to a yes-no question ("yes" or "no")
- presence or absence of some feature ("is present" or "is not present")
- the truth or falsehood of a proposition ("true" or "false")

None of these can take both values at the same time.

Golf balls are an analogy. If golf balls only come in red and yellow, there are only two colors, but a ball that's half red and half yellow means ball color isn't binary. Sex works the same way: there are only two gamete types, so two sexes, but an individual that makes both means sex isn't a dichotomous variable.

### Organisms that are both (P2)

A hermaphrodite is an individual that is both male and female. Simultaneous hermaphrodites are male and female at the same time, and sequential hermaphrodites are male first and then female, or the reverse. Salps, a group of ocean-drifting tunicates, are one example.
[Gershwin 2013](https://publications.csiro.au/rpr/download?pid=csiro:EP1312315&dsid=DS2)

A hermaphrodite organism is both male and female and has both types of sex organs. The earthworm is one: its male and female sex organs sit close together near the front of its body. Two earthworms pair up and exchange sperm, and each later sheds a cocoon that collects its eggs and the received sperm, where fertilization happens.
[BIOL 205 Lecture 29 (2010)](http://s3.amazonaws.com/docuum/attachments/4935/BIOL%20205%20Lecture%2029.pdf?1271436874)

Biologists studying these animals treat each individual as both sexes. Measuring sexual selection is less clear-cut in simultaneous hermaphrodites, because each individual is both male and female at the same time. In the pond snail *Lymnaea stagnalis*, an individual's mating success in the female role seemed to reduce its own reproductive success in the male role, an effect that only showed up several weeks into the experiment.
[Hoffer 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5511009/)

The same applies to sexual conflict. Separate-sexed animals can partly resolve conflicts between the sexes, but simultaneous hermaphrodites can't resolve them as easily, because each individual is both male and female at the same time. In the hermaphroditic flatworm genus *Macrostomum*, a complex sperm design with stiff bristles was associated with reciprocal mating and lost when sperm injection through the skin evolved.
[Schärer 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21220334/)

### Humans

In humans, true hermaphroditism (now called ovotesticular disorder of sex development) means having both ovarian and testicular tissue. Cases exist with both egg and sperm production:
- A 1973 case report described true hermaphroditism with both oogenesis and spermatogenesis.
[Shannon 1973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4532534/)
- A 32-year-old phenotypically male true hermaphrodite had fathered a child, and histology of his removed ovary suggested ovulation had occurred at some point. It was believed to be the first cytogenetically proved true hermaphrodite who was fertile as a male.
[Parvin 1982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7200380/)

## Is sex a spectrum?

What is a spectrum? Older definitions such as is "a condition that is not limited to a specific set of values but can vary without steps, across a continuum" don't fully capture what we mean by spectrum, as discrete spectrums exist in physics.
Spectrums that are continuous variables include frequency/wavelength of light, volume, mass, and temperature.
Spectrums that are discrete variables include the emission spectrum of the hydrogen atom. In these types of spectrums, not any value can be instantiated. The variable is limited to a given number of possible values.
The word "spectrum" has been analogized and transformed to refer to types of data that aren't even continuous or discrete too. Like in biology and medicine, antibiotic spectrum of activity is a component of antibiotic classification. A broad spectrum antibiotic is active against a wide range of bacteria. Notice how the species of bacteria is sensitive to the antibiotic is a [[Levels of measurement|nominal variable]].
So we can say at minimum, in order to exist on a spectrum, a variable must be able to take on a "wide range" of possibilities. What a "wide range" is, is a different topic and may be a sorites problem.
[Wikipedia: Spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum)
[Wikipedia: Hydrogen spectral series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_spectral_series)
[Wikipedia: Broad-spectrum antibiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad-spectrum_antibiotic)
[SEP: Sorites paradox](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sorites-paradox/)

Sex exists as a spectrum if there is a wide range of possible sexes.

## Is sex bimodal?

A bimodal distribution is a multimodal distribution with two different modes, which often appear as distinct peaks (local maxima) in a probability density function.
[Wikipedia: Multimodal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multimodal_distribution)

Categorical, continuous and discrete data can all form bimodal distributions. That includes variables that aren't spectrums because they can't take a wide range of values.

Take an island with only three types of fruit: 48 apples, 48 bananas and 2 cherries. Fruit type isn't a spectrum. It's a [[Levels of measurement|nominal variable]] with only three possible values. But it is bimodal, with two modes (apples and bananas) in data with more than two possible values.

So sex is a bimodal distribution if it has two modes, appearing as distinct peaks, and more than two possible values. In humans, about 98% of people are typical females or males, and somewhere between 0.02% and 1.7% have an intersex condition, depending on which conditions count.
