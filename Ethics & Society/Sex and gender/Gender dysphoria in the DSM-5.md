> [!summary] Bottom line
> In the DSM-5, gender dysphoria requires both a marked incongruence between experienced and assigned gender and clinically significant distress or impairment. Being transgender isn't itself the diagnosis, and simple gender nonconformity doesn't count. Childhood dysphoria persists in a minority (2–30% of natal boys, 12–50% of natal girls), more often when it's more intense. The diagnosis carries higher suicide risk, which may persist after transition.

All of this is from the DSM-5 chapter on gender dysphoria (2013).
[APA 2013](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.books.9780890425596)

## Terms

- **Sex** refers to biological indicators of male and female: chromosomes, gonads, sex hormones and unambiguous genitalia.
- **Gender** is the public, usually legally recognized, lived role as boy or girl, man or woman. The DSM sees biological factors as contributing to gender development alongside social and psychological ones, rather than gender being purely socially constructed.
- **Gender identity** is someone's identification as male, female or occasionally another category.
- **Transgender** covers the broad spectrum of people who transiently or persistently identify with a gender different from their natal gender. **Gender dysphoria** as a diagnosis is narrower, requiring distress.
- **Transsexual** refers to someone who seeks or has undergone a social transition, often but not always with hormones and surgery.

## Diagnostic criteria

Both versions require the incongruence to last at least 6 months and to cause clinically significant distress or impairment in social, school, work or other important areas of functioning.
- **Children** need at least 6 of 8 signs, one of which must be a strong desire to be the other gender or insistence that they are. The other signs include preferences for cross-gender clothing, roles, toys and playmates, rejecting typical toys and play of their assigned gender, disliking their sexual anatomy, and wanting the sex characteristics of their experienced gender.
- **Adolescents and adults** need at least 2 of 6: incongruence with their sex characteristics, wanting to be rid of them, wanting those of the other gender, and a strong desire to be, to be treated as, or conviction of having the feelings of the other gender.
- Clinicians can specify whether it occurs with a disorder of sex development, and for adults whether it's **post transition** (living full time in the desired gender after at least one medical procedure or treatment).
- Presentations that cause distress but don't meet full criteria, such as lasting under 6 months, are coded as **other specified** or **unspecified** gender dysphoria.

The diagnosis isn't meant for gender nonconformity alone, like tomboyish girls or occasional cross-dressing in men. It should be limited to people whose distress and impairment meet the criteria.

## Prevalence

Among adults, 0.005–0.014% of natal males and 0.002–0.003% of natal females have it, based on specialty clinics, so these are likely underestimates. Referred children are mostly natal boys (2:1 to 4.5:1), adolescents are close to even, and adults lean natal male (1:1 to 6.1:1), though Japan and Poland lean natal female.

For comparison, a meta-regression of 12 US population surveys from 2007–2015 estimated that about 390 per 100,000 adults (0.39%, almost 1 million people) identified as transgender in 2016, and the proportion rose with each survey year. That counts self-identified transgender people, not diagnosed gender dysphoria, so the two figures measure different things.

## Development and course

In clinic-referred children, cross-gender behavior usually starts between ages 2 and 4, and anatomic dysphoria becomes more common approaching puberty.

Persistence from childhood into adolescence or adulthood ranges from 2.2–30% in natal males and 12–50% in natal females. It's modestly correlated with how severe the dysphoria was in childhood. It's unclear whether therapeutic approaches affect persistence, or whether children supported to live socially in their desired gender persist more, since they hadn't been followed systematically. Most children who desist are same-sex attracted: 63–100% of natal boys who desist are attracted to males, and 32–50% of natal girls who desist identify as lesbian.

There are two broad paths:
- **Early onset** starts in childhood. Early-onset natal males are almost always attracted to men, and early-onset natal females almost always to women. It's the most common path in natal females.
- **Late onset** starts around puberty or later, and parents are often surprised. Late-onset natal males are often attracted to women, may have a history of cross-dressing with sexual arousal, and seek care later. They may fluctuate more in their dysphoria and be more ambivalent about and less satisfied after surgery. Late-onset natal females are usually attracted to men and identify as gay men after transition.

Most people with a disorder of sex development don't develop gender dysphoria, even with gender-atypical behavior, so gender-atypical behavior alone shouldn't be read as a sign of dysphoria.

## Changes from the DSM-IV

The DSM-5 replaced the DSM-IV's (1994) gender identity disorder. The DSM-IV diagnosis required a strong and persistent cross-gender identification (not just wanting the cultural advantages of the other sex), persistent discomfort with one's assigned sex or its gender role, no physical intersex condition, and clinically significant distress or impairment. The DSM-5 instead centers on a marked incongruence between experienced and assigned gender, uses "gender" rather than "sex", allows alternative gender identities beyond the binary, and lets people with disorders of sex development be diagnosed.

## Risk factors

- Natal males with gender dysphoria more often have older brothers.
- For late-onset cases, factors under consideration include cross-dressing developing into autogynephilia (sexual arousal at the thought of oneself as a woman).
- There's some genetic contribution: weak familiality among siblings, higher concordance in identical than fraternal twins, and some heritability.
- No hormone abnormalities have been found in 46,XY people. 46,XX people may have higher androgen levels (in the range of hirsute women, far below normal male levels). Evidence is insufficient to call gender dysphoria a form of intersex limited to the brain.

## Suicide and functioning

Adolescents and adults with gender dysphoria are at higher risk of suicidal ideation, attempts and suicide before reassignment. After reassignment adjustment varies, and suicide risk may persist.

Distress may not show up in supportive environments, and may be eased by knowing medical treatments exist. Gender dysphoria is associated with stigma, discrimination, victimization, school dropout, unemployment and more mental disorders, and impairment like school refusal, depression, anxiety and substance use may be a consequence of it.

Clinically referred children and adolescents have more anxiety, disruptive and depressive disorders, partly linked to growing non-acceptance by others with age, though anxiety is also common in cultures that accept gender variance. Autism is more common in clinically referred children and adolescents than in the general population.

## What it isn't

- **Transvestic disorder:** cross-dressing for sexual arousal in mostly heterosexual men, without questioning their gender.
- **Body dysmorphic disorder:** wanting to change a body part because it seems abnormally formed, not because it signals the wrong gender. People who want a healthy limb amputated (body integrity identity disorder) usually don't want to change gender.
- **Schizophrenia:** delusions of belonging to another gender are rare. Without psychotic symptoms, insisting on being another gender isn't a delusion.
- Some males seek castration for aesthetic reasons or to remove the effects of androgens without changing male identity, and don't meet the criteria.
